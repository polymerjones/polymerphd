# 01 — Core Principles

Five patterns recur across this collection often enough to treat as load-bearing, even though no
single source states them as a numbered list. Read these first — they're the lens the rest of this
package applies to any specific movement, drill, or practice.

---

## 1. Natural movement is framed as the origin sport and exercise are derived from, not the reverse

The recurring claim across this collection's coaching and founder-level sources is that running,
jumping, climbing, throwing, and carrying are the ancestral, general-purpose movement repertoire
humans evolved with — and that specific sports (soccer, tennis, weightlifting) are narrower,
specialized expressions of that same underlying capability, not the other way around. One source
puts it directly: sport and fitness "enable natural movement" only in the sense that natural
movement is what "make[s] any sports... any physical activity... possible" in the first place, since
"our ancestors evolved with these abilities" long before any sport formalized a narrow slice of
them.

## 2. Movement is framed as a behavior that solves a real problem, not a repetition to perform

Several sources draw a sharp line between "exercise" (repetition performed for its own sake,
described in this collection as potentially "mindless") and "practicing movement" (engaging with a
real physical problem — getting up off the floor, carrying a load, crossing an obstacle). This
distinction is used as a diagnostic tool as much as a philosophy: how a person solves the "get up
off the floor" problem, and which solution they avoid, is treated as revealing something concrete
about their mobility or strength that a repetition-based exercise wouldn't surface.

## 3. Technique and structured progression matter even for "natural" movement

More than one source is explicit that natural movement is not the same as unstructured outdoor
play — it's taught as a systematic method with progressions, the same way a martial art is taught
through trained technique rather than improvised fighting. This collection's sources are
consistent that movements which look effortless on video (a barefoot rock-face slide, a smooth
get-up) are effortless *because* of trained technique, not because they require no skill — a point
several sources say surprises people who try to replicate the movements without preparation.

## 4. Capacity has to be built gradually — the body has been "numbed," and rushing causes injury

A specific, repeated caution across barefoot training, spinal mobility, and general movement
coaching in this collection is that skipping a gradual buildup is the direct cause of overuse
injury (hairline fractures, plantar fasciitis, tendinitis are named examples). The stated mechanism
is that years of cushioned, unchallenged movement dull tissue tolerance, so tolerance has to be
rebuilt in small doses rather than assumed to already be present. A closely related, separately
stated principle for mobility work specifically: "frequency or consistency" beats intensity —
light, frequent practice is described as more effective than infrequent, aggressive stretching.

## 5. Awareness and self-generated effort are treated as doing more than the stretch or load itself

Multiple sources in this collection push back against relying on external leverage (pulling on a
limb, using momentum) as the main driver of a mobility gain, instead directing attention to
visualizing the body's internal shape and using the person's own muscular tension to reach an end
range. The stated reasoning is that awareness — recognizing where a limitation actually lives, not
just pushing harder at the point that's already flexible — is what produces lasting change, with
the stretch or the load treated as the occasion for that awareness rather than the mechanism on its
own.

# 02 — Daily Practices

A by-practice lookup pulling together the concrete, repeatable drills this collection covers. This
is a summary for quick orientation — the individual notes carry the full detail, direct quotation,
and timestamps this file leaves out. Nothing here is medical or injury-rehab advice; where a
practice risks strain, the caution given by the source is repeated here too.

---

## The get-up (as a daily movement, and as a self-check)

Practiced as a training pattern: from lying down, shift body weight onto a bent leg to roll to the
side, transfer weight to the elbow, then the hand, move into a half-kneeling position, and stand —
reversing the sequence to return to the floor. Tools (a kettlebell held overhead, a heavy sandbag)
are used as *teaching constraints*, not as the point of the exercise — the actual goal is always
"can you get up off the floor," not how much weight you can get up with.

The same movement doubles as a **self-check**: which of the few ways of standing up you default to
— and which you avoid — is a plain-language signal of where a limitation might be. Defaulting to a
split-squat stance and never accessing a full squat position when getting up, for instance, points
toward checking ankle or hip mobility rather than assuming it's just "how you get up."

## Barefoot training (building it up safely)

Start with **foot-specific drills**, not by simply removing shoes and running: toe yoga (moving
toes independently), toe drops (lifting all toes, lowering one at a time from the outside in), "toe
rainbow" (pushing the big and little toes down while lifting the middle three), "short foot"
(lifting the arch without curling the toes), and balance work on elevated or unstable surfaces.

Once foot strength is being built, add barefoot conditioning in **short intervals**, not a
continuous run — alternate a short bout of barefoot running/shuffling with a different modality
(rowing, cycling, a ski erg) rather than running the full session barefoot. Progress gradually:
skipping this buildup is named as the direct cause of stress fractures, plantar fasciitis, and
tendinitis in runners who switch too fast. Match the surface to the practice — a natural surface
(grass) is treated as appropriate; hot or debris-covered pavement is not.

## Spinal mobility (three movements, in order: rotation, flexion, extension)

1. **Rotation** — sit elevated (hips at or above knee height) for a neutral spine, then reach
   backward in sequence: head, then torso, then arm. Hold the end-range rotation for about 10
   seconds using your own muscular tension, not by pulling with the opposite hand.
2. **Flexion (rocking)** — flex the spine into a "C" shape working upper back, then mid/lower back,
   then the hips, and rock gently with minimal arm/leg momentum. If the rock feels jarring rather
   than smooth, it's a sign one spinal segment (often the lower back) isn't sharing the flexion
   evenly with the rest.
3. **Extension (prone lying)** — from forearms, press up onto the wrists while looking up to extend
   the upper back. If the stretch is felt mainly in the lower back or the neck instead, that's
   named as a sign the mid/upper back isn't doing its share — back off and redirect the extension
   there rather than pushing further.

Governing principle for all three: **frequency over intensity** — light, daily (even multiple
times daily) practice is described as producing better results than infrequent, hard stretching
sessions.

## Ankle and hip mobility drills (for a limited get-up or squat pattern)

If a get-up or squat check points to a limited ankle or hip: kneeling ankle self-mobilization using
body weight; a toe-inclusive rocking drill; a seated position (sitting on one shin, other foot
forward) with the knee gliding over the toes in a "rainbow" arc; a tall half-kneel with reaching in
multiple directions to shift body weight; and a pull-assisted deep squat with side-to-side weight
shifting at the bottom. All of these are done slowly, breathing through the nose, without forcing —
the stated reason is to avoid triggering a protective, guarding response in the nervous system.
