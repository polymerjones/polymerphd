# 00 — README

**Polymer Ph.D. — Human Performance and Health Optimization knowledge package**

This folder contains a synthesised knowledge base built from YouTube transcripts on human
performance, stress physiology, energy, recovery, and health optimization — drawn from **multiple
different creators' channels**, unlike this project's other libraries, which each cover one
channel.

---

# What this is

**This package synthesises the teachings of several YouTube channels on a shared subject:** human
performance and health optimization. Every claim in it traces back to a specific video, from a
specific creator, at a specific timestamp.

Because sources come from more than one creator, this package does not treat them as one unified
voice. Where two sources agree or disagree, that is noted explicitly rather than blended into a
single house position. Each note names its own source creator directly in its prose and citations.

**Nothing in this package originates outside the source transcripts.**

# How it was built

The package follows the same two-stage process as this project's other libraries:

**Stage 1 — extraction.** Each transcript is read once and turned into a structured note
preserving central claims, glossary terms, analogies, the source's own confidence statements, and
`[mm:ss]` anchors back to the video.

**Stage 2 — synthesis.** The knowledge files are written **from the notes, never from raw
transcripts.**

That separation is the anti-fabrication mechanism. **If a claim is not in a note, it does not
appear in this package.**

# 01 — Core Principles

This library now holds 42 notes from two creators — Seth Capehart MD and Dr. Mike/Matt Jones —
covering physical training, nutrition, sleep, stress physiology, and attention/purpose. A handful
of patterns recur often enough across genuinely independent videos (different topics, sometimes
different creators) to treat as load-bearing. None of the two creators cite the other by name — the
convergence below is this library's own observation, drawn from reading the notes side by side, not
a claim either source makes about the other.

---

## 1. Chronic, low-grade stress is treated as more damaging than acute stress, almost everywhere

The single most repeated distinction in this collection: acute stress (a hard workout, a tough
conversation, a genuine emergency) is treated as harmless or even necessary, while *chronic,
never-fully-resolving* stress is named as a driver behind an unusually wide range of outcomes —
elevated blood pressure, worse cholesterol numbers, accelerated cellular aging (measured via
telomere length), brain fog and impaired hippocampal function, flattened motivation and drive, and
a shortened relative life expectancy comparable in magnitude to smoking. Both creators return to
this mechanism independently, applying it to different specific complaints, which is what makes it
worth naming as a principle rather than a single video's claim. The consistent fix offered across
sources is not eliminating stress (neither creator claims that's possible or desirable) but building
in genuine daily windows where the nervous system shifts out of sympathetic activation — quiet time
outdoors, breathwork, a walk without stimulation, real in-person connection.

## 2. Physical friction beats willpower for changing digital habits

Across several videos on attention, boredom, and reading, a specific practical claim repeats almost
verbatim: software-only interventions (screen time limits, app timers) get overridden in the moment,
while a physical barrier that requires a deliberate action to remove (a lockable device, a phone
left in another room) does not. This is offered as a mechanism, not just a tip — willpower is
described as a poor match for systems specifically engineered to be overridden in a moment of
boredom or impulse, while friction changes the default without requiring a fresh decision every
time.

## 3. Root-cause correction is favored over symptom management, especially for medication

Applied repeatedly to blood pressure, cholesterol, and GLP-1 weight-loss drugs: medication is
treated as a legitimate and sometimes necessary tool, but one source states directly and repeatedly
that a pill "manages the number" without addressing what's driving the number upward in the first
place — and that the same lifestyle inputs (real food, movement, sleep, stress management) that
would have prevented the problem are usually still the actual fix, with medication serving as a
bridge rather than a permanent substitute. This is stated with real nuance in each case: genetic
conditions (familial hypercholesterolemia, a small hereditary blood-pressure-elevation subset) are
explicitly carved out as requiring medication regardless of lifestyle.

## 4. Purpose and direction are treated as physiological inputs, not just psychological ones

Several videos, across both creators, argue that having a clear direction or an obligation that
matters changes the body's stress response and energy allocation, not merely mood — described in
terms of where effort and energy get physiologically "allocated" when there's a demand worth
answering. The absence of direction is described not as sadness but as a kind of idling — still
functioning, but flat. This is presented as a genuine input the nervous system responds to, on par
with sleep or diet, rather than a soft "find your why" platitude.

## 5. Deliberately dosed discomfort is treated as a stimulus the body needs, not a risk to avoid

Heat exposure, hard intervals, small contained failures, and even boredom are each independently
framed as a form of stress the body or mind is built to adapt to — with real, cited physiological
payoffs (cardiovascular training effects from heat, mitochondrial signaling from intensity,
skill-building from small failures, default-mode-network activity from boredom) — as long as the
dose is right and recovery follows. The recurring caution alongside this principle is that more
isn't better past a certain point, and several sources are explicit that the goal is a deliberately
bounded dose, not maximal or constant stress.

## 6. Subtraction beats addition when someone feels stuck or exhausted

Documented earliest in this library's history, and still holding: multiple sources, working from
different starting mechanisms (physiology, time-budget economics, cognitive load, dopamine
recalibration), independently land on protecting a small number of things deliberately rather than
adding more as the actual fix for overload. See each note's own "Conflicts with other sources"
section for the specific comparisons.

## 7. Self-correction is treated as a value, not a failure

At least one video in this library is an explicit, named walk-back of a claim the same creator had
previously promoted, citing new research directly rather than quietly dropping the earlier position.
It's included here as a reminder that even the more confidently stated claims elsewhere in this
library's notes are open to revision as better evidence surfaces — consistent with how this
project's own "Source-stated confidence" sections are meant to be read.

# 02 — Mechanisms and Systems

Physiological and neurological mechanisms this collection returns to, grouped by system. Each is
described here in general terms; see the individual notes for the specific claim, the source's own
hedges, and citations.

## Stress and the autonomic nervous system

- **Cortisol and chronic activation** — the collection distinguishes short, useful cortisol bursts
  from chronic, low-grade activation that never fully resolves. The latter is linked across
  multiple notes to elevated blood pressure, worse lipid numbers, impaired hippocampal function,
  and accelerated cellular aging.
- **Sympathetic versus parasympathetic state** — described repeatedly as a two-setting system
  ("fight or flight" versus "rest and digest/repair"), with modern life keeping most people
  effectively stuck in the first setting nearly all day from non-life-threatening stressors.
- **The vagus nerve** — named as the mechanical route into the parasympathetic state, activated
  deliberately through slow breathing, quiet time in nature, and unstimulated movement.
- **Allostatic load** — the cumulative biological wear from chronic stress, used to explain how
  loneliness and disconnection produce measurable physical health effects over time.

## Sleep

- **The glymphatic system** — active mainly during deep sleep, clearing metabolic waste (including
  amyloid beta) from the brain; the basis for describing under-slept mornings as running on
  "yesterday's garbage."
- **Deep sleep and hormone production** — growth hormone and a large share of daily testosterone
  production are tied to deep sleep specifically, not just total time in bed.
- **Circadian entrainment** — morning light exposure is repeatedly named as the primary signal
  setting the internal clock, downstream of which sit cortisol timing, later melatonin release, and
  metabolic rhythm.
- **Sleep regularity versus duration** — consistent sleep/wake timing is cited as mattering as much
  as, or more than, total hours, with irregular sleepers showing worse outcomes even at adequate
  duration.

## Appetite and metabolic hormones

- **Leptin and ghrelin** — the satiety and hunger hormones, respectively; both cited as measurably
  worsened (lower leptin, higher ghrelin) by excess body fat and by poor sleep, explaining why some
  people are genuinely hungrier at the same intake.
- **Aromatization** — the conversion of testosterone to estrogen by fat tissue, more pronounced
  with more visceral fat, compounding the hormonal barrier to fat loss.
- **GLP-1** — a naturally occurring gut hormone regulating insulin release, gastric emptying, and
  satiety; GLP-1 medications are a synthetic version delivered at a dose the body would never
  produce naturally.
- **Insulin and blood sugar swings** — repeatedly named as the mechanism behind energy crashes,
  cravings, and brain fog, driven by processed/refined carbohydrate intake.

## Cellular and molecular

- **Telomeres** — protective caps on chromosome ends that shorten with cell division; used as a
  measurable marker of cellular aging, cited as shortened by sustained chronic stress in a specific
  caregiver population by roughly the same magnitude attributed to smoking.
- **AMPK** — a cellular signaling pathway triggered by energy stress, central to mitochondrial
  adaptation; higher-intensity exercise activates it much more strongly than low-intensity steady
  cardio.
- **Phosphocreatine and ATP** — muscle's immediate energy reserve system; creatine supplementation
  increases available phosphocreatine, extending short maximal-effort output.
- **Heat shock proteins** — cellular repair molecules triggered by heat stress (e.g. sauna use),
  described as a maintenance crew cleaning up cell damage before it accumulates.
- **Nitric oxide** — a short-lived signaling molecule that relaxes blood vessels; released both by
  UVA sun exposure on skin and by shear stress from blood flow, contributing to blood pressure
  regulation through more than one pathway in this collection.
- **Cholesterol sulfate** — a separate UVB-triggered compound (distinct from vitamin D synthesis)
  used in cell membrane function throughout the body.

## Brain and attention

- **The default mode network** — active during unfocused, task-free mental states; credited with
  memory consolidation, processing experience, and creative idea generation, and disrupted by
  reaching for a phone at the first sign of boredom.
- **Dopamine as "wanting," not "liking"** — the collection is specific that dopamine drives pursuit
  and motivation more than pleasure itself, which is a separate system; this explains why someone
  can know an activity will feel good afterward and still lack the drive to start it.
- **Effort discounting and the anterior cingulate cortex** — the brain is described as computing
  whether an effort is "worth it" before conscious choice, continuously recalibrated by how much
  reward the environment has recently delivered for how little effort.
- **The frontoparietal control network** — the brain system(s) responsible for sustained attention,
  described as trainable (and losable) the same way physical fitness is.
- **Neuroplasticity** — the general mechanism underlying most of the above: repeated behavior
  reshapes the brain's default patterns, for shallow/fragmented attention just as readily as for
  depth and focus.
- **CYP1A2** — the gene controlling caffeine metabolism speed, explaining why identical doses
  produce very different alertness, anxiety, and sleep effects across different people.

## Musculoskeletal and structural aging

- **Sarcopenia** — age-related muscle loss beginning in the 30s, accelerating with age, and tied
  directly to fall risk and loss of independence.
- **Collagen decline and cross-linking** — tendons and ligaments become stiffer and less elastic
  with age, and tendon blood supply (already limited relative to muscle) declines further, slowing
  injury repair.
- **Alpha-2 adrenergic receptors** — found at higher density in lower-abdominal fat cells,
  explaining why that specific region resists fat mobilization longest, independent of overall
  progress.

# 03 — Practices and Protocols

Concrete protocols this collection describes, grouped by domain. Doses, timings, and any stated
contraindications are kept alongside each practice, per this project's standing rule that
cautions travel with the practice rather than being listed separately.

## Sleep

- **The "rule of three"** — pick three calming, low-stimulation actions, done in the same order
  each night (e.g. a calming tea, a breathing or journaling exercise, a non-stimulating physical
  book), until the sequence itself becomes a conditioned sleep trigger.
- **After a bad night:** a capped 20-30 minute nap before mid-afternoon; 10-15 minutes of real
  outdoor light first thing; caffeine front-loaded early and cut off by early-mid afternoon;
  optionally, a single high dose of creatine (0.35 g/kg) taken early in the day. Explicitly not a
  substitute for protecting the following night's sleep, which is described as the actual point of
  the day.
- **General target:** 7-9 hours, consistent bed/wake timing (within ~30-60 minutes daily including
  weekends), dark/cold/quiet room, no screens for the last hour, no late caffeine or alcohol.

## Training

- **Fall prevention** (aimed at maintaining independence into old age, though the underlying
  strength is built decades earlier): unilateral farmer's carries and the Turkish get-up,
  progressed slowly from bodyweight/light load; consult a physician first with an existing shoulder
  injury, hip replacement, or balance issue.
- **After 40:** dynamic (not static) mobility work built into warm-ups regardless of current pain;
  slower, more controlled lifting tempo, especially lowering; smaller, more gradual progressive
  overload since tendons adapt slower than muscle; explicit soreness-versus-pain distinction;
  planned deload weeks rather than waiting for the body to force one.
- **General strength foundation:** compound, multi-joint movements (squat, hinge, push, pull,
  carry) prioritized over isolation work; roughly 3-4 sessions/week, 30-60 minutes, is sufficient
  for most non-competitive goals; progressive overload (usually added weight, tracked week to week)
  is the actual driver of continued results.
- **Cardio balance:** for people training 3-6 hours/week, 2-3 sessions of genuinely uncomfortable
  higher-intensity work (15-20 minutes) plus 1-2 lower-intensity sessions, the latter used
  specifically on lower-recovery days; a simple "can you hold a full conversation" talk test
  substitutes for a heart rate monitor.
- **No-equipment/minimal-equipment training** is presented as fully viable: bodyweight movements
  alone, or a resistance-band set, can cover a complete program; a kettlebell and a doorway pull-up
  bar are named as the highest-value minimal equipment additions.

## Nutrition

- **Whole/"primal" eating** — meat, fish, eggs, vegetables, fruit, tubers, nuts, seeds; no
  processed food or added sugar. Applied across weight loss, cholesterol, blood pressure, and
  general energy/mood claims in this collection.
- **Elimination diet** (a diagnostic tool, not a permanent diet, and not appropriate for everyone):
  roughly 3 weeks removing common reactive foods (gluten, dairy, eggs, soy, corn, refined sugar,
  alcohol, often nightshades), followed by reintroducing one food at a time with a ~3-day
  observation window each, tracking symptoms throughout.
- **Dietary strictness calibrated to the size of the gap to a stated goal** — a large gap to close
  (or a general-health, non-aesthetic goal) tolerates roughly 70-85% adherence; a small remaining
  gap or a specific performance/aesthetic target requires much closer to 90%+ precision and actual
  tracking.
- **Closing a caloric deficit through added output rather than only restriction**, when appetite
  and recovery capacity allow it — cited as producing a different internal outcome (more muscle
  and cardiovascular capacity preserved) than the same deficit created by eating less alone.

## Supplementation

- **Creatine:** 3-5 g/day, any time, indefinitely; loading (20 g/day for a week) is optional, not
  necessary, and mainly causes more GI upset; a separate, much higher one-time dose (0.35 g/kg,
  roughly 20-30 g) is used specifically for acute sleep-deprivation cognitive support, not as a
  daily practice.
- **Sourcing and safety notes recur across supplement discussions:** favor third-party-tested
  brands; existing kidney disease warrants a physician conversation before starting creatine;
  magnesium glycinate specifically (not other forms) is the cited first-line sleep supplement,
  200-400 mg roughly 45 minutes before bed.

## Attention, digital habits, and purpose

- **Physical friction over willpower** for phone/app habits — a lockable device requiring a
  deliberate physical action beats software-only screen-time limits, which are described as
  routinely overridden in the moment.
- **Reading as a deliberate attention-training practice:** 10-20 minutes of a physical book (not a
  screen), phone in a different room, same time nightly, reading something genuinely enjoyable
  rather than "productivity" material.
- **Deliberate boredom windows:** unstimulated time built into the day on purpose (a walk without
  audio, a commute without a podcast, sitting with no plan) rather than filling every gap
  automatically.
- **A daily quiet-outdoor-time practice** (commonly cited around 10 minutes) as a specific,
  repeatable parasympathetic-activation practice, reported across multiple sources as
  disproportionately effective relative to how simple it is.
- **Values clarification exercises** — explicitly defining what a good life looks like for oneself
  (not inherited from career, family, or social comparison), and protecting time for it on the
  calendar as a non-negotiable rather than leftover time.

# 04 — Signals and Self-Assessment

Symptoms, feelings, and self-observable signals this collection describes, gathered from the
individual notes' "Symptoms and body signals addressed" sections. Organized as clusters, since
several sources explicitly connect symptoms across categories that look unrelated at first glance.

## Flatness, low drive, and burnout that isn't quite depression

- Things that used to feel worth doing (a workout, a book, a project) no longer feeling worth
  doing, despite being fully capable of doing them.
- Functioning normally on the outside while quietly feeling depleted, wired-but-tired, or "not
  really there."
- A sense of going through the motions despite an objectively fine life (job, sleep, labs all
  "normal" on paper).
- Winds and small wins not landing the way they used to.

**Explicitly flagged across multiple sources:** these signals overlap heavily with real, treatable
medical conditions (thyroid dysfunction, anemia, sleep apnea, depression), and more than one note
states directly that a medical workup should happen before concluding the cause is
lifestyle/existential rather than physiological.

## Cognitive: brain fog, attention, and reading difficulty

- Trouble concentrating, rereading the same paragraph, losing a train of thought mid-sentence.
- A reliable early-afternoon energy/cognition wall.
- Buying books and never finishing them; losing engagement a few pages in; picking up a phone with
  no memory of deciding to.
- Blanking on why you walked into a room.

## Sleep debt, specifically

- Deliberate effort now required for previously automatic tasks (driving, following conversation).
- A short fuse, reduced patience, worse decisions — described as measurable, not just subjective.
- Craving processed/sugary food more than usual.
- Multiple awakenings that fragment sleep depth even when total time in bed looks adequate.

## Physical and structural signals

- A stubborn lower-abdominal fat layer persisting despite otherwise good body composition —
  described as anatomical, not a sign something is broken.
- Feeling hungrier than expected at a given intake — described as a real, measurable hormonal
  effect (leptin/ghrelin), not a willpower failure.
- Sharp, localized pain that changes movement and worsens with activity — explicitly distinguished
  from ordinary training soreness (dull, delayed, improves with movement).
- A reflexive, automatic reach for a phone during any unoccupied moment — named repeatedly as a
  useful self-diagnostic for trained overstimulation.

## Silent/asymptomatic risk markers

Several notes emphasize that some of the most consequential signals produce **no felt symptom at
all** until late: elevated blood pressure, elevated cholesterol, and early muscle loss (sarcopenia)
are all described as symptomless for long stretches, which is presented as the specific reason they
deserve proactive measurement rather than waiting to feel something wrong.

## Explicitly distinguished from the above: signals requiring urgent medical attention

A few notes are careful to name a specific presentation that is *not* what the rest of the note
describes, and requires emergency evaluation rather than a lifestyle response — most commonly sharp,
sudden, severe pain paired with fever or a sudden focal neurological change (weakness, loss of
speech). These are flagged individually in the relevant notes and are not general wellness signals.

# 05 — Cross-Source Comparison

This library intentionally spans two creators (Seth Capehart MD and Dr. Mike/Matt Jones) plus,
where genuinely relevant, this project's other libraries. Each individual note's own "Conflicts
with other sources" section is the primary, timestamped record of these comparisons; this file
summarizes the patterns that showed up repeatedly once enough notes existed to see them.

## Strong convergence between the two creators, arrived at independently

Neither creator references the other by name anywhere in this collection. That makes it notable
when both land on the same conclusion from different starting points:

- **Subtraction over addition** as the fix for feeling overloaded or exhausted — one creator
  reasons from cognitive load and dopamine recalibration, the other from physiology and a
  societal time-budget argument. Same practical answer, different mechanism.
- **Chronic stress as more damaging than acute stress**, applied by one creator to cardiovascular
  and metabolic markers (blood pressure, cholesterol, cellular aging) and by the other to
  motivation, drive, and cognitive function. Neither treats stress itself as avoidable; both treat
  daily parasympathetic recovery as the actual lever.
- **Fitness and cardiorespiratory capacity mattering independently of body weight or appearance** —
  cited via population mortality data by one creator and via a specific "fat but fit" research
  finding by the other.
- **Physical friction beating willpower** for digital habits — both creators independently arrived
  at, and specifically recommend, the same category of lockable physical device rather than
  software-only limits, for the identical stated reason (software limits get overridden in the
  moment).

## Genuine tensions worth holding onto rather than smoothing over

- **Coffee/caffeine:** one video in this library's companion nutrition library argues against
  coffee specifically for methylxanthine-driven mineral depletion, while this library's dedicated
  caffeine video cites a large meta-analysis finding a mortality benefit around 3-3.5 cups/day.
  These aren't strictly incompatible (mineral loss could coexist with a net benefit at the
  population level), but neither source engages with the other's specific claim, and a reader
  should treat this as an open tension, not a resolved one.
- **Creatine, daily use versus food-first framing:** one creatine-focused video argues food should
  be the default source of nutrients like this where possible, while explicitly treating creatine
  as an exception worth supplementing directly since it's "just applied battery physics," not a
  whole-food nutrient profile — a distinction worth noting rather than treating all supplement
  recommendations as following one consistent rule.
- **Explore/fail small versus protect a few things intensely:** one video argues for deliberately
  trying many small, cheap, fast experiments; another argues for cutting distractions down to a
  small number of protected priorities. Read together rather than in isolation, these describe two
  different phases (exploring what's worth committing to, versus protecting something once it's
  working) rather than a real contradiction — but a reader skimming only one of the two could walk
  away with the opposite advice.

## A demonstrated self-correction, worth treating as a model

One video in this collection is an explicit, named reversal of a claim (zone 2 cardio's uniqueness
for mitochondrial health) the same creator had previously promoted on the same channel, citing a
specific 2025 research review rather than quietly dropping the earlier position. It's flagged here
because it's the clearest instance in this library of a source updating on evidence in public, and
it's a reasonable model for how to hold every other confidently stated claim in this collection —
provisionally, not as settled fact.

## Where this library connects to this project's other libraries

- The restorative-physiology library's treatment of general adaptation syndrome, progressive
  overload, and detraining timelines is a more physiologically detailed companion to this library's
  training-related notes, which tend to stay at a practical, prescription level.
- The restorative-physiology library's coverage of isometric wall sits (a specific, well-studied
  blood-pressure intervention) and this library's coverage of sauna use converge on a shared
  underlying vascular mechanism (nitric oxide, endothelial signaling) reached through two very
  different practices.
- The nutrition library's Paul Saladino content and this library's Seth Capehart content converge
  heavily on general dietary philosophy (whole/unprocessed food, seed oil and processed sugar as
  primary dietary concerns) despite being different creators and different libraries — worth
  reading as independent corroboration where the underlying claims overlap.

# 06 — Open Questions

What this corpus doesn't yet resolve, or leaves genuinely unsettled — either because the sources
themselves flag uncertainty, or because two sources point in different directions.

## Flagged as uncertain by the sources themselves

- **Whether specific mortality/risk comparisons across different causes are apples-to-apples** —
  more than one video compares a lifestyle risk factor (loneliness, chronic stress) to smoking in
  magnitude, while explicitly cautioning that the comparison is illustrative, not a precise
  equivalence.
- **How much of a cited sex-based injury-risk gap (ACL tears) is pure biology versus differences in
  training history, coaching, and resource access** — named directly as a live scientific debate in
  the source covering it, not resolved one way or the other.
- **Whether population-level "Blue Zones"-style longevity research holds up** — one source uses the
  concept of long-lived, socially tight-knit communities as an illustration while explicitly stating
  the broader research on why certain regions produce so many long-lived people is still debated
  among scientists.
- **The precise dose-response relationship for several emerging findings** — high-dose creatine for
  acute sleep deprivation, and a proposed link between creatine and Alzheimer's progression, are
  both named as promising but based on small, early-stage studies rather than settled evidence.

## Real tensions between sources in this library

- See `05_CROSS_SOURCE_COMPARISON.md` for the coffee/mineral-depletion-versus-mortality-benefit
  tension, and the explore-small-failures-versus-protect-few-priorities framing, neither of which
  this collection resolves definitively.

## Gaps in current coverage

- Neither creator's videos in this collection address female-specific hormonal cycles (beyond
  training-intensity planning) or pregnancy/postpartum recovery in depth.
- Nutrition coverage in this library leans heavily on one dietary philosophy (whole-food,
  primal/ancestral eating); this library does not yet contain a source arguing a substantially
  different nutritional framework for direct comparison.
- Supplementation coverage is currently limited to creatine and a brief mention of a magnesium/
  melatonin/valerian sleep stack; most other popular supplements in this space are not yet covered.

This file will grow as more videos are added and as genuine disagreements or unresolved questions
surface across new notes.
