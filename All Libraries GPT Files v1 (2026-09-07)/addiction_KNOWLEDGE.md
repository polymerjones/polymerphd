# 00 — README

This package synthesizes {videos} sources on addiction, compulsion, and behavior change — drawn
from creators across psychology, journalism, and self-improvement writing, not a single channel or
clinical corpus. It covers phone/tech addiction, habit mechanics, goal and values psychology, and
nervous-system-based behavior change.

## What this collection is, and isn't

Every video here approaches "why do we keep doing the thing we don't want to do, and what actually
breaks the pattern" from a different discipline — a habit-science journalist, two sports-and-
performance writers working from one book, a clinical psychologist, a productivity author, and a
philosophy-and-social-psychology writer. None of them are addiction-medicine clinicians treating
substance-use disorder directly; several explicitly reach for addiction and substance-dependence
comparisons (heroin, "the same neurochemistry") as a rhetorical anchor for describing modern
behavioral traps like phone use and status-chasing, not as clinical claims about diagnosable
addiction. That distinction is worth keeping in mind throughout this package.

## How this package is organized

- **01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md** — the patterns that recur across separately-produced videos from
  different creators, treated as this collection's load-bearing ideas.
- **02_MECHANISMS_OF_COMPULSION.md** — the "why": the specific mechanisms each source names for why
  a pattern persists (dopamine, the habit loop, nervous-system familiarity, disevolution).
- **03_BREAKING_THE_LOOP.md** — the "what to do": practices and techniques collected across sources
  for interrupting or replacing a compulsive pattern.
- **04_SIGNALS_AND_SELF_ASSESSMENT.md** — the internal states and signals sources say to watch for,
  gathered across all eleven notes.
- **05_CROSS_SOURCE_COMPARISON.md** — where different creators' frameworks converge, extend each
  other, or use different vocabulary for what looks like the same underlying claim.
- **06_OPEN_QUESTIONS.md** — claims stated without a named source, internally hedged, or otherwise
  worth treating with more caution than the collection's more directly cited material.
- **07_PLAIN_ENGLISH_GLOSSARY.md** — every named term, alphabetically, no citations.
- **08_QUESTIONS_AND_ANSWERS.md** — worked example answers in this package's own voice.
- **09_SOURCE_CATALOG.md** — every source, auto-generated, regenerates itself after each new note.

Per this project's provenance rule, files 01–08 carry no video-ID citations — only the individual
notes in this library's `notes/` folder do that.

## From 01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md

# 01 — Core Principles

Six patterns recur across this collection often enough to treat as load-bearing, even though no
single source states them as a shared list — each emerged independently, from a different
discipline, on a different video. Read these first.

---

## 1. Change comes from small, sustained increments — not willpower spikes or sweeping reinvention

The single most repeated claim in this collection, arrived at from habit science, sports
performance, and clinical psychology independently: lasting change is built from the smallest
workable version of a new behavior, repeated consistently, rather than from a dramatic act of will
or a total life overhaul. A habit gets changed one substituted routine at a time, not extinguished
outright. A goal survives on process, not on the willpower that started it. A nervous-system
pattern shifts through daily "small promises," not through shame-driven overnight change. A
resisted task gets started by negotiating its scope down until it's small enough to actually
begin. The shared logic: whatever mechanism is maintaining the old pattern (a neural loop, a
nervous-system habit, fading motivation) doesn't respond to a single act of force, only to
repeated, low-friction pressure in a new direction.

## 2. The behavior isn't a character flaw — it's a mechanism operating beneath conscious control

Every source in this collection is explicit that the target behavior — checking a phone, a food
craving, procrastination, an old relational pattern — is not evidence of weak character or
insufficient willpower. Different sources locate the mechanism differently (a dopamine
anticipation-of-reward signal, a cue-routine-reward loop automated by the basal ganglia, a nervous
system defending a familiar pattern, an "emotional brain" that drives while the rational mind only
narrates), but the shared move is the same: relocate the explanation from moral failing to
mechanism. This matters practically — several sources argue that shame or self-blame applied to the
behavior actively works against changing it, rather than motivating change.

## 3. Comfort-seeking and avoiding discomfort is the trap, not the escape from one

A second recurring reversal: several sources argue that the behaviors people reach for to feel
better — numbing a negative emotion with a screen, chasing a quick dopamine hit, optimizing away
every inconvenience, pursuing happiness directly — are themselves what sustains the underlying
problem, while deliberately tolerating discomfort (boredom, a plateau, an uncomfortable feeling as
it's actually felt) is what actually resolves it. This shows up as: comfort with boredom being a
prerequisite for flow; a plateau being a required, not failed, phase of progress; a stress reaction
needing to be felt and regulated rather than immediately soothed away; and genuine excellence
requiring effortful, sometimes unpleasant practice rather than optimized comfort.

## 4. Identity is both the trap and the lever

Behavior in this collection is repeatedly tied to identity rather than treated as a free-floating
habit. A single changed habit can cascade into unrelated life changes by altering self-image. A
threat to identity-certainty — even a *positive* one, like a good opportunity — can trigger
avoidance. Long-standing patterns are described as adaptations formed in childhood that got fused
with a person's sense of self. And the proposed fix, in more than one source, is to either loosen
identity-certainty (accepting more uncertainty about who one is) or anchor identity in something
external and stable (a cause, a relationship, a craft) rather than in the outcome of the behavior in
question. Both moves treat identity as the actual lever, with the surface behavior downstream of it.

## 5. Genuine values, not stated ones, determine whether a change sticks

Multiple independent frameworks in this collection converge on the same underlying claim: people
are unreliable narrators of their own priorities, and a goal or habit that doesn't actually connect
to a person's real (not claimed) values will be quietly resisted or abandoned, no matter how
sensible it looks from outside. The proposed diagnostics differ (an honest audit of where time
actually goes; noticing an involuntary "that is me" reaction to a named value; noticing which
change feels irrationally frightening) but the underlying logic is the same: surface the actual
value, then either build the change around it or reframe the change's language to connect with it.

## 6. Modern environments exploit old mechanisms built for a different world

Several sources frame the target behavior as evolutionarily explicable rather than a modern
personal failing: dopamine's anticipation-of-reward mechanism evolved for genuine scarcity and is
now triggered by notifications and slot machines; cravings shaped by historical food scarcity are
now met with engineered abundance; a device that promises certainty about one's own body ("your
recovery score") substitutes for felt, embodied judgment evolution actually equipped people with.
The shared implication is that the pull toward the compulsive behavior is not a personal defect to
be ashamed of — it is a predictable consequence of an old system meeting a new environment, which
several sources treat as a reason for self-compassion rather than resignation.

# 02 — Mechanisms of Compulsion

The specific "why" each source in this collection offers for why a behavior persists. Organized by
mechanism, not by source — several sources describe the same underlying phenomenon in different
vocabulary, cross-referenced here.

---

## The dopamine anticipation-of-reward loop

Dopamine is described not as a pleasure signal but as an anticipation-of-reward signal — it drives
seeking and checking behavior rather than delivering satisfaction itself. This is the stated
mechanism behind phone-checking, notification-seeking, and slot-machine design, and is explicitly
described as running on "the same neurochemistry" whether the trigger is a substance, a phone, or a
gambling device. The reward being "juiced" artificially (methamphetamine cited as producing roughly
ten times a natural reward's dopamine response) is the same mechanism, at a different intensity,
across substances and behaviors alike.

## The habit loop (cue, routine, reward)

A separate but compatible mechanism: the basal ganglia automates any behavior that reliably follows
a cue-routine-reward pattern, regardless of whether the behavior benefits the person. This loop
cannot be erased once formed — the neural connection persists indefinitely — which is why change
has to work by substituting a new routine at the same cue, delivering a similar reward, rather than
attempting to simply stop the old behavior. Misidentifying the actual reward being sought (assuming
it's the obvious one, like taste or energy, when it's actually something like social connection) is
named as a common reason habit-change attempts fail.

## Nervous-system familiarity and identity-fused patterns

A third mechanism, from clinical/nervous-system framing rather than neurochemistry: the nervous
system develops a preference for familiar patterns — including dysfunctional ones — because
familiarity itself registers as safety. Patterns adapted in childhood (overachieving, shutting down
in conflict) can become fused with identity, socially rewarded as "drive" or being "easy," while
remaining unhealthy for the person living them. Change, in this framing, works by consciously
noticing a stress reaction building in the body and deliberately responding differently in the
moment, which is described as literally strengthening a different neural pathway over repeated
practice.

## Identity-threat avoidance

A distinct mechanism describes avoidance as driven by whatever threatens a person's certainty about
their own identity — and explicitly, this applies symmetrically to threatening *and* beneficial
things. A good opportunity or a healthy relationship can be sabotaged by the same mechanism that
makes someone avoid a hard conversation, because both threaten an existing self-concept. This
reframes some instances of self-sabotage as identity-protection working exactly as designed, not as
a special kind of self-destructiveness.

## Values misalignment producing involuntary resistance

Two independently-arrived-at frameworks describe resistance to a goal or behavior as a symptom of
misalignment with a person's actual (not stated) values, rather than a motivation problem to be
solved with more willpower. A goal that conflicts with a person's strongest value — for instance,
one coded as pure conformity to someone who values self-direction — is described as provoking a
visceral, involuntary "get this out of my face" reaction, distinct from ordinary difficulty.

## Disevolution: engineered abundance meeting evolved scarcity-cravings

A specific evolutionary-mismatch mechanism: cravings for calorie-dense food, social connection, and
validation evolved under conditions of scarcity, and modern engineered environments (fast food,
social media, pornography) exploit those same cravings under conditions of abundance the evolved
mechanism was never calibrated for. This is offered as an explanation for why willpower alone
struggles against these environments — the craving mechanism is functioning correctly, just against
an environment radically different from the one that shaped it.

## Comfort and optimization as their own trap

A final mechanism, somewhat different in kind from the others: excessive self-optimization and
tracking is described as producing fragility rather than resilience, by substituting a device's
measurement for a person's own embodied judgment. Treating an external score (a "recovery score," a
step count) as more authoritative than lived experience is described as "sucking the feeling" out
of performance and making a person's capability contingent on conditions being externally validated
as correct — the inverse problem from the other mechanisms above, but converging on the same
practical advice: trust and rebuild felt judgment rather than outsourcing it entirely.

## From 03_BREAKING_THE_LOOP.md

# 03 — Breaking the Loop

Practices collected across this collection, organized by what they're used for. These are the
sources' own stated techniques — nothing here is invented or generalized beyond what a source
actually describes doing.

---

## Diagnosing what's actually driving a habit

Before changing a habit, isolate its actual cue and reward rather than assuming them: track the
five categories present whenever a craving hits (time, location, company, emotional state, and the
immediately preceding action) to isolate the cue; then substitute different candidate rewards one
at a time to find which one actually satisfies the craving, since the obvious assumed reward (taste,
energy) is often wrong. A related diagnostic works on values rather than habits: track actual time
spent across a week honestly against how much each activity is claimed to matter, to expose gaps
between stated and lived priorities.

## Replacing, not extinguishing, a habit

Once a habit's cue and reward are identified, design a specific new routine — performed at the same
cue, delivering a similar reward — rather than attempting to simply stop the old behavior, since the
old neural connection persists regardless of whether it's still used. This is described as an
"implementation intention": a concrete substitute action decided on in advance, not a vague
intention to "do better."

## Nervous-system regulation in the moment

When a stress reaction is building (tightening chest, quickening breath, a rising urge to act
immediately), the described technique is to notice the sensation as it builds, deliberately relax
tensed muscles and slow the breath, and pause before reacting — rather than immediately coping by
acting on the urge to end the discomfort quickly. Naming several neutral or comforting objects in
one's immediate environment is offered as a way to orient to present safety and further activate a
calmer physiological state. A daily standalone practice — a brief pause to notice bodily sensation,
breath, and current thoughts, done at a consistent trigger point — is described as the foundational
awareness training that later makes in-the-moment regulation possible.

## Structuring goals so they survive fading motivation

A goal should be treated as a prediction to be revised with feedback, not a guaranteed outcome to
be judged pass/fail. A recurring four-part cycle appears across sources in compatible form: define
both a goal's outcome and its process; act on it without relying on initial motivation, since
desire is expected to fade well before completion; revise the goal itself when the evidence (not
doing it anymore) suggests it needs editing rather than more willpower; and maintain a goal after
success, since automated, no-longer-effortful behaviors are prone to being dropped and forgotten
once they stop feeling like active accomplishments.

## Countering specific reasons a task feels resistant

A task or goal becomes aversive for identifiable, addressable reasons — being boring, frustrating,
unpleasant, unstructured, far off in the future, or disconnected from a real value — and each has a
named countermeasure: gamifying a boring task, pairing an unpleasant one with something enjoyable,
setting milestones for something distant, adding external accountability for something
unstructured, and reconnecting a task's language to a genuine value without necessarily changing
its underlying actions. When resistance itself is the obstacle (procrastination), negotiating a
task's scope down to the smallest version a person is actually willing to start is described as
effective specifically because resistance concentrates at the beginning of a task rather than
spreading evenly through it.

## Reconnecting a stuck goal to genuine values

When a goal or task provokes disproportionate resistance, the described fix is not to abandon it
but to identify which underlying value it's actually in tension with, then reframe the goal's
language (not its substance) to connect with a value the person actually holds. The same logic
appears in reverse as a diagnostic: noticing what a person gravitates toward during genuinely
unstructured time reveals real values more reliably than what they'd claim to prioritize if asked
directly.

## Distinguishing helpful immersion from harmful immersion

Rather than judging an absorbing activity by how it feels during the experience, several sources
point to how a person feels once the experience ends: exhaustion combined with fulfillment
suggests a genuinely valuable immersive state, while exhaustion combined with regret, restlessness,
or an urge to "reset" suggests an immersive state that mimics the first but runs against the
person's actual values.

## Working with plateaus and setbacks rather than against them

A plateau or a lapse is treated across sources as an expected, even necessary, phase — not evidence
that an approach has failed. The described response is to shift the source of motivation from
externally visible progress toward curiosity, values-alignment, and consistency, and to explicitly
avoid self-shame after a lapse, since shame is described as working against the very nervous-system
or habit change being attempted.

## From 04_SIGNALS_AND_SELF_ASSESSMENT.md

# 04 — Signals and Self-Assessment

Internal states and observable signals this collection's sources say to watch for, gathered across
all eleven notes and grouped by what they indicate. Matches this library's "Signals and internal
states addressed" section, aggregated here rather than left scattered per-note.

---

## Signs a habit or compulsion is running on autopilot, not choice

- A craving that persists despite satisfying the assumed reward (sugar, caffeine, a snack) — a sign
  the actual reward hasn't been correctly identified yet.
- Reflexive, repeated urges to check a device — more than once every five minutes when it's put
  away is offered as one source's own rough self-test for behavioral dependence.
- Feeling restless, anxious, or in need of a "reset" after an absorbing digital session — a
  retrospective marker that the immersion ran counter to actual values, distinct from a fulfilling
  kind of absorption.
- Long stretches of acting without noticing a choice point was ever available — described as time
  spent in default, conditioned intention rather than deliberate choice.

## Signs of identity-protective avoidance

- Avoiding a *good* opportunity, not just a bad one, or sabotaging a relationship that's going
  well — the counterintuitive signature of identity-threat avoidance, which applies symmetrically
  to beneficial and harmful things.
- A disproportionate, "irrational" anxiety response to an objectively low-stakes change — reframed
  as a positive diagnostic (a keystone habit or identity-linked pattern worth deliberately
  confronting) rather than a reason to avoid the change.
- A visceral, "get this out of my face" resistance to a specific goal or task — named as a values
  conflict, not a motivation or willpower deficiency.

## Signs of values misalignment

- Claiming to value something (honesty, health) while consistently acting against it — described
  as the ordinary, unremarkable way self-deception about one's own values shows up, not a special
  kind of hypocrisy.
- A goal that sounded appealing when set but is now actively resented in daily practice — a
  mismatch between the idea of a goal and its lived reality.
- Consistently not doing something despite genuinely intending to — treated as the signal that the
  goal itself (not the person's discipline) needs to be revised.
- Feeling paralyzed or adrift when facing many possible paths — named as a symptom of values
  unclarity that gets *harder*, not easier, as available options increase.

## Signs of the "comfort trap"

- Feeling that an external device's score or metric should override one's own judgment about
  readiness or capability — named as a source of fragility rather than reliability.
- Exhaustion, apathy, and dread despite a low or moderate workload — distinguished from
  conventional overwork burnout, and attributed instead to insufficient meaningful engagement.
- Judging oneself for not feeling "happy enough," or for feeling discomfort during otherwise
  meaningful work — reframed as a symptom of treating happiness itself as the goal, rather than a
  sign something is wrong.

## Signs of productive struggle, not failure

- Progress stalling after an initial period of fast, visible gains — reframed as the ordinary
  transition into a plateau, itself often a necessary precondition for a breakthrough, not a sign
  the approach has failed.
- Feeling disoriented or unfulfilled immediately after achieving a long-pursued goal — a named,
  common reaction ("the arrival fallacy"), not evidence the goal was the wrong one.
- Difficulty translating a new insight into consistent action — framed as an expected friction
  between insight and an already-established nervous-system or habitual pattern, not a personal
  failing.
- A sense that self-awareness about one's own patterns arrived "too late" — explicitly countered in
  this collection as a false framing; awareness is treated as always usable, not time-limited.

## From 05_CROSS_SOURCE_COMPARISON.md

# 05 — Cross-Source Comparison

Where this collection's creators converge, extend each other, or use different vocabulary for what
looks like the same underlying claim. Individual notes carry the specific, timestamped version of
each comparison below — this file gathers the pattern across all of them.

---

## Convergence: willpower is not the mechanism, and shouldn't be the strategy

Every source that addresses behavior change directly arrives at some version of the same claim —
that the target behavior runs on a mechanism other than conscious willpower, using different
vocabulary: a dopamine anticipation-of-reward signal; an automated cue-routine-reward loop; an
emotionally-driven "feeling brain" that a rational "thinking brain" can only steer, not override; a
nervous system defending a familiar pattern. None of these sources appear to be responding to each
other directly — the convergence looks like independent arrival at a similar conclusion from
different disciplinary starting points (habit science, neuroscience communication, philosophy,
clinical psychology).

## Convergence: small steps beat large ones, across every framework

The "small, sustained increment" principle (see `01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md`) shows up in essentially
every source that offers a change technique, using different names: an "implementation intention"
(habit science), keeping most effort at 5-7 out of 10 intensity and stopping "one rep short"
(sports performance), a "small daily promise" (clinical psychology), and shrinking a resisted task
down until it's small enough to start (productivity writing). These are not the same technique, but
they share a common shape and appear to be independently-derived versions of one underlying idea.

## Convergence: two independent value-alignment frameworks

Two sources independently converge on the claim that a goal or behavior's durability depends on
alignment with a person's genuine values, using incompatible but complementary specific frameworks:
one names three abstract tests for a good value (immediate/controllable, reality-based, socially
constructive) plus practical exercises (a time audit, a deathbed exercise) for surfacing actual
values; the other cites a named academic taxonomy of twelve specific values and a five-layer
"intention stack" connecting values down to moment-to-moment action. Despite different structures,
both treat a values conflict as the explanation for otherwise-unexplained resistance to a
reasonable-looking goal.

## Notable coincidence: identical phrasing, different targets

Two different creators in this collection each use the phrase "sepia-toned" to describe something
that looks appealing as an idea but is disliked in lived reality — one applies it to *goals*
specifically (a goal that sounds good in the abstract but is resented once actually lived day to
day), the other to *values or identity aspirations* more broadly. Both are Big Think Clips uploads,
which makes a shared editorial or cultural source for the phrase plausible, but nothing in either
transcript indicates the two speakers are quoting or referencing each other — treated here as an
interesting convergence in language rather than a documented influence.

## Extension: the same reframe applied to different domains

Several pairs of sources apply what amounts to the same reframe to different specific domains
rather than genuinely disagreeing: a plateau in physical training and a plateau in personal-pattern
change are both reframed as a necessary, invisible-tension-building phase rather than a stalled
process; the "arrival fallacy" (achievement not producing expected fulfillment) is illustrated with
both a championship win and, in a different source, a thought-experiment about permanent
guaranteed pleasure (the "experience machine") — both concluding that the sought-after end state
doesn't deliver what was expected of it, from a sports-psychology angle in one case and a
philosophical angle in the other.

## Explicit addiction/substance comparisons as a rhetorical device

Two sources reach for direct addiction or substance-dependence comparisons to make a point about a
non-substance behavior: one explicitly states that phone use is "the same neurochemistry" as
substance addiction; another compares a philosophical thought experiment about guaranteed pleasure
directly to "a heroin addiction" narrowing a person's life. Neither source is presenting a clinical
diagnosis or citing addiction-medicine research — both use the comparison rhetorically, to make an
abstract mechanism (dopamine anticipation, the pull of guaranteed pleasure) feel concrete via a
widely-understood reference point. Worth keeping the distinction visible: this collection's sources
are describing behavioral patterns and drawing an analogy to addiction, not making claims about
diagnosable substance-use disorder.

# 06 — Open Questions

Claims in this collection that are stated without a named source, hedged by the source itself, or
otherwise worth treating with more caution than the collection's more directly cited material.
Nothing here is resolved — the point is to keep the derived-versus-stated distinction visible
rather than smoothing it away.

---

## Claims stated without a named source

- A prefrontal-cortex mechanism for how repeated in-the-moment self-regulation "rewires" a reactive
  nervous-system pattern is stated as fact but without naming a specific study.
- A critique that SMART goals "don't work" per unspecified research, and a "research waste" claim
  around the SMART framework generally, are both relayed without naming the underlying research.
- Specific procrastination statistics (15-20% of adults chronically procrastinate; a near-universal
  99% admission rate in one survey) are attributed to an unnamed "procrastination researcher" and an
  unnamed survey.
- A claim that a particular thought-technique (visualizations, affirmations, mirror self-talk)
  measurably works better for people who don't need it than for people who do is attributed to
  unnamed research.

## Claims explicitly flagged by the source itself as uncertain or speculative

- One source is explicit that a chain of reasoning connecting lactate to a "precursor to happiness"
  is "not a substantiated theory" — offered as the speaker's own extrapolation, not a settled
  finding, and flagged as such directly in the source material.
- Cross-culturally lowest-rated status of a specific human value, despite the same value's
  structural necessity in some social roles, is presented descriptively without methodological
  detail on how the ranking was established.

## Places where this collection's coverage is thin

- No source in this collection addresses diagnosable, clinical substance-use disorder directly —
  every treatment of "addiction" here is either about a specific non-substance behavior (phones,
  screens, status-chasing) or uses addiction/substance-dependence as an illustrative comparison
  rather than its actual subject. A user asking about clinical addiction treatment, withdrawal
  management, or diagnosis would not be well served by this collection alone.
- Commercial interest is disclosed directly by more than one source (a paid certification, program,
  book, or community tied to the framework being taught) — worth surfacing when relaying a claim
  from a source that also monetizes the framework it's describing, consistent with how each
  individual note already flags this where it's stated.
