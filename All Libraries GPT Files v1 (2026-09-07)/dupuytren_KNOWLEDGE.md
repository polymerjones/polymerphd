# Dupuytren — Knowledge Base Overview

This package synthesizes 145 sources on Dupuytren's contracture: recorded conference talks (the
2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium and the 2015 Dupuytren Symposium make up the bulk of the corpus),
standalone lectures and campaign videos from the Dupuytren Foundation, peer-reviewed systematic
reviews and journal articles, and a small number of patient-generated sources (Reddit and YouTube
comment threads).

**This is a dense, clinical, conference-proceedings corpus, not a patient-education pamphlet.**
Most sources are surgeons, hand therapists, and basic scientists talking to each other — the
material assumes familiarity with hand anatomy and clinical trial methodology, and the notes
preserve that register rather than simplifying it. Where a patient-voice source appears (the
Reddit thread, the YouTube comments), it is flagged as such and read differently: as anecdote and
lived experience, not as a controlled finding.

## What's in each file

- **`01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md`** — the handful of ideas that recur across nearly every talk in this
  collection, regardless of which specific treatment or mechanism the speaker is presenting.
- **`02_CELL_AND_MOLECULAR_BIOLOGY.md`** — the myofibroblast-centered biology of the disease: what
  drives contraction, what drives spread, and the signaling pathways (TGF-beta, Wnt/beta-catenin,
  TNF, MMP/TIMP) researchers are targeting.
- **`03_CLINICAL_COURSE_AND_DIAGNOSIS.md`** — how the disease actually behaves over time and across
  populations: natural history, staging, epidemiology, associated conditions, and the long-standing
  absence of a diagnostic or predictive biomarker.
- **`04_TREATMENT_MODALITIES.md`** — the major interventions (needle fasciotomy, collagenase, open
  fasciectomy and its variants, radiotherapy, splinting) and the evidence behind each.
- **`05_RECURRENCE_AND_OUTCOME_MEASUREMENT.md`** — why "did it come back" is a far harder question
  than it sounds, and how that measurement problem distorts comparisons between treatments.
- **`06_PATIENT_EXPERIENCE_AND_PROMS.md`** — what patients actually report wanting, how that differs
  from what a surgeon can measure, and the tools (and their limits) built to capture it.
- **`07_PLAIN_ENGLISH_GLOSSARY.md`** — every technical term introduced across the collection,
  defined in plain English, alphabetical, no citations.
- **`08_QUESTIONS_AND_ANSWERS.md`** — worked example answers in this package's house style. Not yet
  populated for this library (see that file).
- **`09_SOURCE_CATALOG.md`** — auto-generated index of every source; do not hand-edit.

## A note on scope

This collection is overwhelmingly hand-surgery and hand-surgery-adjacent in its source material.
It has essentially nothing on primary care recognition, physical therapy as a sole non-surgical
management path, or long-term disability accommodation — a reader looking for those angles would
not be well served by this collection alone.

# 01 — Core Principles

The ideas below recur across many independent talks, spanning different decades, institutions, and
sub-specialties within this collection. They are not any single speaker's claim — they are the
patterns visible once dozens of talks are read side by side.

---

## Every current treatment manages the deformity, not the disease

Needle fasciotomy, collagenase injection, limited fasciectomy, dermofasciectomy, and radiotherapy
all reduce or remove the visible contracture. None of them stops the underlying disease process
that produced it. Multiple speakers state this as the field's defining limitation rather than a
footnote: surgeons are, as one speaker puts it, "the vehicle to go from the beginning to the end"
of a single episode, but chronic biologic disease control is a different job than the one surgery
is built to do. This is why recurrence is treated by virtually every speaker in this collection as
an expected feature of the disease, not a treatment failure — see
`05_RECURRENCE_AND_OUTCOME_MEASUREMENT.md`.

## The field is structurally blocked by the absence of a biomarker

Across talks spanning from 2010 to a 2026 update, the same obstacle recurs: there is no blood test,
imaging finding, or other objective measure that can diagnose Dupuytren's before it is visible, or
predict who will progress quickly, recur after treatment, or respond to a given intervention.
Diagnosis and staging both currently depend on looking at (or feeling) the hand — a purely
mechanical measure of damage already done. Multiple speakers frame this not as a minor gap but as
*the* reason the field is stuck managing damage instead of preventing it, and describe active work
(a preliminary biomarker panel, blood-based biology research) aimed specifically at closing it.

## Mechanical severity and biological severity are different axes

Whether a finger *can* be straightened (a mechanical, in-the-moment question) is separate from
whether it *stays* straight (a biological question about ongoing disease activity). A joint can be
fully correctable intraoperatively and still lose most of that correction within months if the
underlying disease is highly active; conversely, a severe-looking joint in an indolent phase may
hold a correction well. Several speakers argue this distinction — not treatment technique — is the
real explanation for why outcomes vary so much between patients treated identically.

## Recontracture and true recurrence are often conflated but are not the same thing

"Recontracture" is mechanical — the correction achieved at treatment is lost over time, whether or
not new disease tissue has formed. "True recurrence" is biological — new disease activity appears
where none was present after treatment. Studies that don't separate the two produce numbers that
look comparable but aren't measuring the same thing, which is one of several reasons recurrence
rates are so difficult to compare across the literature (see `05_RECURRENCE_AND_OUTCOME_MEASUREMENT.md`).

## The disease is not simply progressive

More than one long-term prospective study finds that a majority of patients do not follow a steady,
one-directional worsening course — some plateau, some regress, and some genuinely fluctuate over
time in ways not explained by measurement error. This complicates any claim that a given treatment
"worked" based on a single before/after comparison, since some of what looks like treatment benefit
could reflect the disease's own natural fluctuation.

## Counterintuitive findings are reported straight, not smoothed into the expected narrative

A recurring, notable pattern in this collection is scientific honesty about results that cut against
the field's own assumptions: a family-history/early-onset finding that predicts *slower*, not
faster, progression; a surgeon reporting an indication bias that weakened her own headline finding;
an argument that a truly valid randomized trial of certain techniques may not be constructible at
all; and a satisfaction study whose strongest predictor was a pre-treatment appearance score, not
any factor the surgical team could control. None of these are papered over with a tidier-sounding
explanation in the source material, and this collection preserves that same directness.

## Skin grafting specifically protects against true recurrence, but not against disease extension

Several independent case series report that a full-thickness skin graft, placed over the site of
surgery, is followed by near-zero *true* recurrence at that specific site over long follow-up — a
striking and consistent finding across different surgeons and techniques (open-palm technique with
bioengineered graft, dermofasciectomy with autologous graft). This does not mean the disease is
cured: it can still appear at other, ungrafted sites in the same hand. The mechanism most often
proposed involves the graft's skin lacking the hair follicles implicated in normal palm/sole skin's
myofibroblast biology (see `02_CELL_AND_MOLECULAR_BIOLOGY.md`).

## The single dominant molecular actor is the myofibroblast, but the trigger for its behavior is still unsettled

Across the basic-science talks, the myofibroblast — not the fibroblast in general — is treated as
the cell responsible for both the contractile force and (via matrix-mediated signaling) the spread
of disease to adjacent tissue. What causes fibroblasts to convert to myofibroblasts, and what
sustains that conversion, is approached from many different angles (TGF-beta, Wnt/beta-catenin,
mechanical matrix stiffness, TNF, hair-follicle-derived signals) without full consensus — see
`02_CELL_AND_MOLECULAR_BIOLOGY.md` for how these threads relate to each other.

## Practice varies enormously by geography, institution, and individual conviction — more than the evidence would predict

An eight-fold variation in surgical rate between hospitals in the same country, and an international
survey finding practice patterns that track national and individual habit more than data, both
appear in this collection. More than one speaker treats this variation itself as a research finding
worth explaining, not just background noise — evidence that Dupuytren's treatment choice is, in
practice, driven as much by belief and institutional culture as by trial data.

## Lack of awareness and funding, not lack of scientific effort, is repeatedly named as the field's limiting factor

Multiple speakers — across a 2010 introduction, a 2017 awareness campaign, and later biomarker
updates — independently frame Dupuytren's under-recognition and resulting lack of dedicated
research funding, not any shortage of good ideas, as the primary obstacle standing between the
current damage-control model and a genuinely disease-modifying one.

# 02 — Cell and Molecular Biology

The basic-science talks in this collection converge on the myofibroblast as the central actor, but
approach it from several different angles: what turns a fibroblast into a myofibroblast, what keeps
the matrix imbalanced toward excess collagen, how disease spreads to tissue that was never directly
involved, and what genetics can and can't explain.

---

## The myofibroblast is the contractile engine, and its contraction is passive, not active

Contraction in Dupuytren's tissue is argued to work differently than contraction in a healing
wound. Active contraction (the wound-healing model) pulls a straight structure into a bent one.
Passive contraction — the model argued for Dupuytren's — is the resting posture of the hand,
combined with a myofibroblast's weaker, periodic (calcium-regulated) contraction rather than
isometric (Rho-kinase-pathway) contraction, locking in slack that already exists in the tissue.
This is offered as an explanation for why contracture is site-specific to the palm and sole despite
similar histology showing up at other sites (e.g., dorsal knuckle pads) that don't contract the
same way — tissue laxity, not the myofibroblast's biology alone, determines where contracture
actually develops.

## The matrix is an active participant in disease spread, not a passive scaffold

A 3D migration assay comparing Dupuytren's disease cells to matched normal fascia cells from the
same patient found the cells themselves are not more migratory. Instead, a conditioned-collagen
experiment showed that genetically normal fascia cells exposed to factors secreted by disease cells
began expressing disease markers themselves. This reframes disease spread as chemical conversion of
adjacent normal tissue via the extracellular matrix, rather than physical migration of diseased
cells along the fascia — with beta-catenin and periostin proposed as relevant matricellular/
signaling markers in that conversion process.

## Wnt/beta-catenin signaling is a recurring convergence point across independent findings

Several distinct research threads converge on canonical Wnt/beta-catenin signaling: the
matrix-conversion work above implicates beta-catenin directly; separately, TNF is found to inhibit
GSK-3 beta specifically in Dupuytren's palmar fibroblasts, which preserves beta-catenin levels and
links TNF signaling to the same Wnt pathway. This is offered as a candidate explanation for why
prior anti-TGF-beta-only fibrosis trials underperformed — TGF-beta may not be the only signaling
axis worth targeting.

## TGF-beta remains a central and independently-supported driver

Separately from the Wnt/TNF work above, TGF-beta is implicated as the radiosensitive driver of
myofibroblast proliferation that gives radiotherapy its mechanism of action (see
`04_TREATMENT_MODALITIES.md`) — radiation is proposed to work via a post-mitotic shortcut,
effectively short-circuiting the TGF-beta-driven proliferative signal before it can produce more
myofibroblasts.

## TNF has a cell-type-specific effect unique to Dupuytren's palmar fibroblasts

Dupuytren's palmar fibroblasts, cultured fresh (not as passaged cell lines, which one speaker
argues is a more informative preparation), become more contractile when exposed to TNF at the same
concentration they themselves secrete — an effect not seen in normal or non-palmar fibroblasts from
the same patients. Anti-TNF exposure reverses the contractility increase and downregulates the
myofibroblast phenotype, work that has since progressed to a funded clinical trial injecting
anti-TNF directly into early-disease nodules. Macrophages are also found alongside myofibroblasts
in disaggregated nodules, suggesting an inflammatory component to early disease that a purely
fibrotic model would miss.

## The matrix metalloproteinase/TIMP balance is tipped toward under-degradation, not just over-production

Dupuytren's tissue doesn't only make more collagen — it also breaks down less of it. The normal
balance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs, which degrade collagen) and their tissue
inhibitors (TIMPs) is shifted toward inhibition in diseased tissue. One speaker's own data
identifies MMP-14 specifically as a candidate "resistant" collagenase that evades TIMP-1 control
and activates MMP-2, potentially explaining part of why degradation can't keep pace with
production — notably, this same speaker also reports a genuine paradox between clinical and
laboratory evidence on MMP inhibitors, which is preserved here rather than resolved, since the
source itself does not resolve it.

## Genetics explain heritability but only partially

Heritability estimates for Dupuytren's run high in twin and family studies, but a genome-wide
association study identifies specific genetic variants accounting for only a fraction of that
estimated heritability — meaning most of the heritable risk is not yet attributable to any known
gene. This "missing heritability" gap is presented as unresolved, not explained away by any
single mechanism in this collection.

## Fascial changes measurably precede any visible or palpable disease

Cadaver and mechanical stress-strain testing on apparently normal palmar fascia finds detectable
viscoelastic differences (altered creep and recovery time) before any nodule or cord is visible —
evidence the disease process begins earlier than it can currently be clinically detected. This
converges with the biomarker-absence problem in `03_CLINICAL_COURSE_AND_DIAGNOSIS.md`: if disease
onset precedes any way of detecting it, damage-control treatment will always be starting later than
the biology already has.

## Hair-follicle biology is a newer candidate explanation for why skin grafting protects against recurrence

A more recent finding proposes that hair follicles — present in normal skin grafted from elsewhere
on the body, but absent from palm, sole, and knuckle-pad skin — may secrete a bone-morphogenic-
protein-mediated signal that converts myofibroblasts toward a fat-cell fate rather than allowing
them to persist and drive renewed contracture. This is offered as a candidate mechanism for the
skin-grafting protective effect described in `01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md`, not a settled explanation.

## Novel compounds are being designed to hit myofibroblasts specifically, sparing other cell types

At least one experimental approach — a cyclic peptide linking interferon-gamma to a PDGF-receptor-
beta-targeting sequence — is designed explicitly to activate an anti-fibrotic signal only on
myofibroblasts (which express PDGF receptor beta at higher levels than alpha-smooth-muscle actin
alone would predict) while avoiding an unwanted broader immune effect on macrophages. Tested in a
purpose-built 21-day ex vivo tissue model built directly from excised Dupuytren's tissue, it reduced
fibrotic load comparably to pirfenidone, the field's existing anti-fibrotic reference compound. This
targeted-delivery logic — hit the myofibroblast specifically, not fibrosis in general — recurs as a
design principle across more than one experimental therapy in this collection.

# 03 — Clinical Course and Diagnosis

How Dupuytren's actually behaves over time and across populations, how it's currently staged and
diagnosed, and the related/associated conditions that appear to share its underlying biology.

---

## Natural history is not simply progressive

A 150-patient prospective study with clinical exams every six months over two years used latent
class modeling to identify up to six distinct disease-course clusters — not just "progressing,"
"stable," or "regressing," but genuine fluctuation over time in a majority of patients. Up to 75%
showed a disease course other than steady progression, and no association was found to any
well-known risk factor. This matters directly for evaluating early-phase treatments like
radiotherapy or splinting: a wide interquartile range around an unchanging median can hide large
individual variation, and some apparent treatment response could reflect the disease's own natural
fluctuation rather than the intervention.

## A counterintuitive progression-rate finding challenges the "aggressive disease" model

Analysis of two large patient databases found that patients with a family history of Dupuytren's
and early disease onset — the textbook predictors of supposedly more "aggressive" disease — actually
showed a *slower* measured rate of contracture progression (degrees per year) than patients without
those risk factors. This is used to argue Dupuytren's is best understood as a pathologically slowed,
incomplete version of normal wound healing (stuck at inflammation, proliferation, collagen
deposition, or contracture) rather than an accelerated destructive process — with "diathesis" (a
soft, unformalized bundle of risk factors including family history, early onset, ectopic disease,
and bilateral involvement) used across this collection as a marker of recurrence risk despite not
predicting progression rate the way that framing might suggest.

## Diagnosis and staging are still purely mechanical, not biological

Every staging system in this collection (Tubiana staging with specific degree ranges attached to
each stage; a simpler three-stage severity classification in one epidemiological study) measures
what can be seen or felt — nodules, cords, and finger angle. None measures underlying disease
activity directly. One 2026 source is explicit that finger-angle measurement can only show damage
already done, not predict it, and frames a preliminary biomarker panel (see `01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md`)
as the mechanism that could eventually shift the field from diagnostic to predictive testing — the
same rheumatoid-arthritis/anti-TNF precedent is invoked as a model for how a blood biomarker could
enable prevention rather than damage control.

## Prevalence is far higher, and far less exclusively Nordic, than commonly assumed

A street-survey epidemiological study in Bosnia found Dupuytren's is genuinely common there — not
the near-exclusively-Nordic disease it is often assumed to be — with a strong, quantified
association to diabetes, and an ethnic/religious prevalence difference the source itself treats as
possibly, not definitely, real. A separate street-survey study in Flanders independently replicates
similar prevalence numbers. Both studies make the same point from different populations: the gap
between "has the disease" and "needs or wants treatment" is much larger than symposium discussion
usually acknowledges, and prevalence numbers alone overstate the population actually seeking care.

## Surgical rates vary enormously by institution, independent of disease prevalence

Analysis of English national hospital data covering 34,000 Dupuytren's contracture release
procedures over two years found an eight-fold variation in surgical rate between hospitals, with
larger units operating less than expected relative to population served, and smaller units
operating considerably more. This pattern is attributed to practice preference rather than
evidence or geography, and pairs with the international practice-variation survey described in
`01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md` — together, these two sources suggest treatment rate is currently driven
more by institutional and individual habit than by a shared, evidence-based threshold for
intervention.

## Ledderhose disease and Peyronie's disease are treated as manifestations of the same underlying biology

Both conditions appear repeatedly in this collection as part of Dupuytren's diathesis rather than
as unrelated coincidences. In a series of over 400 Peyronie's disease patients, 22% also had
Dupuytren's disease. A joint patient survey covering both Dupuytren's and Ledderhose found a
striking gender skew specific to Ledderhose — women outnumbering men, the reverse of Dupuytren's
usual male predominance in this collection's sources — alongside patients rating radiotherapy far
above every other Ledderhose treatment option (roughly 60% satisfied versus under 15% dissatisfied).
The source itself flags that this gender skew could reflect survey sampling bias rather than a
genuine prevalence difference, a distinction preserved here rather than resolved.

## A structural comparison to burn-scar contracture is offered as deliberately speculative

One source explicitly proposes treating burn scar contracture and Dupuytren's contracture as more
alike than different, floating burn-scar treatment modalities (serial casting, pressure garments,
negative-pressure wound therapy, dermal substitutes, continuous passive motion, mechanical
stretching devices) as candidates for cross-application, and drawing a historical connection through
Dupuytren's own 1832 burn-severity classification. The source is explicit that this comparison is
speculative and raises open questions (including whether Dupuytren's serves any evolutionary or
physiological purpose) rather than answering them — preserved here as a hypothesis-generating
source, not a validated finding.

## Diagnostic delay is patient-reported and substantial

At least one source reports that patients with a very initial sign of Dupuytren's disease delay
seeking care by an average of 2.5 years — meaning by the time a patient is seen clinically, the
disease is typically already considerably older than its visible presentation suggests. This
compounds the biomarker-absence problem: current diagnosis depends on a patient noticing and acting
on an early sign that many people apparently overlook or postpone addressing for years.

## From 04_TREATMENT_MODALITIES.md

# 04 — Treatment Modalities

This collection covers five major intervention categories, plus several adjunct and experimental
approaches. None is presented in this collection as curing the underlying disease — see
`01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md` — and every comparison between them is complicated by the recurrence
measurement problems covered in `05_RECURRENCE_AND_OUTCOME_MEASUREMENT.md`.

---

## Percutaneous needle fasciotomy (PNF)

PNF divides the diseased cord through the skin with a needle, without an open incision. Proponents
in this collection emphasize low cost, fast recovery, and repeatability — retreatment after
recurrence is reported as roughly as effective as the first treatment. One 43-patient (58-ray)
retrospective series reports a mean 39.4-degree improvement, a 10% recurrence rate at 11 months,
and zero nerve or vessel injuries, addressing the two specific safety concerns (nerve injury and
recurrence) the study set out to test. A separate argument for the technique holds that open
surgery's own biology — skin dissection and flap elevation — provokes prolonged post-operative
inflammation that needle fasciotomy avoids entirely, with a stated safety principle: if the
fingertip stays sensate and active flexion doesn't catch the needle tip, no harm has been done.
Drawing on experience across over 8,000 hands, one source explicitly connects patients' preference
for needle release over surgery to prospect theory — patients weighing a modest, familiar risk
against a modest gain differently than a formal risk-benefit calculation might predict. A formal
debate source argues that once recurrence is measured using the same definition as the collagenase
literature, PNF and collagenase produce strikingly similar recurrence rates at both the MCP and PIP
joint — directly undercutting the idea that one is clearly more durable than the other.

## Collagenase injection (collagenase clostridium histolyticum, brand name Xiaflex)

Collagenase dissolves the cord enzymatically via injection, avoiding both needle mechanical
division and open incision. A debate source argues its biggest practical advantage isn't outcome
data but ease of learning and use — "there's no learning curve to it," in contrast to needle
fasciotomy's years-long learning curve — while candidly admitting uncertainty about whether it
produces less recurrence than needle fasciotomy. A separate observation flags a genuinely
surprising pharmacodynamic property: the drug's clinical effect appears to outlast its own short
pharmacological half-life by far, an open question this collection does not resolve. One proposed
explanation for collagenase-specific recurrence is that it removes collagen but not the cells that
produced it, leaving the myofibroblast population intact and able to rebuild the matrix. A
propensity-score-matched real-world comparison against limited fasciectomy found similar MP-joint
correction between the two, fasciectomy favored at the PIP joint, but collagenase patients reporting
significantly larger functional and satisfaction gains — and every serious adverse event in that
study occurred in the surgical group, none in the collagenase group. Repeat collagenase treatment
for recurrence is reported as viable regardless of what treatment (surgical or collagenase itself)
preceded it, provided technique is careful and patient expectations are managed; double dosing
(splitting anesthesia and injection across two cords in one visit) is also described as a practical
technique refinement. Collagenase has also been tested outside Dupuytren's: a 50-patient phase 2
dose-ranging trial repurposed it for adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder) — chosen specifically for
its high patient overlap with Dupuytren's — at the same clinical dose, producing substantially
larger range-of-motion, function, and pain gains than exercise alone, framed as one example of
collagenase's broader potential across fibro-proliferative disorders. Separately, collagenase's
results in Peyronie's disease are reported as more modest than in Dupuytren's, with regulatory
approval considerably more recent.

## Open fasciectomy and its variants (limited fasciectomy, dermofasciectomy, palmar/digital fasciotomy)

Open surgical excision of diseased tissue is presented across this collection as more effective at
achieving intraoperative correction — particularly at the PIP joint, where minimally invasive
techniques struggle more — but carrying a higher risk of rare serious complications, longer
recovery, and (per the propensity-matched comparison above) a higher share of serious adverse
events than nonsurgical alternatives. Severity-specific flap algorithms (Z-plasty for PIP
contracture under 30 degrees; transposition flaps with skin grafting above 30 degrees) are used to
manage skin closure, with a five-year, 50-patient series reporting that contractures at or above 90
degrees could not be fully corrected even intraoperatively despite arthrolysis. Dermofasciectomy,
which removes overlying skin along with diseased tissue and replaces it with a graft, is described
in one source as the current gold-standard *biology-modifying* procedure — distinguished from
procedures that only address anatomy — precisely because of the skin-grafting recurrence-protection
effect covered in `01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md` and `02_CELL_AND_MOLECULAR_BIOLOGY.md`. Barrier
procedures (an absorbable sheet or fascial flap placed within the surgical field) are raised as an
emerging alternative, though one source explicitly voices skepticism about whether they actually
change disease biology or only mechanically delay recontracture — a cellulose "fire break" implant
placed within a segmental fasciectomy incision is reported, in a randomized trial of deliberately
high-recurrence-risk patients, to measurably reduce the 12-week rebound that otherwise erodes
minimally invasive surgery's results.

## Skin grafting as an adjunct (autologous, bioengineered/Apligraf)

Covering an open surgical wound with a skin graft rather than leaving it open (the McCash
open-palm technique) or closing it directly is reported to reduce true recurrence dramatically at
the grafted site specifically. A bioengineered (tissue-engineered) skin graft, tried as an
alternative to autologous skin with a prior off-label precedent in epidermolysis bullosa hand
surgery, showed zero recurrences in a small, long-followed subgroup and reduced post-operative
donor-site pain — but counterintuitively also healed more slowly (about 40 days versus 27 days for
uncovered wounds) than simply leaving the wound open. The study's own framing describes itself
candidly as "a focus group, not a trial," a hedge preserved here rather than smoothed over.

## Radiotherapy

Used specifically for early, progressive disease rather than as a competitor to surgery, radiotherapy
(typically delivered as 10x3Gy or 7x3Gy schedules) is argued to work by exploiting TGF-beta-driven
myofibroblast proliferation's radiosensitivity via a post-mitotic mechanism — see
`02_CELL_AND_MOLECULAR_BIOLOGY.md`. A 612-patient controlled trial with five-year follow-up found a
statistically significant reduction in later surgery need (8% and 14% across two dosing schedules
versus a considerably larger share of the control group). The source is explicit that radiotherapy
is a delaying treatment for a specific disease-stage window, not a general-purpose alternative to
surgery, and this framing is echoed in the Ledderhose satisfaction data in `03_CLINICAL_COURSE_AND_DIAGNOSIS.md`,
where patients rated radiotherapy far above other Ledderhose treatment options.

## Splinting

Splinting evidence in this collection is mixed and explicitly debated rather than settled — a
formal pro/con debate structure appears directly in the symposium sources. The most detailed
practical guidance in this collection (a 2023 full lecture) gives specific fit guidance and names a
particular over-tightening failure mode that trains patients to abandon splinting entirely,
implying that much of splinting's disappointing real-world track record may reflect technique and
adherence problems rather than the intervention itself being ineffective.

## Symptomatic and experimental nodule-directed treatments

A comprehensive 2023 review lists a wide range of options aimed at symptomatic nodules specifically
(distinct from contracture-directed treatment): cortisone injection, off-label colchicine,
intranodular Humira injection, radiotherapy for nodules, topical Voltaren and topical verapamil, and
oral supplements including acetylcysteine, sulforaphane, magnesium, and caffeine. The source draws a
sharp distinction between symptomatic nodule treatment (where measurable benefit is possible) and
anxiety-driven treatment of early disease (where benefit is not measurable), and frames debate over
which surgical *procedure* is superior as missing the field's actual problem, via a "broom on the
beach" analogy. Digit widget splinting, PIP fusion, and amputation are named as salvage options for
severe, otherwise unmanageable cases. On the experimental end, `02_CELL_AND_MOLECULAR_BIOLOGY.md`
covers targeted anti-TNF and myofibroblast-specific interferon-gamma peptide approaches still in
preclinical or early clinical stages.

## Head-to-head evidence synthesis

A GRADE-approach systematic review and meta-analysis comparing percutaneous needle fasciotomy,
collagenase, and limited fasciectomy head-to-head concludes that no treatment wins outright: all
three reduce contracture by a similar amount in the long term, but limited fasciectomy holds that
reduction better, at the cost of a higher risk of rare serious complications — closely matching the
pattern described qualitatively across the individual talks above. The review states its own
motivation directly: prior meta-analyses were limited by suboptimal data-synthesis methodology,
underscoring how much the recurrence-definition problem in `05_RECURRENCE_AND_OUTCOME_MEASUREMENT.md`
has hampered even the field's own attempts to compare treatments rigorously.

## From 05_RECURRENCE_AND_OUTCOME_MEASUREMENT.md

# 05 — Recurrence and Outcome Measurement

Recurrence sounds like a simple yes/no question. Across this collection, it turns out to be one of
the field's hardest measurement problems — and a major reason treatment comparisons in
`04_TREATMENT_MODALITIES.md` are so difficult to make cleanly.

---

## The same dataset can be made to show almost any recurrence rate, depending on definition

A literature review found more than forty different published definitions of "recurrence." Applying
different definitions to a single dataset produced reported recurrence rates ranging from 2% to
86%. This finding directly motivated a formal international Delphi consensus process aimed at
producing one standardized definition — a process that deliberately excluded nodules and cords from
the definition and debated whether a six-week or perioperative baseline should be used for
comparison. The scale of this discrepancy is worth holding onto whenever a specific recurrence
percentage is quoted anywhere else in this collection: the number is only meaningful alongside the
definition that produced it.

## Recontracture and true recurrence are conflated in much of the literature

As covered in `01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md`, "recontracture" (mechanical loss of correction) and "true
recurrence" (new disease activity) are mechanistically different phenomena that many studies do not
distinguish. Multiple speakers argue this conflation is itself a major contributor to the
definitional chaos described above — a study measuring only finger angle at follow-up cannot tell
which of the two it is actually detecting.

## Straighter is not automatically better

More than one source argues directly against treating "degrees of correction" as a complete outcome
measure. A literature review of recurrence rates across surgery, needle, and enzyme treatment argues
that a straighter finger is not automatically a better outcome, framing "straight" and "functional"
as competing rather than identical definitions of surgical success. This connects directly to
`06_PATIENT_EXPERIENCE_AND_PROMS.md`: patients often prioritize a hand that works well over one that
is maximally straight, and recurrence framed purely mechanically can miss what patients actually
care about.

## Recurrence reflects persistent disease, not treatment failure

Across sources discussing repeat treatment, the consistent framing is that recurrence after any
treatment (surgical, needle, or enzymatic) reflects the underlying disease continuing to be active —
not a mistake made during the original treatment. This framing supports repeat treatment (including
repeat collagenase after a prior collagenase treatment) being offered without it being treated as a
correction of an earlier error.

## Skin grafting is the one intervention in this collection that appears to change true recurrence specifically

As detailed in `01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md` and `04_TREATMENT_MODALITIES.md`, multiple independent
surgical series report near-zero true recurrence specifically at sites covered with a skin graft,
distinguishing it from every other intervention in this collection, none of which is reported to
meaningfully reduce true recurrence rather than merely delaying or reducing recontracture.

## Rigorous randomized comparison may not be achievable for some technique comparisons

At least one source argues directly that a genuinely valid randomized controlled trial comparing
certain techniques (in context: percutaneous needle fasciotomy) cannot actually be designed, given
practical and ethical constraints on blinding and technique standardization in hand surgery. This is
preserved here as a stated position, not a resolved methodological consensus — but it helps explain
why propensity-score matching (used in at least one comparison in `04_TREATMENT_MODALITIES.md`)
appears in this collection as a stated alternative when randomization is considered impractical.

# 06 — Patient Experience and Outcome Measurement (PROMs)

What patients report wanting, how that diverges from what a surgeon can directly measure, and the
tools built (with acknowledged limits) to capture the patient's own view of an outcome.

---

## Patients want information first, then a functional hand — not cosmetic perfection

Across many independently interviewed surgeons, the same two-part answer recurs about what patients
actually want: information about the condition, and a genuinely functional hand rather than
cosmetic perfection. One surgeon's framing, echoed by others: patients "are really not never looking
for perfection — they just want a hand that works better." PIP joint contracture is reported to
bother patients more than MCP contracture despite MCP often being more visible, and patients are
often surprised to learn the condition is lifelong rather than a one-time fix — connecting directly
to the "manages the deformity, not the disease" framing in `01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md`.

## A full straightening is not always the right trade-off

Directly connecting to `05_RECURRENCE_AND_OUTCOME_MEASUREMENT.md`'s "straighter is not automatically
better" theme, more than one source notes that full straightening can come at the cost of flexion
(grip strength or the ability to close the hand), and that this trade-off matters more to patients
than an angle measurement alone would suggest. Pain is generally reported as an atypical feature of
Dupuytren's itself — several sources note the condition is typically painless, and that pain, when
present, usually signals something else worth investigating rather than the expected disease course.
Some patients present for care motivated by fear of cancer rather than functional impairment,
underscoring how much patient understanding of the condition (see the diagnostic-delay point in
`03_CLINICAL_COURSE_AND_DIAGNOSIS.md`) shapes when and why care is sought.

## Preoperative appearance, not any factor the surgical team controls, is the strongest predictor of satisfaction

A 194-patient prospective study using the Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire (MHQ) found the
single significant independent predictor of postoperative satisfaction after limited fasciectomy
was the patient's *preoperative* hand-appearance subscore, not any demographic or clinical factor
typically assumed to matter. A one-point higher preoperative appearance score corresponded to a 1.4
times higher odds of satisfaction. Gender approached but did not reach statistical significance as
an additional factor, and a separate study reports a striking gender gap in satisfaction that its
own source leaves unresolved. Preserved here as a genuinely open, unexplained variance rather than
smoothed into a tidier story: satisfaction has real predictors this collection's sources can name,
and a substantial amount that they candidly cannot.

## Patient-rated outcome measures are now required, but the field lacks its own key benchmark number

One source states plainly that "you cannot just go with objective measures anymore" in clinical
research — a PROM is now a required ingredient, not an optional add-on. But the same source spends
most of its argument on why PROM data is less trustworthy than a clean p-value makes it look,
citing specific failure modes that can corrupt PROM data even when it appears statistically clean.
Central to this argument is the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) — the smallest
change in a PROM score that actually matters to a patient — which most fields, including this one,
have not yet formally established. Several competing hand-outcome instruments are named across this
collection (the Michigan Hand Outcomes Questionnaire, URAM, PEM, and DASH), reflecting a field still
without one agreed-upon measurement tool. Drawing on a 12,000-patient, roughly 70,000-form dataset,
the same source reports an unflattering result about its own author's outcomes rather than omitting
it — stated directly: "That is not clean science yet. We are on a journey, and we haven't even
established the measuring tool."

## Funnel-plot analysis surfaces performance variation, separate from PROMs

Funnel-plot analysis with control limits is used in this collection both to visualize inter-hospital
surgical-rate variation (see `03_CLINICAL_COURSE_AND_DIAGNOSIS.md`) and, separately, as a tool for
individual surgeon-performance monitoring — a use distinct from, but complementary to, PROM-based
outcome tracking.

## Patient-generated sources (forum and comment threads) show a pattern of mixed outcomes and unverified self-treatment

Two patient-voice sources in this collection — a Reddit thread and a YouTube comment thread under an
educational video — are explicitly paraphrased summaries rather than verbatim transcripts, and are
read accordingly as anecdote rather than evidence. Across both, self-reported treatment outcomes
skew negative or mixed, and both threads contain a cluster of unverified self-treatment remedies
(boron, hormone replacement therapy, manual stretching, a fasting/supplement combination, ice water)
that individual commenters reported trying, several of which drew explicit pushback from other
commenters in the same thread. Family clustering and Northern European ancestry are self-reported
recurring themes, alongside disagreement among commenters about whether alcohol use or hand-use
patterns worsen the condition — disagreement this collection does not resolve, since no controlled
source in this collection specifically tests either claim.
