# 00 — README

This package synthesizes {videos} sources on character, relationships, and everyday life advice —
drawn from two very different kinds of channel, which this synthesis treats as distinct rather than
blending into one voice.

## What this collection actually is

**Two clusters with very different evidentiary footing sit side by side in this library:**

1. **Named-researcher content** (a minority of notes) — relationship science from John and Julie
   Gottman, attachment and dating philosophy from Alain de Botton, developmental psychology from
   Lisa Damour and Becky Kennedy, moral/social psychology from Paul Bloom, trauma research from
   Rachel Yehuda, friendship research from Robert Waldinger, and communication frameworks from
   Charles Duhigg and Vanessa Van Edwards. These cite real research, name real studies, and are
   attributable to specific credentialed people.
2. **"Shi Heng Yi Wisdom" / Inspire Forward content** (the majority of notes by count) — third-
   person motivational narration nominally attributed to a Shaolin monk, covering solitude,
   boundaries, character, composure, and self-development. This cluster is, by its own individual
   notes' documented findings, thinly and inconsistently sourced: the name behind it is spelled at
   least three different ways across notes ("Shi Heng Yi," "Shiheni," "Shihangi"), at least two
   pairs of videos share duplicate on-screen titles with unrelated content, at least two videos
   share near-verbatim scripted templates with the topic swapped, and several videos borrow
   neuroscience or physics vocabulary ("quantum field," "heart-brain coherence," "the collective
   unconscious") to back claims that have no named study behind them.

This distinction matters for how confidently anything in this library should be relayed — see
`06_OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` for the full accounting, and `05_CROSS_SOURCE_COMPARISON.md` for where the
two clusters converge on similar themes despite the gap in evidentiary rigor.

## How this package is organized

- **01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md** — patterns that recur across both clusters, independently arrived at.
- **02_RELATIONSHIPS_AND_ATTACHMENT.md** — partnership dynamics, dating, attachment, and how to
  handle someone returning after rejection or estrangement.
- **03_CHARACTER_TRUST_AND_EMOTIONAL_REGULATION.md** — how character and trustworthiness are
  revealed, boundary-setting, composure under provocation, and reading/being read by other people.
- **04_SELF_DEVELOPMENT_AND_GROWTH_CLAIMS.md** — productivity, self-talk, inner-work practices, and
  a set of more idiosyncratic claims (birth-month psychology, a vitamin-D muscle claim) that warrant
  more scrutiny than this file's other content.
- **05_CROSS_SOURCE_COMPARISON.md** — where the two clusters converge, and documented cases of
  template reuse or duplicate content within the Shi Heng Yi Wisdom cluster.
- **06_OPEN_QUESTIONS.md** — the specific sourcing weaknesses, unverifiable claims, and internal
  inconsistencies this library's notes have already flagged.
- **07_PLAIN_ENGLISH_GLOSSARY.md** — every named term, alphabetically, no citations.
- **08_QUESTIONS_AND_ANSWERS.md** — worked example answers (not yet populated).
- **09_SOURCE_CATALOG.md** — every source, auto-generated.

Per this project's provenance rule, files 01–08 carry no video-ID citations — only the individual
notes in `notes/` carry those.

## From 01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md

# 01 — Core Principles

Six patterns recur across this collection's notes — often across its two very differently-sourced
clusters independently — often enough to treat as load-bearing. Read these first.

---

## 1. What's revealed in unwitnessed, low-stakes moments outweighs what's performed in visible ones

A recurring claim across many notes: a person's real character, a relationship's real health, and a
person's real growth all show up more reliably in small, private, or easily-dismissed moments than
in dramatic or observed ones. How someone treats a person who "can't reciprocate," whether a small
promise gets kept, private discipline nobody applauds, and consistency "even in private, especially
in private" all appear as diagnostic in this sense — the shared logic being that performance is
cheap and consistency under no observation is not.

## 2. Real strength or composure is trained in small reps, not summoned in a crisis

Emotional composure, non-reactivity, and self-possession are consistently described as skills built
through repeated small practice — pausing at a minor daily irritation, treating traffic or a slow
reply as a "training rep" — rather than as a trait a person either has or lacks under real pressure.
This mirrors a similar claim in this project's `addiction` library about habit change, arrived at
here from a relationship- and character-focused angle instead of a behavioral-science one.

## 3. Genuine connection requires selectivity, not availability

Across both clusters, openness and access to a person's inner life are treated as something to be
earned through demonstrated trustworthiness, not extended by default. This shows up as boundaries
distinguished explicitly from walls (letting in what aligns with one's values, rather than blocking
everyone), as guarded disclosure of specific vulnerable categories (regrets, finances, pain), and as
relationship research showing that friendship and repeated small positive interaction — not
proximity or infatuation — is what sustains a partnership or a friendship over time.

## 4. A returning or repaired relationship needs demonstrated change, not just an apology or regret

Multiple notes converge on distinguishing a felt emotion (regret, remorse) from a demonstrated
behavior (accountability, follow-through, consistent action) — regret is described as something
that can be entirely self-interested, while trust is described as something that has to be rebuilt
incrementally through observed, sustained action rather than restored by a single gesture.

## 5. Difficulty, isolation, or being alone is reframed as generative rather than purely negative

A strong recurring move across this collection is to reinterpret an apparently negative state —
loneliness, rejection, social isolation, being single, a period of hardship — as the necessary or
even valuable precondition for a stronger, clearer, or more capable version of a person. This
appears in registers ranging from clinical (a psychiatrist's friendship research) to overtly
spiritual (isolation as divine preparation) to folk-motivational (solitude as a "mirror"). The
underlying claim recurs even where the framing and evidentiary basis differ sharply.

## 6. Communication breaks down more from mismatched intent than from disagreement over facts

Several notes independently locate conversational conflict not in what's actually said but in a
mismatch between what kind of conversation each person thinks they're having — practical
problem-solving versus emotional support versus social/identity affirmation — or in a mismatch
between what a person needs (being heard) and what they're offered (advice, solutions, or
correction). This reframes many conflicts as a category error in conversation type, not a
disagreement about content.

## From 02_RELATIONSHIPS_AND_ATTACHMENT.md

# 02 — Relationships and Attachment

Partnership dynamics, dating, attachment, solitude before partnership, and handling someone
returning after rejection or estrangement — drawn from both this library's research-backed cluster
and its motivational-narration cluster, kept distinguishable where it matters.

---

## What actually sustains a partnership (research-backed)

Long-running couples research locates the foundation of a lasting partnership in friendship and
repeated small positive interaction, not in compatibility, romantic spark, or absence of conflict.
A specific finding: roughly two-thirds of relationship conflicts are "perpetual" and never fully
resolve — the distinguishing factor between couples who last and those who don't is described as
the capacity to repair a rupture and manage recurring friction, not the ability to solve it
permanently. A named pattern of four escalating behaviors — criticism, defensiveness, contempt, and
stonewalling — is identified as predictive of relationship failure, with contempt named as the most
corrosive of the four. Deep, attentive listening is described as outperforming a more mechanical
"active listening" formula, which research is stated to have found can perform worse than no
structured intervention at all.

## Attachment, dating, and why love is treated as a skill

A separate, philosophically-grounded thread argues that romantic compatibility and readiness are
learnable rather than instinctive: pessimism about a partner (expecting friction and crisis) is
described as a better ally of lasting love than optimism about finding a flawless match, and a
specific screening question — asking how a prospective partner is "crazy" — is offered as more
useful than surface compatibility-checking. Adult attachment patterns (including avoidant
attachment, read as fear of intimacy) are traced to an unconscious "emotional grammar" acquired in
early childhood from caregivers, with a repetition-compulsion dynamic (attempting to master old
childhood conflicts using adult resources) offered as one explanation for repeated relationship
patterns. A historical argument is layered on top: the romantic-era shift toward instinct-based
partner selection is argued not to have produced more relationship satisfaction than the more
pragmatic, reflection-based partner selection it replaced.

## Solitude and self-completion before partnership

A large, recurring thread — entirely from this library's motivational-narration cluster — argues
that a period of being single, alone, or without close friends is not a deficit but a deliberate or
valuable state: a partner is repeatedly described as something that should "compliment" an already
whole life rather than "complete" a lacking one. This cluster consistently frames the discomfort of
being alone (checking a phone for messages that don't arrive, silence, an empty schedule) as a
"mirror" that reveals rather than a void that harms, and treats standards/boundaries maintained
during this period as a filter for future connection rather than a wall against it. This directly
echoes, and should be read alongside, `03_CHARACTER_TRUST_AND_EMOTIONAL_REGULATION.md`'s coverage
of boundaries and selective disclosure — the same underlying claim (selectivity as strength, not
deficiency) appears in both contexts.

**Confidence note:** this cluster's claims about solitude are delivered as universal, unqualified
assertion, with no named research anywhere. It should not be read as complementing or corroborating
the research-backed attachment/relationship material above — the two simply address adjacent
territory from very different evidentiary standing. See `06_OPEN_QUESTIONS.md`.

## A returning partner or estranged person: regret versus accountability

Multiple notes converge on a specific practical distinction for handling someone who has previously
mistreated or abandoned the viewer and now wants back in: regret ("I'm sorry") is treated as
cheap and potentially self-interested — often, per this content, simply missing the comfort or
convenience the relationship provided — while accountability (consistent, demonstrated behavior
change over time) is treated as the only thing that should actually reopen access. One thread
frames strategic silence and withheld reaction as the mechanism that can induce this regret in the
first place; a separate thread argues the regret itself, however induced, still shouldn't
automatically grant re-admission — the two takes converge on distrust of regret alone but diverge on
whether inducing it is itself a legitimate goal.

## Loneliness, friendship, and its treatment

Loneliness is treated as a real, physiologically damaging state, not merely an unpleasant one — one
line of argument frames it as an evolved drive serving the same signaling function as physical pain.
Its treatment is described as counterintuitive: not directly pursuing closeness or deep connection,
but engaging in repeated, casual, low-stakes contact through a shared activity, which is stated to
build the relationship infrastructure that deeper connection later depends on. A caution appears
about AI companions or substitute connection specifically not filling this role, described in one
source as a hollow substitute for the genuine article.

## Trauma's relationship to the story told about it

A research-backed thread on trauma (distinct from, but relevant to, relationship repair) argues that
the ongoing impact of a traumatic event is driven less by the event itself than by the self-blaming
narrative a person builds around it afterward — and that therapeutic work aimed at revising that
narrative on an emotional (not just intellectual) level is what produces lasting change.

## From 03_CHARACTER_TRUST_AND_EMOTIONAL_REGULATION.md

# 03 — Character, Trust, and Emotional Regulation

How character and trustworthiness are read (in yourself and others), how to set and hold
boundaries, how to stay composed under provocation, and how to communicate well — spanning both of
this library's clusters.

---

## How character actually gets revealed

A specific, recurring diagnostic: character is read from how a person treats someone who "cannot
reciprocate" — holds no power, benefit, or influence over them — and from whether they keep small,
easily-broken promises rather than only large, visible commitments. Consistency, particularly
*inconsistency*, is treated as more diagnostic than any single data point: a person whose warmth or
kindness is unpredictable, or who is respectful publicly but not privately, is treated as more
concerning than someone consistently difficult, on the logic that an open adversary is easier to
plan around than an inconsistent ally. A related diagnostic looks at how a person reacts to someone
else's good news specifically — genuine warmth versus a flicker of visible discomfort is treated as
revealing a hidden competitive or conditional dynamic in what otherwise looks like support.

## Boundaries versus walls, and what to disclose to whom

A consistent distinction across several notes: a wall blocks everyone indiscriminately, while a
boundary (or a "gate with a lock the person controls") lets in selectively based on demonstrated
alignment or trustworthiness. This is applied concretely to a short list of disclosure categories
argued to warrant more caution than typical conversation — deep regrets, one's full financial
picture, felt pain, future plans, and long-held resentments — on the stated logic that these are
specifically vulnerable to being misused, minimized, or weaponized by someone who has not earned the
access. A useful practical tell for whether trust has been earned: noticing whether a person feels
"lighter or heavier" after spending time with them, offered as a simple somatic check on whether a
relationship is functioning as care or as control.

## Composure, non-reaction, and the pause before responding

A large cluster of material treats emotional composure — staying non-reactive under criticism,
provocation, or minor daily friction — as a trainable discipline built through repeated small
practice, not a fixed personality trait. The consistent mechanism offered: notice the moment between
a triggering stimulus and a reaction, and deliberately widen it, using minor daily irritations
(traffic, a slow reply, a passive-aggressive comment) as low-stakes practice for higher-stakes
moments later. Several notes are explicit that this is not the same as suppressing or numbing
feeling — the stated distinction is *owning* an emotional reaction fully enough that it isn't
dictated by someone else's provocation, versus not feeling it at all. Criticism specifically is
treated as something to filter deliberately (asking whether the source has relevant standing or
experience) rather than absorb automatically.

## Reading other people, and being read accurately yourself

A separate research-backed thread addresses the mechanics of first impressions and ongoing social
reading: impressions form within seconds along two axes (perceived warmth and competence), and
specific physical "blocking" behavior (crossed arms, closed posture) is identified as especially
damaging to how quickly someone is trusted. A stated framework for reading other people's cues
accurately emphasizes never interpreting a single signal alone — checking it against context,
cultural norms, and a cluster of other simultaneous signals instead. Active listening itself is
treated as requiring visible, demonstrated cues (leaning in, nodding, a head tilt) rather than
silent internal attention alone, alongside an awareness that a person's own resting facial
expression can be misread as disengagement or hostility by others without any intent behind it.

## Matching the right kind of conversation to what's actually needed

A specific, recurring communication finding: many conversational breakdowns come from a mismatch
between the *kind* of conversation each party thinks is happening — practical (solving a problem),
emotional (offering support), or social/identity-based (affirming belonging or being heard) —
rather than from actual disagreement about the topic at hand. In a conversation someone has entered
for social or identity reasons, offering a solution or a counter-argument is treated as
systematically unhelpful, even when the solution offered is objectively correct, because what's
being sought is acknowledgment, not resolution.

## Emotional development, shame, and identity in parenting

A research-backed thread on parenting and emotional development draws a consistent line between a
child's (or adult's) *behavior* and their *identity or worth*, arguing that shame — treating a
behavior as evidence of who someone fundamentally is — produces short-term compliance at the cost of
long-term self-concept, while separating the two allows correction without that cost. A specific
child temperament ("deeply feeling") is named as needing this distinction applied with particular
care, since ordinary correction can be experienced with disproportionate intensity. Teen-specific
research adds a concrete, counterintuitive finding: teenagers report wanting to be listened to and
given space far more than given advice when upset, with a stated pattern where withdrawal from
parents during adolescence is normal developmental work rather than a relational rupture.

## Moral judgment and social psychology

A separate thread on moral and social psychology examines how people assign blame and judge
character in others: retaliation is consistently perceived as more justified by the person doing it
than by an observer (a documented asymmetry termed "moral typecasting" in this material), and a
broader account of resistance-to-persuasion ("reactance") is treated as only one narrow slice of a
larger human drive toward autonomy that shows up across many "destructive"-looking decisions.

## From 04_SELF_DEVELOPMENT_AND_GROWTH_CLAIMS.md

# 04 — Self-Development and Growth Claims (Variable Rigor)

Productivity systems, self-talk and inner-work practices, and a set of more idiosyncratic claims —
grouped together here because they share a family resemblance (an inner discipline claimed to
produce outer results) despite ranging from well-established to essentially unsupported. Confidence
levels differ sharply within this file — treat each subsection's own confidence note as load-bearing,
not just this file's general framing.

---

## Daily structure and prioritization (moderate confidence)

A cluster of practical productivity claims: planning the next day the night before rather than
reacting to it in the morning; distinguishing a "task" (something to do) from a "priority"
(something that must be done); scheduling priorities directly rather than leaving them as unscheduled
intentions; and applying a delegate/automate/eliminate filter to a task list. The 80/20 (Pareto)
principle is invoked by name to justify concentrating effort on a small number of high-impact
actions. These are standard, widely-circulated productivity heuristics (one unattributed phrase,
"sharpen the saw," closely echoes Stephen Covey's well-known work without naming him) — plausible
and consistent with mainstream productivity writing, but not independently cited to research within
this material itself.

## What separates extraordinary success from ordinary effort (low-to-moderate confidence)

A specific claim about outsized success: what separates it from ordinary effort is not superior
habits or discipline alone, but holding a genuinely contrarian idea, being correct about it, and
having the conviction to act on it before it's validated — with the implication that the visible
surface habits of successful people (their routines, schedules) are downstream of this, not the
cause of their success, and are often the wrong thing for an imitator to copy.

## Self-talk, neuroplasticity, and where the claims overreach (mixed — real mechanism, overstated effect)

A detailed protocol claims that timed, emotionally intense spoken self-talk (in specific morning,
afternoon, and evening windows) can rewire neural pathways, change body chemistry, and alter gene
expression within three days. The underlying mechanism named — neuroplasticity, and the principle
that repeated firing strengthens a neural pathway — is real and established. However, the same
material extends, with identical confident phrasing and no distinction in register, into physics-
adjacent claims with no accepted scientific basis: that thoughts broadcast an "electromagnetic
signature" into a "quantum field," and that the protocol constitutes "quantum entanglement." A
related thread makes a parallel but more restrained version of a similar claim — that focused
attention itself reinforces neural pathways ("neuroplasticity follows attention") — while staying
within nervous-system and behavioral-psychology vocabulary (cortisol, the prefrontal cortex, the
sympathetic nervous system) rather than extending into physics language. **Treat the neuroplasticity
component as a real if simplified mechanism, and the quantum/energy-broadcast component as
unsupported assertion dressed in the same confident register — see `06_OPEN_QUESTIONS.md`.**

## Shadow work, ego versus soul, and manifestation (low confidence)

A separate self-development thread frames unmet desires (for love, money, validation) as symptoms of
an unaddressed unconscious "shadow" rather than as straightforward wants, and recommends turning
inward (a physical grounding gesture, asking why a desire is being chased) rather than continuing to
pursue the desire directly. This is presented in Jungian-adjacent vocabulary (shadow, individuation)
mixed with unhedged claims that "the universe responds to vibration, not intention" and that this
process constitutes "quantum alignment" — the same physics-misappropriation pattern flagged above.

## Birth-month psychology and a vitamin/muscle claim (low confidence, presented as an outlier each)

Two specific claims stand apart from the rest of this collection's content by subject matter and are
worth flagging as outliers rather than corroborated material: a claim that birth month (via
"archetypes" tied to the season of birth) shapes personality traits and life lessons, dressed in the
vocabulary of academic psychology ("the collective unconscious") without citing any actual
psychological source or study; and a claim that vitamin D supplementation, combined with protein
intake and resistance exercise, can measurably reverse age-related muscle loss within two weeks,
repeatedly invoking unnamed "studies" and "research" for specific numeric and mechanistic claims
that are never sourced. Both should be treated as unverified assertions rather than established
findings — see `06_OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` for the full detail on each.

## A contemplative framework for working with difficult mental states (moderately well-attributed, within its own tradition)

Distinct from the above, one source presents a named Buddhist framework — five specific mental
states ("hindrances") that obstruct clear thinking and decision-making — delivered in the first
person by a speaker describing his own monastic training, and explicitly credited to a named
tradition rather than invented or left unsourced. This is the most directly and consistently sourced
self-development content in this collection, though its authority still rests on personal account
and lineage rather than external, independently verifiable citation.

## From 05_CROSS_SOURCE_COMPARISON.md

# 05 — Cross-Source Comparison

Where this collection's two clusters converge on similar claims despite very different evidentiary
standing, and documented cases of template reuse or duplication within the motivational-narration
cluster. Individual notes carry the specific, timestamped version of each comparison below.

---

## The central methodological fact about this library: two clusters, not one voice

Before any topical comparison, the single most important comparison in this library is structural:
roughly half its notes come from named, credentialed sources (relationship researchers, a
psychiatrist, a philosopher, developmental psychologists) making citable, falsifiable claims, and
roughly half come from a single motivational-narration channel whose own notes have independently
documented inconsistent attribution (a central figure's name spelled at least three different ways
within and across videos), duplicate scripts with the topic swapped, duplicate on-screen titles
covering unrelated content, and confident physics/neuroscience-adjacent language with no citation.
Treat these as two separate evidentiary registers throughout, not as one uniformly-sourced library.

## Convergence: selectivity and solitude, from two different registers

The claim that being alone (single, without close friends, without a partner) can be a strength
rather than a deficiency appears in both clusters, but with sharply different grounding: the
research-backed cluster frames friendship-building and deliberate low-stakes social contact as the
actual antidote to damaging loneliness, while the motivational-narration cluster frames prolonged
solitude itself, faced rather than escaped, as revealing an already-latent strength. These are not
strictly contradictory — one is about how to reduce loneliness's harm, the other about what
withstanding it is claimed to build — but they should not be read as corroborating a single unified
claim about solitude.

## Convergence: shame and identity-separation, independently arrived at

The specific idea that a behavior should be corrected without treating it as evidence about a
person's fundamental identity or worth appears independently in the research-backed parenting
material (behavior versus identity) and, in a different form, in the motivational-narration
cluster's framing of self-worth as "non-negotiable" independent of how someone is treated. Different
audiences (parents raising children; adults processing being mistreated), same underlying structural
move.

## Documented duplicate and template content within the motivational-narration cluster

Two specific, fully-documented cases exist within this library (each already flagged in detail in
its own note's "Conflicts with other sources" section, referenced here for visibility): two videos
share extensive near-verbatim opening and closing script language with only the topic swapped (a
returning ex versus prolonged singleness), and two separate pairs of videos share an identical
on-screen title while covering entirely unrelated content (one pair splits into a religious sermon
versus a secular composure video; construct your own picture of which pair from the individual
notes rather than assuming). These are documented facts about this specific channel's production
process, not claims requiring further verification — but they mean that apparent "independent
corroboration" between videos in this cluster should be checked against whether they in fact share a
script before being treated as two separate sources agreeing.

## Convergence: the pause between stimulus and response, across clusters

The concept of a trainable gap between a triggering event and a person's reaction — named
explicitly as "the space between stimulus and response" in one source — recurs across nearly every
composure-related note regardless of cluster, and is functionally the same underlying claim as this
project's `addiction` library makes about nervous-system regulation (noticing a stress reaction
building and deliberately responding rather than reacting), arrived at independently from a
character/self-mastery framing rather than a clinical one.

## A shared weak spot: appeal to unnamed "studies" and "research"

Both clusters, at points, invoke "research shows" or "studies confirm" for specific claims without
naming the study — this happens in the well-attributed cluster too (e.g., a specific procrastination
statistic or a claim about self-affirmation technique effectiveness), not only in the motivational-
narration cluster. The difference in degree is large (the research-backed cluster's speakers are
independently verifiable named experts whose broader work can be checked; the motivational cluster's
narrator is not consistently even a real, findable person), but the specific practice of citing
unnamed research appears on both sides and is worth checking claim-by-claim rather than assumed
reliable purely because a speaker is credentialed elsewhere in the same note.

## From 06_OPEN_QUESTIONS.md

# 06 — Open Questions

Claims in this collection stated without a named source, flagged by their own note as unverifiable,
or otherwise worth treating with real caution. This library has an unusually large amount of
material worth flagging here, concentrated in its motivational-narration cluster (see
`00_README.md` and `05_CROSS_SOURCE_COMPARISON.md`) — the point of this file is to keep the
derived-versus-stated distinction visible, not to discard the content.

---

## Attribution problems specific to the "Shi Heng Yi Wisdom" cluster

- The central named figure behind roughly half this library's notes is rendered inconsistently
  across (and within) individual videos as "Shi Heng Yi," "Shiheni," and "Shihangi" — in at least one
  video, two different spellings appear within the same transcript, a stronger signal of an
  unedited or auto-generated script than of a deliberate citation practice.
- Several videos attribute a short aphorism to this figure by name but never quote or elaborate a
  specific, traceable teaching, text, or date — the name functions as a general credibility marker
  rather than an actual citation in most instances.
- At least one video is narrated in the first person by a speaker introducing himself as this
  figure directly, while every other video in the same cluster references him only in the third
  person — a structural inconsistency worth noting for anyone trying to establish this channel's
  actual voice or authorship.
- One quoted line attributed to this figure ("who looks outside dreams, who looks inside awakens")
  is a widely circulated aphorism attributed in many other contexts to Carl Jung — this collection
  does not resolve which attribution, if either, is correct.
- One video contains an internal transcript duplication (the same ~8 minutes of content repeats
  almost verbatim partway through a video that does not appear to be a compilation), and a separate
  video's transcript becomes textually incoherent for its final several minutes, interleaving
  unrelated nature-documentary content — both flagged as likely caption/production artifacts rather
  than intentional content.

## Claims that borrow scientific vocabulary without scientific support

- A three-day self-talk protocol claims measurable changes to gene expression and a personal
  "quantum field," describing this explicitly as "quantum mechanics in action" — no study,
  researcher, or institution is named anywhere in the source.
- A separate video makes a similar move with "quantum alignment" to describe an emotional/
  psychological shift.
- A birth-month "archetype" framework uses academic-psychology vocabulary ("archetypes," "the
  collective unconscious") to support an unfalsifiable claim structurally similar to astrology, with
  no actual psychological source named.
- A vitamin D and muscle-loss video repeatedly invokes unnamed "studies" and "research" for specific
  claims (a two-week improvement timeline, the existence and function of vitamin D receptors on
  muscle cells) without naming a single study, journal, or researcher.

## Claims stated without a named source in the research-backed cluster too

- A specific claim that self-affirmation techniques (mirror work, visualizations) work better for
  people who already feel good about themselves than for people who don't is attributed to
  unnamed research.
- Specific procrastination statistics in this project's `addiction` library's Chris Bailey notes
  (cross-referenced here since the same speaker also appears in that library) are attributed to an
  unnamed "procrastination researcher."

## Unverifiable personal narratives presented as evidentiary basis

- One video's entire argument rests on an unverifiable claim of personal experience ("I've spoken
  to hundreds of people... one regret echoes louder than all others") followed by five short,
  named illustrative stories with no indication of whether they are real accounts or composites.
- Claims about universal male or female psychology and behavior (what "most men fear," what a
  specific gendered behavior "really means") are made throughout the motivational-narration cluster
  as generalized fact with no survey, study, or named source.

## A genuine, if thin, religious/contemplative source, for contrast

Not every low-citation video in this cluster is equivalent: one video presents a named Buddhist
framework in the first person, explicitly credited to a specific tradition and the speaker's own
described training — a different, more traceable kind of authority (personal account within a named
lineage) than the unattributed psychological or physics-adjacent claims elsewhere in this cluster,
even though it too carries no external, independently verifiable citation.

Worked answers in this package's house style, with citations shown. Added once there are enough
