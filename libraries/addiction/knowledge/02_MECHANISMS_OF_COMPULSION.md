# 02 — Mechanisms of Compulsion

The specific "why" each source in this collection offers for why a behavior persists. Organized by
mechanism, not by source — several sources describe the same underlying phenomenon in different
vocabulary, cross-referenced here.

---

## The dopamine anticipation-of-reward loop

Dopamine is described not as a pleasure signal but as an anticipation-of-reward signal — it drives
seeking and checking behavior rather than delivering satisfaction itself. This is the stated
mechanism behind phone-checking, notification-seeking, and slot-machine design, and is explicitly
described as running on "the same neurochemistry" whether the trigger is a substance, a phone, or a
gambling device. The reward being "juiced" artificially (methamphetamine cited as producing roughly
ten times a natural reward's dopamine response) is the same mechanism, at a different intensity,
across substances and behaviors alike.

## The habit loop (cue, routine, reward)

A separate but compatible mechanism: the basal ganglia automates any behavior that reliably follows
a cue-routine-reward pattern, regardless of whether the behavior benefits the person. This loop
cannot be erased once formed — the neural connection persists indefinitely — which is why change
has to work by substituting a new routine at the same cue, delivering a similar reward, rather than
attempting to simply stop the old behavior. Misidentifying the actual reward being sought (assuming
it's the obvious one, like taste or energy, when it's actually something like social connection) is
named as a common reason habit-change attempts fail.

## Nervous-system familiarity and identity-fused patterns

A third mechanism, from clinical/nervous-system framing rather than neurochemistry: the nervous
system develops a preference for familiar patterns — including dysfunctional ones — because
familiarity itself registers as safety. Patterns adapted in childhood (overachieving, shutting down
in conflict) can become fused with identity, socially rewarded as "drive" or being "easy," while
remaining unhealthy for the person living them. Change, in this framing, works by consciously
noticing a stress reaction building in the body and deliberately responding differently in the
moment, which is described as literally strengthening a different neural pathway over repeated
practice.

## Identity-threat avoidance

A distinct mechanism describes avoidance as driven by whatever threatens a person's certainty about
their own identity — and explicitly, this applies symmetrically to threatening *and* beneficial
things. A good opportunity or a healthy relationship can be sabotaged by the same mechanism that
makes someone avoid a hard conversation, because both threaten an existing self-concept. This
reframes some instances of self-sabotage as identity-protection working exactly as designed, not as
a special kind of self-destructiveness.

## Values misalignment producing involuntary resistance

Two independently-arrived-at frameworks describe resistance to a goal or behavior as a symptom of
misalignment with a person's actual (not stated) values, rather than a motivation problem to be
solved with more willpower. A goal that conflicts with a person's strongest value — for instance,
one coded as pure conformity to someone who values self-direction — is described as provoking a
visceral, involuntary "get this out of my face" reaction, distinct from ordinary difficulty.

## Disevolution: engineered abundance meeting evolved scarcity-cravings

A specific evolutionary-mismatch mechanism: cravings for calorie-dense food, social connection, and
validation evolved under conditions of scarcity, and modern engineered environments (fast food,
social media, pornography) exploit those same cravings under conditions of abundance the evolved
mechanism was never calibrated for. This is offered as an explanation for why willpower alone
struggles against these environments — the craving mechanism is functioning correctly, just against
an environment radically different from the one that shaped it.

## Comfort and optimization as their own trap

A final mechanism, somewhat different in kind from the others: excessive self-optimization and
tracking is described as producing fragility rather than resilience, by substituting a device's
measurement for a person's own embodied judgment. Treating an external score (a "recovery score," a
step count) as more authoritative than lived experience is described as "sucking the feeling" out
of performance and making a person's capability contingent on conditions being externally validated
as correct — the inverse problem from the other mechanisms above, but converging on the same
practical advice: trust and rebuild felt judgment rather than outsourcing it entirely.
