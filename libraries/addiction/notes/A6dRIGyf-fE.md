# The push to self-optimize is hollowing out your happiness | Brad Stulberg

## Central claim

**Modern life presents two opposite traps that both undermine genuine excellence: "disevolution"
(evolved cravings hijacked by engineered abundance — fast food, social media validation,
pornography) and its overcorrection, "optimization culture" (obsessive self-tracking that turns a
person into "a robot" and makes performance more fragile, not less) — with the source arguing real
excellence requires a felt, embodied experience that neither extreme allows.** `[03:06]` "When we
let these devices control us instead of controlling them, and when we build an entire life around
raw optimization, it sucks the feeling out of life, it sucks the humanity out of life, it sucks the
joy out of life, and we struggle to have our best performances."

## Optimization as fragility: the J.J. Spaun story

`[04:07]` Stulberg's central illustration against over-optimization: golfer J.J. Spaun was awake
most of the night before winning the US Open, tending to his vomiting child at a 24-hour drugstore
— **"if J.J. Spaun would have been wearing a recovery device... it would have told him, 'your
recovery score is zero, you didn't sleep, you should stay home.' J.J. Spaun won the US Open the next
day."** The stated lesson: treating optimization metrics as authoritative rather than informative
creates the false belief that "everything needs to be going a certain way... to perform well,"
which the source calls "utter nonsense" given how much of performance science remains unknown.

## Genuine flow versus "shitty flow"

`[05:08]`–`[09:11]` Flow (credited to Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi) is described as values-neutral —
achievable "playing an instrument... coaching a team... [or] scrolling Twitter... gambling." Stulberg
adopts David Pizarro's term **"shitty flow"** for the latter category: immersive states that share
flow's phenomenology (lost sense of time and self) but run counter to a person's actual values. The
stated diagnostic is retrospective, not in-the-moment: **"ask yourself not how you feel during the
experience, but how you feel after... if you feel exhausted and like you were just on a bender, it's
probably shitty flow. If you feel exhausted but fulfilled and satisfied, it's probably the good
kind."** Excellence, in this framing, is a much broader concept than flow — encompassing the
effortful practice before a flow state and the deliberate "coming down" after it, not just flow
itself.

## Zombie burnout: exhaustion from too little engagement, not too much

`[09:11]`–`[13:15]` Stulberg names a second burnout type distinct from conventional overwork
burnout: **"zombie burnout doesn't come from doing too much, it comes from not doing enough of what
lights you up"** — someone working only 30 hours a week can still feel "exhausted... apathetic...
dread" if disengaged and passive. He illustrates with two people both working 40-hour weeks: one
engaged and values-aligned "might absolutely flourish," the other "just totally going through the
motions" and burns out despite identical hours — arguing burnout must be assessed by engagement
quality, not just workload quantity. Countermeasures named: "job crafting" (adjusting a role toward
personal values where possible) and replacing passive leisure with active, effortful leisure.

## The "happiness industrial complex," and the experience-machine thought experiment

`[14:16]`–`[18:20]` Stulberg argues happiness is "never the result of aiming for happiness... it's
always a byproduct of doing something else," naming his own most engaging activities (writing,
deadlifting, coaching his son) as ones where, in the moment, "the answer would undoubtedly be no" if
asked whether he's happy — he's engaged, uncomfortable, or satisfied instead. He presents a
philosophy-classroom thought experiment: offered a tube providing permanent drug-induced euphoria in
exchange for all real relationships and struggle, most people decline — and Stulberg draws the
comparison explicitly: **"what the tube starts to sound quite a bit like [is] a heroin addiction...
it shrinks your life."** The stated implication: happiness pursued directly and continuously
resembles a narrowing, addictive state rather than a flourishing one.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Feeling that a device's recovery/readiness score should override your own judgment about
  whether you can perform** — named directly as a source of fragility, via the J.J. Spaun example.
  `[04:07]`
- **Feeling restless, aggravated, or in need of "a hot shower" after an immersive digital session**
  — named as the retrospective marker of "shitty flow," distinguished from genuine flow's
  fulfillment-plus-exhaustion. `[08:11]`
- **Exhaustion, apathy, and dread despite a low or moderate workload** — named as "zombie burnout,"
  distinct from and easily mistaken for conventional overwork burnout. `[09:11]`
- **Judging yourself for not feeling happy "enough," or for experiencing discomfort during
  meaningful work** — reframed as a symptom of the "happiness industrial complex" rather than
  evidence something is wrong. `[15:16]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Disevolution** — credited to evolutionary scientist Daniel Lieberman: the mismatch between
  cravings evolved under historical scarcity and a modern environment engineered for abundance.
  `[00:00]`
- **Shitty flow** — credited to psychologist/philosopher David Pizarro: an immersive, flow-like
  state that is values-discordant, identifiable by post-experience regret rather than fulfillment.
  `[05:08]`
- **Zombie burnout** — Stulberg's own term for burnout arising from insufficient meaningful
  engagement rather than from overwork. `[09:11]`
- **The happiness industrial complex** — Stulberg's term for cultural pressure to treat happiness
  itself, rather than meaning or satisfaction, as life's central goal. `[14:16]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The experience machine ("the tube")** — a hypothetical offering guaranteed chemical euphoria at
  the cost of real life, explicitly compared to heroin addiction's narrowing effect, used to argue
  that most people implicitly value meaning over pure pleasure. `[17:17]`
- Pornography versus an intimate relationship, and passive spectating versus "stepping into the
  arena" — both used as examples of low-friction, high-reward substitutes that feel good without
  producing lasting fulfillment. `[01:02]`–`[02:04]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as the author's own framework (Brad Stulberg, *The Way of Excellence*), explicitly
crediting specific terms to their originators (disevolution to Daniel Lieberman, shitty flow to
David Pizarro, flow itself to Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi) rather than claiming them as his own —
personal anecdotes (J.J. Spaun, his own deadlifting practice) are clearly framed as illustrative
examples, not data.
