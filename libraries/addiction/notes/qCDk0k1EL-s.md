# These 12 values drive every decision you've ever made | Chris Bailey

## Central claim

**Everyone holds the same 12 fundamental human values (per Shalom Schwartz's 1992 research), just
in different proportions, and a goal that conflicts with a person's strongest values will provoke
involuntary resistance regardless of how sound the goal is — so a stuck goal is often better fixed
by reframing it to connect with a top value than by abandoning it.** `[01:00]` "The research shows
there are 12 fundamental human values that we all share. Every single one of us has these but in
different amounts... our values are our motivational core. They are at the core of everything that
we want to do and everything we don't want to do."

## The 12 values and their two generative polarities

`[01:00]`–`[02:01]` Bailey states all 12 values emerge from two basic polarities: whether a person
is oriented toward **enriching themselves or enriching others**, and whether they're oriented
toward **conserving things as they are or improving/changing them**. The 12 named values:
**self-direction** (going your own way), **stimulation** (novelty, excitement), **pleasure**,
**achievement**, **power** (dominance over resources/people — named as rating lowest both
cross-culturally and generally, despite structural roles like "every city has a mayor" requiring
it), **face** (avoiding humiliation, managing how others see you), **security** (personal and
societal), **tradition**, **conformity**, **humility**, **universalism** (protecting people and
nature broadly), and **benevolence** (kindness, serving others).

## Diagnosing a stuck goal via values conflict

`[07:06]`–`[08:07]` Bailey's worked example: a goal like "write employee handbook" can feel like
pure conformity to someone who values self-direction, provoking an internal "get this goal out of
my face" reaction. Reframed as "mentor new employees and welcome new employees through the
handbook update" — describing largely the same actions — the goal now connects to a value of
benevolence instead, and the resistance is described as dissolving because "one is aligned to what
you actually care about. The other just feels like something you have to do."

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **A visceral, "get this out of my face" resistance to a specific goal** — named as a values
  conflict (e.g. a self-direction-valuing person facing a conformity-coded goal), not a motivation
  or willpower deficiency. `[07:06]`
- **Consistently gravitating toward certain activities during unstructured free time** — named as
  a person's "default intentions" revealing their top values even without conscious reflection.
  `[06:06]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Schwartz's 12 fundamental human values** — self-direction, stimulation, pleasure, achievement,
  power, face, security, tradition, conformity, humility, universalism, benevolence; credited to
  Professor Shalom Schwartz's 1992 theory. `[01:00]`

## Analogies worth reusing

The "write employee handbook" → "mentor new employees through the handbook update" reframe — a
concrete, reusable template for how to edit a goal's framing to connect with a different value
without changing its underlying actions. `[07:06]`–`[08:07]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented with an explicit, named academic citation (Professor Shalom Schwartz, 1992) rather than
Bailey's own invention — one of the more directly sourced claims in this collection's self-
improvement content, though the transcript doesn't detail Schwartz's original methodology beyond
naming him. Bailey also discloses a commercial connection: he co-created a values assessment with
Schwartz distributed with his own book, disclosed directly rather than presented as neutral.
