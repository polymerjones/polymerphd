# Goals set us up for disappointment. Build this instead | Chris Bailey

## Central claim

**A goal is fundamentally a prediction, not a guarantee, and treating it as a guaranteed outcome is
what manufactures the disappointment cycle — the fix is a four-step process (shape, act, edit,
maintain) that treats goals as hypotheses to be revised with feedback, rather than commitments to be
judged pass/fail.** `[00:00]` "A goal is really a prediction which often turns into an expectation
of what will end up happening in our life, which turns into disappointment when it turns out that
we can't predict the future, which turns into yet another goal that we set."

## SMART goals don't hold up, per the source

`[01:00]`–`[02:03]` Bailey directly challenges a widely-circulated heuristic: **"there's a lot of
advice out there about SMART goals... but the research shows that they don't work."** His stated
critique: the criteria (specific, measurable, attainable, realistic, time-bound) are redundant with
each other, specificity isn't helpful during a goal's early learning phase, and he cites unnamed
"research waste" around the SMART framework generally — offered as a claim from research he
references but doesn't cite by name in this transcript.

## The four-step cycle: shape, act, edit, maintain

`[03:03]`–`[19:24]` **Shape**: define both the outcome (what) and the process (how, day to day) —
Bailey flags "sepia-toned" goals here, ones that sound appealing in the abstract (his own example:
wanting to be an early riser) but prove disliked once actually lived. **Act**: begin, accounting for
the six aversion triggers below rather than relying on initial desire alone, since desire
"fluctuates incredibly over the span of a goal." **Edit**: revise the goal or its process as
feedback arrives — "a big sign that you should be editing a goal is you're not doing it anymore."
**Maintain**: goals that succeed enough to become automatic risk being taken for granted and
dropped; Bailey recommends explicitly adding a "maintenance goal" to a goal inventory, and — by his
own admission the hardest advice for him to follow — actually celebrating completion rather than
moving straight to the next target.

## Six aversion triggers, and a specific counter for each

`[09:08]`–`[13:14]` A task or goal feels aversive when it is: **boring, frustrating, unpleasant,
unstructured, far away in the future,** or **meaningless** (disconnected from one's values).
Countermeasures given per trigger: gamify a boring task, pair a frustrating one with something
pleasant (Bailey's example: "a fancy latte"), set milestones for something far off, add an
accountability partner for something unstructured, and reconnect a meaningless task to a genuine
value. **Aversion journaling** is offered as a catch-all: at the moment of wanting to skip
something, choose either to do it or to journal honestly about exactly why it feels ugly.

## "Shrink the resistance" and the procrastination numbers

`[10:11]`–`[12:13]` Citing an unnamed "procrastination researcher," Bailey states **15–20% of
adults chronically procrastinate**, about a third of students admit to chronic procrastination, and
a separate survey found **99% of a surveyed population** admitted to procrastinating on goals at
all — with his own aside that "that 1% is lying through their teeth." The practical tactic:
negotiate a resisted task down in scope (45 minutes of meditation → 30 → 20 → 10) until it's small
enough to actually start, on the stated logic that resistance is concentrated at the beginning of a
task rather than spread evenly through it.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Repeated disappointment after setting and missing goals** — reframed as a structural feature of
  treating a prediction as a guarantee, not a personal shortcoming. `[00:00]`
- **A goal that sounded appealing when set but is now actively disliked in daily practice** — named
  as a "sepia-toned" goal, a mismatch between the idea of a goal and its lived reality. `[04:03]`
- **Consistently not doing a goal despite intending to** — named directly as the signal that the
  goal (not the person's willpower) needs editing. `[14:16]`
- **Procrastinating on a specific task** — reframed as a normal, near-universal reaction driven by
  one or more of six specific aversion triggers, not a character flaw. `[10:11]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Sepia-toned goal** — a goal that looks appealing as an idea but proves disliked once actually
  lived day to day. `[04:03]`
- **Aversion triggers** — the six named qualities (boring, frustrating, unpleasant, unstructured,
  far-off, meaningless) that make a task or goal feel resistant to start. `[09:08]`
- **Goal inventory** — a reviewed (weekly, per Bailey's own practice) list of every goal a person is
  currently pursuing, organized by life context. `[16:19]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- "It takes a little bit of time to jump into the cold pool, but once we're in there, we warm up
  very quickly" — used for why resistance to a task is front-loaded rather than sustained.
  `[12:13]`
- The Annie Dillard line, "how we spend our days is of course how we spend our lives" — used to tie
  daily process back to the broader goal it's meant to serve. `[04:03]`
