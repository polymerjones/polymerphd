# Getting 1% better every day is mathematically impossible. Do this instead | Brad Stulberg

## Central claim

**The popular "get 1% better every day" framework accurately describes early-stage rapid
improvement but becomes literally impossible to sustain — Layne Norton, a world-champion
powerlifter, added only 8 lb to his deadlift (about 0.8% per year) across an entire decade — so
lasting progress after the early gains requires shifting motivation from observable daily gains to
curiosity, values-alignment, and consistency through extended plateaus.** `[09:09]` "If Layne
Norton was addicted to that kind of progress, if Layne Norton said, 'I just need to get 1% better
every day,' he would have quit. He worked 10 years to gain less than a percentage point of
performance."

## The arrival fallacy: Ray Allen's championship morning

`[02:02]` Stulberg's central illustration for why achievement itself doesn't deliver lasting
fulfillment: NBA player Ray Allen believed winning a championship would fill an internal void, and
after finally winning one in 2008, **"the morning after winning that championship was one of the
most disorienting mornings of his life because he realized that it didn't fulfill him."** Stulberg
names this the **arrival fallacy** and connects it to "post-marathon blues" — the letdown after
achieving a heavily-built-up goal — arguing satisfaction actually lived in the daily training, not
the outcome.

## A process-over-outcomes framework, and why to almost forget the goal

`[00:00]`–`[01:02]` The stated sequence: pick an aspirational goal to give the pursuit shape, break
it into manageable milestones, then **"largely forget about the big goal"** and focus on small
daily or weekly steps — using stress or worry about the big goal as a cue to ask "what could you be
doing right now" rather than continuing to dwell on the goal itself. SMART goals (specific,
measurable, actionable, relatable, time-bound) are offered as a starting framework for those new to
structured goal-setting.

## Consistency over heroic intensity, backed by sports-injury data

`[05:03]`–`[11:09]` Stulberg contrasts "heroic days" (an all-nighter, an extreme workout) with
sustained progress, arguing the former undermines the latter: "if I tried to build a career writing
like that, my writing would go to crap." He cites sports-science research directly: **"when
athletes increase their acute training load... by more than 10% of their chronic training load...
rates of injury and illness skyrocket"** — used as cross-domain evidence that short-term intensity
spikes borrow against, rather than build, long-term capacity. His practical guidance: keep most
effort at 5-7 out of 10, and stop "one rep... one sentence... one meeting short" of maximum, to
preserve the next day's rhythm.

## Plateaus as a required, not failed, part of progress

`[13:11]`–`[15:12]` Stulberg reframes a plateau as an active, necessary phase rather than stalled
progress: **"plateaus are also a feature because generally speaking, before we have a breakthrough,
we experience a plateau"** — likened to a stone that "might crack on the 32nd pound" of hammering,
with invisible tension accumulating through the prior 31. He states this is also where intrinsic
motivation (values, curiosity) has to replace the "external... dopamine hit" of externally visible
progress.

## Micro-objectives: Kaillie Humphries' four-year goal broken into days

`[15:12]`–`[17:14]` Five-time world champion bobsledder Kaillie Humphries' stated goal — "win a gold
medal every 4 years" — is broken down recursively: 4 years into two 2-year phases, into years, into
quarters, into months, into weeks, into single daily workouts. Stulberg frames this structure as
solving two problems at once: it keeps attention on the immediate, manageable next step (rather
than an overwhelming distant peak) and supplies motivating "contrived little victories" along the
way.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Discouragement or demotivation when daily/weekly progress stops being observable** — reframed
  as the normal, expected transition into a plateau phase rather than a sign of failure. `[08:05]`
- **Feeling disoriented or unfulfilled immediately after achieving a long-pursued goal** — named
  directly as the "arrival fallacy," illustrated by Ray Allen's post-championship reaction and
  "post-marathon blues." `[02:02]`
- **The urge to push to maximum intensity ("leave it all out there") on a given day** — reframed as
  a trap that borrows against future consistency rather than a sign of dedication. `[12:10]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Arrival fallacy** — the mistaken expectation that reaching a goal will itself produce lasting
  satisfaction, rather than the pursuit of it. `[02:02]`
- **"Going to see God"** — a phrase Stulberg attributes to collaborator Steve Magness, for the rare
  moments of maximal, all-out effort that a sustained practice occasionally calls for, as distinct
  from routine training intensity. `[07:05]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Pounding a stone** that "might crack on the 32nd pound" — used to describe invisible tension
  building through a plateau before a breakthrough. `[14:12]`
- **Climbing a mountain while looking only at the next section, not the distant peak** — used to
  explain why micro-objectives prevent both discouragement and the risk of "tripping" from not
  attending to the immediate step. `[15:12]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as the author's own synthesis (Brad Stulberg, *The Way of Excellence*), combining named
case studies (Ray Allen, Layne Norton, Kaillie Humphries) offered as illustrative anecdotes with one
explicitly cited body of sports-science research (acute:chronic training load and injury risk) —
the two registers are kept distinguishable in the source's own framing.
