# Why Your Body Was Actually Designed for Squatting

## Central claim

**The deep resting squat — not sitting in a chair — is the posture the human body was built to default to, and losing the ability to hold it is disuse, not aging: joints and soft tissue throughout the hip-spine-pelvis-knee-ankle chain depend on the full-range compression and release that only a deep squat provides, and most of what gets attributed to "getting older" in these tissues is reversible.** Stated directly: **"none of it is decline in the way we usually think of decline, as something irreversible, something owed to time. It's disuse, stacked day after day until disuse starts to look indistinguishable from aging. And disuse, unlike aging, can be reversed"** `[13:18]`.

## The ancestral default

`[03:03]`–`[05:06]` — Before chairs, "the default off switch for the human body was the ground," settling into a deep squat — heels flat, hips low, spine stacked — for rest, eating, and low-to-the-ground labor (grinding grain, tending fire, nursing a child). The source describes anthropological observation of modern hunter-gatherer groups resting in full squats for 20–40 minutes at a stretch, "sometimes falling asleep sitting flat on their heels," and states the deep squat is documented as "one of the most common resting postures on record" across dozens of such societies. Chairs are framed as a historically recent invention, originally reserved for status ("a throne, not a default"), meaning **"your skeleton has had nowhere near enough time to evolve around it."**

## Why the joint needs the full range: synovial fluid and cartilage

`[06:07]`–`[07:09]` — The hip is a ball-and-socket joint lubricated by synovial fluid that "isn't pumped in from some external source. It's distributed by movement itself" — compression and release across the joint's full range. Cartilage has no blood supply and cannot repair itself the way skin or muscle does; its survival depends entirely on this mechanical squeeze-release cycle. Sitting at roughly 90° for 8–12 hours a day never reaches that full range — **"a joint that never closes fully is a joint that's slowly starving a tissue it has no other way of feeding."**

## Gluteal amnesia and shortened hip flexors

`[07:09]`–`[09:12]` — At a seated hip angle, the glutes — "the largest and most powerful muscle group in your entire body" — go nearly silent because the chair, not the muscle, is bearing the load; disused muscles lose neural connection to the brain, a pattern the source calls **gluteal amnesia**. Simultaneously, hip flexors held in a shortened position for hours adapt to that length, so "standing up straight starts to feel like a small negotiation with your own body." This shows up later as unexplained lower-back ache, glutes that "never quite engage properly even when you do try to exercise them," and stiffness getting out of a car attributed to age rather than posture.

## The spine: static compression versus natural decompression

`[09:12]`–`[10:14]` — Spinal discs have no independent blood supply and rely on movement-driven pumping for nutrients. Seated posture (especially slumped) loads discs with continuous, unevenly distributed pressure — "like standing on a sponge and never lifting your foot." A deep squat does close to the opposite: as the pelvis tilts, the lumbar spine gently lengthens, vertebrae separating slightly — **"spinal decompression built into a posture you already know how to do, or once did."**

## The puborectalis and elimination

`[11:16]`–`[12:17]` — The puborectalis wraps around the rectum like a sling and, in upright sitting, stays contracted, bending the rectum at an angle that supports continence day-to-day but becomes an obstacle during elimination — "the equivalent of trying to drain a garden hose that's been bent at a sharp angle," requiring straining that over decades "contributes to hemorrhoids and pelvic floor strain." Deep hip flexion relaxes the puborectalis and straightens the bend into an open channel, which the source links to lower rates of elimination difficulty in populations where deep squatting remains a daily default.

## The pelvic floor: needs release, not just strength

`[13:18]`–`[15:22]` — The pelvic floor is described as a hammock between pubic bone and tailbone that needs to lengthen and release as well as engage; deep hip flexion is "one of the few positions that actually stretches this hammock out to its full, relaxed length" — sitting and standing do not ask this of it. Without that release, the pelvic floor doesn't simply weaken; it can become **hypertonic** — chronically gripped and unable to generate real force when needed, "the same way a fist clenched all day has nothing left to give" — or it can go the opposite direction and weaken from partial-range living. Either path traces to the same root: a floor "stuck in the narrow middle of its range."

## The knee: depth is not the danger

`[16:23]`–`[18:25]` — The source directly challenges the advice to avoid letting the knee travel past the toes or to stop at 90°. In a shallow squat, kneecap stress concentrates on a small patch of cartilage; in a full deep squat, that load spreads across a much larger surface at hip, knee, and ankle simultaneously — **"wider, distributed pressure isn't more dangerous. It's safer,"** compared to lying on a bed of nails resting on one nail versus a thousand. Knee cartilage shares the hip's avascular, compression-fed biology. The source attributes fear of deep squatting to observing poor technique (rounded backs, collapsing arches) rather than depth itself: **"depth was never the villain. Poor mechanics were."**

## The ankle: the silent, unfelt loss

`[18:25]`–`[19:26]` — Ankle **dorsiflexion** (the shin tipping forward over a planted heel) is framed as the range most people lose without noticing, because unlike a sore knee, a stiff ankle "doesn't announce itself with pain" — it forces compensation upstream: a rounding lower back, knees caving inward, or weight shifting onto the toes. Heels lifting off the ground during an attempted deep squat is attributed to this locked ankle rather than general weakness. Regular deep squatting is described as "ongoing ankle maintenance" as much as a hip/knee exercise.

## Independence in later life

`[20:27]`–`[22:30]` — The ability to lower to the ground and rise again unaided is framed as one of the strongest predictors of continued independence in older adults — training balance, ankle mobility, and hip strength together, the combination the source says prevents a stumble from becoming "a hospital stay that turns into a loss of independence that never fully reverses."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Heels lifting off the ground, knees "screaming," or losing balance when attempting a deep squat** — attributed to locked ankle dorsiflexion and lost range rather than injury risk `[00:00]`, `[19:26]`.
- **Unexplained lower back ache**, and **glutes that never fully engage during exercise** — linked to gluteal amnesia and chronic hip-flexor shortening from chair sitting `[09:12]`.
- **Straining during bowel movements**, linked over decades to hemorrhoids and pelvic floor strain — the puborectalis's seated anorectal bend `[11:16]`.
- **Stiffness getting out of a car, commonly attributed to age** — reframed as accumulated disuse rather than an inevitable aging process `[09:12]`.
- **A chronically gripped or unresponsive pelvic floor** — hypertonicity or weakness from a lifetime of partial-range use `[14:20]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Gluteal amnesia** — reduced neural connection to the glute muscles from prolonged seated disuse `[07:09]`.
- **Dorsiflexion** — the ankle motion of tipping the shin forward over a planted foot `[05:06]`, `[18:25]`.
- **Puborectalis** — the sling muscle around the rectum that maintains the anorectal bend during upright sitting `[11:16]`.
- **Hypertonic (pelvic floor)** — chronically tight and gripped rather than weak, from never fully releasing `[14:20]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Watering a plant only at the surface** — partial-range movement failing to reach cartilage that needs deep flexion `[06:07]`.
- **A rubber band scrunched too long forgetting how to stretch back out** — hip flexors adapting to a chronically shortened seated length `[08:11]`.
- **Standing on a sponge and never lifting your foot** — static, unrelieved disc compression from prolonged sitting `[10:14]`.
- **A bed of nails: painful on one nail, fine on a thousand** — why wider load distribution in a deep squat is safer for knee cartilage than a shallow one `[17:24]`.
- **A fist clenched all day has nothing left to give** — a pelvic floor that never releases losing its ability to generate force `[14:20]`.
