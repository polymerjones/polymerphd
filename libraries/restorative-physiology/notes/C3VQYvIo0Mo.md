# The 3 Fall Directions YOUR Body Has Never Rehearsed

## Central claim

**Balance operates independently in three planes, most people have trained only one, and the falls that
injure arrive from the other two** `[01:01]`, `[27:30]`.

> "**The balance the 10-second stand measures is real. The balance the fall demands is different. The
> directions you have trained are not the directions in which you will fall**" `[02:04]`.

## The opening test

`[00:00]`:

> **Shift your weight onto one foot. Reach the other foot 12 inches to the side and bring it back.**

> "**The wobble in your standing leg. The scramble at the hip. The correction you barely made happened in a
> direction your balance has almost certainly never been trained.**"

## Three planes, three systems

`[02:04]`–`[05:07]`

*(Independence established by **Winter**, University of Waterloo, through force plate analysis —
"demonstrating that the control strategies for anteroposterior and mediolateral balance use fundamentally
different muscles, different neural pathways, and different reflex timings. **Training one does not transfer
to the others.**")*

| Plane | Movement | Everyday examples | Stabilisers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sagittal** | Forward and back | **Walking, leaning to reach a shelf** | **Ankle strategy** — gastrocnemius and tibialis anterior modulating ankle angle |
| **Frontal** | Side to side | **Stepping around furniture, reaching sideways for a handrail, getting into a car** | **Hip strategy** — gluteus medius on the stance side preventing pelvic drop; adductors on the swing side controlling the return |
| **Transverse** | Rotational | **Turning to look behind you, twisting to reach a side table** | **Deep hip rotators** — piriformis, obturator internus and externus, gemelli, quadratus femoris — plus the oblique abdominal chains |

**Why the ankle cannot help laterally** `[03:06]`–`[04:07]` — **a purely geometric argument:**

| Direction | Base of support |
|---|---|
| **Anteroposterior** | **~25 cm** |
| **Mediolateral** | **~8–10 cm** |

> "**There is simply not enough platform for ankle modulation to be effective laterally. The hip must do the
> work that the ankle cannot.**"

**What clinical tests actually measure** `[03:06]`:
> "**The single leg stand, the tandem stance, the Romberg test — all sagittal, all anteroposterior, all
> testing the ankle strategy while leaving the hip strategy and the rotational stabilisers unmeasured.**"

> **And the deep rotators are the least trained of the three** `[04:07]`: "the smallest and least trained of
> the three stabilisation groups, **and the stabilisers most relevant to the rotational falls that occur
> when older adults turn while walking.**"

**Why static standing does not fill the gap** `[05:07]`:
> "**Because the body is static, no lateral or rotational perturbation arrives.** The ankle strategy muscles
> improve. The hip strategy muscles and rotational stabilisers receive minimal stimulus."

## What each tap trains

`[05:07]`–`[13:15]`

### Forward tap — the posterior chain

**A 12-inch reach shifts the centre of mass 5–8 cm forward** — "**you are creating a perturbation with your
own moving leg**," fundamentally different from passive sway during static standing.

| Muscle | Action |
|---|---|
| **Gluteus maximus** | Extends the hip backward |
| **Hamstrings** | Prevent the knee flexing under the shifted load |
| **Gastrocnemius** | Presses into plantarflexion, pulling the tibia backward and shifting weight toward the heel |

**Real-world scenarios it rehearses** `[06:08]`: "reaching for something on a shelf further away than you
estimated, being nudged from behind in a queue, bending to pick something up while standing on one leg
because the other foot is on a step or a curb."

**And the calibration is two-sided** `[06:08]`–`[07:08]`:
> "**Too much response pulls you backward into overcorrection. Too little lets you fall forward past the
> recovery point.** The cerebellum computes the error between the intended correction and the actual
> outcome and adjusts the motor command for the next cycle."

### Lateral tap — the gluteus medius

`[07:08]`–`[11:11]`

> "**The highest demand gluteus medius activation available from a standing position — higher than lateral
> stepping, which allows weight transfer to the moving foot and distributes the load across both legs.
> During the lateral tap, the stance leg bears full body weight while the perturbation occurs**" `[07:08]`.

**The 200 millisecond window** *(attributed to Maki and McIlroy, *Journal of the American Geriatrics
Society*)*: compensatory stepping responses to unexpected perturbation **must initiate within this window
to prevent a fall in older adults.**

**Why this direction matters most** `[08:09]`–`[10:09]`:

> **In a lateral fall, "the greater trochanter of the femur strikes the ground. The force transmits through
> the femoral neck — the narrowest point of the thigh bone."** It fractures because **the bone there is
> thin, the trabecular lattice is sparse from years of declining loading and hormonal support, and the
> force arrives laterally — the direction the femoral neck's geometry is least equipped to resist.**

| Fall direction | Typical injury |
|---|---|
| **Forward** | **Wrist fracture** — "the instinct to extend the arm is protective of the hip at the cost of the wrist" |
| **Backward** | **Occipital skull injury and cervical spine trauma** — dangerous but least frequent |
| **Lateral** | **Hip fracture — the highest morbidity and mortality of any fall-related injury** |

> **20% of adults over 65 with a hip fracture do not survive the following year. Among survivors, 50% do not
> return to their pre-fracture mobility level** `[09:09]`.

> "**The hip fracture is not merely an injury. It is the entry point to a deconditioning cascade**"
> `[09:09]`.

> "**Only lateral perturbation trains the lateral response**" `[10:09]`.

### Backward tap — the anterior chain

`[11:11]`–`[12:12]`

**Quadriceps stiffen the stance leg, hip flexors pull the pelvis forward, tibialis anterior dorsiflexes to
shift weight toward the toes.**

> "**Backward perturbation is the direction nobody trains because no common daily movement produces it.
> Walking is forward. Stair climbing is upward. Even lateral stepping is sideways**" `[11:11]`.

> "**The direction is neglected because it is rare in daily life, but its rarity is precisely why the
> corrective response is weakest.** The anterior chain has never been asked to fire reactively against
> backward displacement, and **the cerebellar computation for backward correction has no training data
> set**" `[11:11]`.

## Why three directions in sequence beats three separate exercises

`[13:15]`–`[17:19]` — **the analytical core.**

**The cerebellum learns from prediction error** — the difference between where it expected the body to be
after the correction and where it actually ended up. **Purkinje cells**, each receiving input from **up to
200,000 parallel fibres**, detect the error and adjust the next command.

> "**More error means more learning. The synaptic weights change faster when the prediction was wrong**"
> `[14:16]`.

**Data set density** `[14:16]`:

| Protocol | Data sets per session |
|---|---|
| **Three taps × five cycles × two legs** | **30 data sets across three motor strategies** |
| **Static single leg standing** | **One data set — anteroposterior sway correction — repeated for 30 seconds** |

**And the switching is itself the training** `[15:16]`:
> "The cerebellum must shift from posterior chain activation to gluteus medius activation to anterior chain
> activation — **three different motor strategies executed in 6 seconds. The switching itself is the
> training.**"

> **Because real perturbations do not announce their direction** `[15:16]`: "A lateral push in a crowd
> arrives without warning. A backward slip on a wet surface announces itself only through the sudden loss of
> traction under the heel. A rotational stumble during a turn reveals its direction only as the torso begins
> spiralling past the vertical axis."

> "**In each case the cerebellum has approximately 200 milliseconds to identify the perturbation direction,
> select the correct motor strategy from its database, and execute the corrective response at the required
> magnitude**" `[15:16]`.

**The trial evidence** `[16:17]` *(attributed to Mansfield and colleagues, *Gait and Posture*, 2015)*:

> **Perturbation-based balance training reduced fall rates more effectively than traditional balance
> exercises including static tandem stance** — "**and the difference was not marginal**… despite spending
> equivalent total time in balance training."

> "**The body learns what it rehearses. It does not generalise well to what it has not rehearsed**"
> `[17:19]`.

## Five factors, and why they multiply

`[17:19]`–`[22:26]`

| Factor | Decline | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Gluteus medius force** | **1–2%/year sarcopenia** | **"The gluteus medius that generated 50 newtons of peak abduction force at 40 generates 35 at 60. The perturbation that required 40 newtons to catch is no longer within the muscle's capacity. The timing was adequate. The force was not"** |
| **Purkinje cell density** | **~2.5% per decade after 50** | The computation loses precision — **"the body overcorrects and stumbles in the opposite direction, or undercorrects and continues falling"** |
| **Vestibular hair cells** | Progressive loss | **"The system may not recognise the lateral displacement until the body has already moved further than the correction can recover"** |
| **Proprioceptive receptor density** | Thinning in ankle and hip | **"A system that reported ankle angle with 1° accuracy at 40 reports with 3° accuracy at 70"** |
| **Peripheral conduction velocity** | Slowing, plus slower central processing | **"The 200 ms that was met with 50 ms to spare at 40 is met with 10 ms remaining at 65 — or not met at all"** |

**The multiplicative argument** `[21:24]` — **the clearest statement of compounding in the corpus:**

> "**If each of the five factors reduces balance performance by 10% independently, the compound reduction is
> not 50%. It is not additive. Each factor multiplies the effect of the others.**"

> "**A late, degraded, less accurate, weaker correction arrives more slowly.** The multiplicative effect
> produces a compound performance reduction that exceeds any individual factor, **and this is why fall rates
> increase non-linearly after 65 rather than linearly from 50 onward.**"

**What the protocol can and cannot reach** `[20:23]`–`[22:26]` — **stated honestly:**

| Addressable by the protocol | Not addressable |
|---|---|
| **Corrective force magnitude** — repeated practice at near-maximum demand | Vestibular hair cell loss |
| **Cerebellar computation and switching speed** — three data sets per cycle | Proprioceptive receptor thinning |
| | Peripheral conduction slowing |

> "**The two trainable factors partially compensate for the three that training cannot reach — buying
> milliseconds in a window measured in milliseconds, buying newtons of force in a correction measured in
> newtons**" `[22:26]`.

*(Partial help for the other three is attributed to the head turn progression and barefoot practice.)*

## The protocol

`[23:28]`–`[27:30]`

### Setup

> **Stand behind a sturdy chair, fingertips resting on the chair back — "not gripping, not leaning, just
> touching for emergency support that the balance system knows is available."**

> **Why touch matters** `[24:29]`: "**The knowledge of the support changes the neural drive. The motor cortex
> allows greater perturbation magnitude when it knows a recovery option exists.**"

### The cycle

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **1** | Shift weight onto the right leg, lift the left foot 1 inch |
| **2** | **Forward tap** — reach 12 inches forward, **touch the toe lightly** (lightly enough that the stance leg bears full body weight; the touch is only a spatial target), return to centre |
| **3** | **Lateral tap** — 12 inches to the left, touch, return |
| **4** | **Backward tap** — 12 inches behind, touch, return |

**One cycle = three taps, three planes, ~6 seconds.**

| Parameter | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Cycles** | **5 per leg** |
| **Time** | **~45 seconds per side, 90 seconds total** |
| **Pace** | **~2 seconds out, 2 seconds back** |
| **Frequency** | **Daily** |
| **Best time** | **Morning** |

**Why slow** `[25:29]`:
> "**Speed defeats the purpose. Fast tapping uses momentum rather than muscular control, and momentum
> bypasses the cerebellar computation the protocol is designed to train.** The learning occurs during the
> slow controlled phase where the balance system must compute continuously."

**Why morning** `[25:29]`:
> "**The first session of the day produces the largest cerebellar prediction errors** because the system has
> not computed balance corrections during the overnight period. **Larger errors mean faster learning** — the
> Purkinje cells adjust their synaptic weights more aggressively when the prediction error is large."

### Progression

| Week | Support | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Fingertips on the chair | — |
| **2** | **One finger** | — |
| **3** | **Hand hovering above the chair without contact** | — |
| **4** | **No chair** | — |

> **Each reduction increases stance leg muscle activation by approximately 15%**, "because the motor cortex
> can no longer rely on the hand to catch an overcorrection" `[26:29]`.

**After four weeks — add the head turn** `[26:29]`:
> **Turn to look over the opposite shoulder while tapping to the side.** "This creates a vestibulo-visual
> conflict. The semicircular canals report the head rotation while the visual system reports the room
> spinning in the opposite direction. **The cerebellum must resolve the conflict while simultaneously
> computing the balance correction for the lateral perturbation.**"

> "**This is the exact scenario that produces falls in daily life** — turning to look at something while
> walking."

### Safety — stated by the video

`[23:28]`:

> "**Anyone with a recent hip or knee replacement, active vertigo, or a diagnosed balance disorder should
> perform the tap protocol with both hands on a sturdy chair until their physician confirms readiness for
> reduced support. The deliberate perturbation may exceed the reactive capacity of a system still recovering
> from surgery or vestibular disruption.**"

## Practices

1. **The three-direction tap cycle, 90 seconds daily** — five cycles per leg, forward / lateral / backward.
2. **Move slowly — 2 seconds each way.** Momentum bypasses the computation being trained.
3. **Morning, for the largest prediction errors and fastest learning.**
4. **Reduce hand support one level per week over four weeks.**
5. **Add a head turn during the lateral tap after four weeks** to train the vestibulo-visual conflict where
   real falls happen.
6. **Both hands on the chair if recovering from joint replacement, vertigo, or a diagnosed balance
   disorder.**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Wobble in the standing hip when stepping sideways around furniture** `[00:00]`.
- **A hesitation when turning to look behind while walking** `[00:00]`.
- **The standing leg working harder when reaching sideways than forward** `[00:00]`.
- Moments filed under clumsiness or aging `[00:00]`.
- Stumbles arriving from the side or during turns rather than straight ahead `[02:04]`.
