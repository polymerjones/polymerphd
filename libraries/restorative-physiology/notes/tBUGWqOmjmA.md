# The Morning Exercise That Exceeds the SAFETY LIMIT for YOUR Spine

## Central claim

**Every sit-up generates roughly 3,400 newtons of lumbar compression — 100 newtons above the industrial
safety threshold at which a worker would be pulled off the line** `[00:00]`–`[01:02]`.

> "**A factory worker who loaded their spine to 3,400 newtons repeatedly would be pulled off the line. You
> do it 50 times before breakfast**" `[00:00]`.

> "**The exercise chosen to protect the back applies the force that damages it**" `[02:02]`.

## The threshold, and why the comparison is fair

`[00:00]`–`[02:02]`

**NIOSH sets the action limit for spinal compression at 3,300 newtons** — "the threshold above which an
industrial worker is considered at risk for cumulative disc injury and would be reassigned or the task
redesigned."

**It is not arbitrary** `[01:02]`: "derived from injury surveillance across millions of worker years."

**A warehouse worker generating 3,400 N would trigger a mandatory ergonomic intervention** — task
redesigned, load reduced, or worker rotated. **"The assessment would not wait for an injury."**

**Why no such assessment exists for exercise** `[01:02]`–`[02:02]`: "because the occupational and
recreational domains do not share a regulatory framework."

> "**The spine does not distinguish between occupational and recreational loading. The disc does not know
> whether the 3,400 newtons came from lifting a box or performing a sit-up**… **The regulatory distinction
> between workplace and gym does not exist at the tissue level**" `[02:02]`.

## Where the force comes from — the psoas

`[03:04]`–`[06:07]`

*(Measured by Stuart McGill, University of Waterloo, across 30 years using intradiscal pressure
transducers and biomechanical modelling — **~3,400 N of compression on the L4–L5 disc at peak sit-up
motion.** Documented in *Low Back Disorders*.)*

**The psoas major** originates from the transverse processes and vertebral bodies of **T12 through L5**,
crosses the pelvis, and inserts on the **lesser trochanter of the femur.**

> **So when it contracts during a sit-up, it pulls the lumbar vertebrae downward and forward toward the
> femur.** "The vertebrae above are pulled toward the vertebrae below through the muscle's contraction, and
> **the disc between them absorbs the compressive force**" `[04:06]`.

**The disc is a hydraulic cushion** `[04:06]`: the **nucleus pulposus** is **70–80% water under pressure**,
contained by **annular fibres in concentric laminated layers like the plies of a radial tire** — each layer
at a different angle, creating a cross-hatch that distributes stress in multiple directions.

| Loading | Stress distribution |
|---|---|
| **Pure axial compression** | Nucleus pressurises uniformly; annular layers absorb it through **hoop stress across the cross-hatch** |
| **Combined flexion and compression** | **Posterior layers bear disproportionate load as the nucleus pushes toward the back of the disc** |

> "**The harder the psoas contracts, the tighter the press closes** and the more the nucleus pressurises
> against the annular wall that contains it" `[05:06]`.

## Why this is precisely the herniation mechanism

`[07:08]`–`[11:12]`

**In flexion:** the anterior annulus compresses, the posterior annulus stretches, and the nucleus — a
pressurised gel under **Pascal's law** — shifts posteriorly **toward the weakest point of the wall.**

**The geometry is the problem** `[08:09]`:

| Wall | Thickness |
|---|---|
| **Anterior annulus** | ~3 mm |
| **Posterior annulus** | **~1–1.5 mm** |

**And the posterior longitudinal ligament that reinforces it narrows at L4–L5 and L5–S1** — **the two
segments bearing the highest proportion of the sit-up's compressive force.**

> "**The weakest point of the wall sits at the segment bearing the highest load.** The geometry concentrates
> the failure risk at the exact location the nucleus pushes hardest during flexion."

**One rep = one posterior nucleus migration above the industrial threshold.**

### The cycle arithmetic

`[08:09]`–`[10:10]`

| | Count |
|---|---|
| **50 reps/day × 365 days × 10 years** | **182,500 high-force flexion cycles** on the posterior annulus |
| **Cadaveric testing shows measurable annular delamination at** | **~5,000–10,000 cycles** |
| **50 reps/day reaches the lower end in** | **~100 days — about three months** |

> "**The first measurable delamination… begins within months of starting the exercise**" `[09:09]`.

**Paris law again** `[08:09]`: each cycle advances existing micro-damage "by increments too small to feel."

> "**The failure threshold for a single cycle is far above the load any single sit-up applies. But the
> failure threshold for 182,000 cycles is far below it**, because fatigue damage accumulates with each
> cycle regardless of whether the single-cycle threshold is exceeded" `[09:09]`.

**And the silence is the point** `[10:10]`–`[11:12]`:
> "**You felt nothing during the accumulation.** The annular damage propagated below the threshold of
> sensation… **The herniation appeared sudden.** The morning you could not stand straight… **was the end
> point of a fatigue process that had been running below the pain threshold for years or decades.**"

> "**The damage was two decades in the making**, loaded 50 times every morning by an exercise you believed
> was protecting the structure it was progressively weakening" `[11:12]`.

## The EMG — the wrong muscle is doing the work

`[11:12]`–`[14:16]` — **the practical heart of the video.**

*(Attributed to Juker and colleagues, *Clinical Biomechanics*, from the McGill laboratory.)*

| Phase of the sit-up | Prime mover | Compression |
|---|---|---|
| **First 30° of trunk flexion** (the curl, shoulders leaving the floor) | **Rectus abdominis, maximally active** | **~1,400–1,800 N — below the NIOSH threshold** |
| **30° to 90°** (the hip flexion phase) | **Psoas — rectus activity plateaus** | **Climbs from 1,800 to 3,400 N** as the moment arm lengthens |

> "**70% of the sit-up's range of motion trains the psoas.** The abdominals, the intended target, are
> maximally trained in the first 30% of the range… **The portion generating the dangerous compression
> trains a hip flexor you never intended to strengthen, using the lumbar spine as the anchor point you
> never knew the muscle was attached to**" `[12:13]`.

**The reframe of what the burn means** `[13:14]`–`[14:16]`:
> "**The portion of the exercise that felt most productive was the portion producing the most spinal
> compression with the least abdominal contribution**… The harder I pushed, the more the psoas pulled, the
> more the discs compressed, and the less the abdominals contributed. **The effort and the benefit were
> inversely correlated beyond 30°.**"

## The institutional verdict

`[14:16]`–`[16:19]`

**The US Army removed the sit-up from the Army Combat Fitness Test in 2021, replacing it with the plank.
The Canadian Armed Forces followed.**

> **The assessment:** "**Sit-ups were the single highest contributor to training-related lower back injuries
> across military populations**" — across millions of service members, decades, and standardised
> conditions.

> "**The decision was actuarial.** The cost of treating the injuries exceeded the benefit of the training…
> **The exercise damages more spines than it strengthens cores**" `[15:16]`.

**The conceptual shift** `[16:19]`: **from training core flexion to training core stability — from
producing movement to resisting movement.**

**The guy-wire model:** the rectus resists extension, the obliques resist rotation, the multifidus resists
flexion — **all three pulling against each other to create the stiffness that prevents the spinal column
buckling under load.**

> "**Guy-wires resist movement. They do not produce it.**"

**The plank generates ~1,000–1,200 N** — well below the threshold — **with comparable core activation**
through isometric contraction. **The spine stays neutral, the nucleus stays centred, the annulus bears no
flexion-driven posterior loading.**

## The replacement — the McGill curl-up

`[17:20]`–`[20:24]`

**Setup:** supine, **one knee bent, one leg straight**, **hands placed under the lumbar spine.**

> **The hand position is engineering, not comfort** `[17:20]`: "The hands prevent the lumbar spine from
> flattening into the floor during the curl, maintaining the natural lordotic curve **that keeps the
> nucleus centered in the disc.** Without the hands, the lower back presses flat, the lumbar spine flexes,
> and the nucleus shifts posteriorly — **recreating the loading pattern the curl-up was designed to
> avoid.** The hands are a mechanical stop."

**Movement:** head and shoulders lift **2–3 cm off the floor** — **the first 15–20° of trunk flexion only.**
**Hold 10 seconds.**

**Why the hold rather than reps** `[18:22]`:

| Contraction type | Fibres recruited | Matches |
|---|---|---|
| **Sustained isometric** | **Slow-twitch type I — fatigue-resistant** | **The core's actual job: sustained postural resistance for minutes to hours** |
| **Dynamic sit-ups** | **Fast-twitch type II** | Rapid force production and quick fatigue — "explosive movement the core rarely needs to produce" |

**The measured comparison** `[19:23]` *(attributed to Axler and McGill, *Medicine and Science in Sports and
Exercise*)*:

| | Rectus activation | Lumbar compression |
|---|---|---|
| **Full sit-up** | 100% (reference) | **~3,400 N** |
| **McGill curl-up** | **85–90%** | **~1,400–1,600 N — 50% below the NIOSH threshold** |

> **Why it works:** "**The curl-up stops at the transition point — 30°, where the rectus peaks and the psoas
> has not yet engaged.**"

**And why you never noticed the transition in a sit-up** `[19:23]`:
> "**The burn masks the transition.** You feel abdominal effort throughout the movement, but the force
> generating the motion shifted from the rectus to the psoas at 30° and the compression climbed with it."

**The daily arithmetic** `[20:24]`:

| | Total daily lumbar compression |
|---|---|
| **50 sit-ups** | **170,000 N** |
| **50 curl-ups** | **75,000 N — a 56% reduction**, at 85–90% of the same rectus activation |

## The big three, and the programming

`[20:24]`–`[22:26]`

| Exercise | Plane resisted | Trains |
|---|---|---|
| **Curl-up** | **Sagittal — resists extension** | Rectus abdominis |
| **Side plank** | **Frontal — resists lateral flexion** | Obliques, lateral stabilisers |
| **Bird dog** (contralateral arm and leg from quadruped) | **Transverse — resists rotation** | Multifidus, erector spinae |

> "Each exercise addresses one plane. **Together, they create the three-dimensional stiffness the guy-wire
> system requires**" `[21:24]`.

**The protocol:**

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| **Reps** | **6–8 repetitions of 10-second holds** per exercise |
| **Sets** | **One set of each** |
| **Frequency** | **Daily** |
| **Total time** | **~8 minutes** |
| **Total lumbar compression, all three combined** | **~12,000–15,000 N — less than four sit-ups** |

> **Why daily and simple** `[21:24]`: "The core stabilisation function operates every waking hour, and the
> training that maintains it should be **sustainable daily rather than demanding enough to require recovery
> days.**"

**The principle underneath it all** `[22:26]`:
> "**Every sit-up trains the core to produce flexion.** The spine needs the core to **resist** flexion…
> **Concentric training and isometric resistance are opposite motor patterns. The exercise trained the
> opposite of what the joint requires.**"

## Why the crunch is not the answer

`[23:26]`–`[24:27]` — **an important qualification.**

**A crunch generates ~1,800–2,000 N** — below NIOSH — **but still loads the disc under flexion.**

> "**Safer per rep, but it preserved the mechanism.**"

**And the fatigue arithmetic bites back** `[24:27]`: replacing 50 sit-ups with 150 crunches **reduces
per-cycle force by ~40% but triples the cycle count**, "potentially producing equivalent or greater
cumulative annular fatigue."

> "**The mathematics of cumulative fatigue is indifferent to the per cycle force below the threshold. What
> matters is the total energy delivered to the posterior annulus across all cycles**" `[24:27]`.

**The curl-up eliminates the mechanism rather than reducing its dose:** 15–20° of flexion, hands preserving
lordosis, **no posterior nucleus shift, no flexion under load.**

## The absence-of-pain warning

`[25:28]` — **worth quoting in full.**

> "**If you have been performing sit-ups daily for years and have not experienced back pain, the absence of
> symptoms does not confirm the absence of fatigue damage.** Annular delamination progresses below the pain
> threshold for years before the breach occurs. **The Paris law mechanics run silently.**"

> "The damage accumulates silently across years of loading. **The symptom arrives later, often attributed
> to the specific event that completed the fracture rather than the years of cyclic loading that propagated
> it.**"

> "**The sit-up you performed this morning was safe by itself. The question is how many preceded it**"
> `[25:28]`.

**Clinical routing** `[24:27]`: whether a current programme should be modified, whether existing symptoms
relate to sit-up history, and what replacement is appropriate is a conversation with a physiotherapist or
physician. "**The physics teaches what the exercise does to the disc. The clinician determines what your
specific spine requires.**"

## Practices

1. **Stop doing full sit-ups.** Every repetition exceeds the industrial action limit, and 70% of the range
   trains the psoas rather than the abdominals.
2. **Do the McGill curl-up instead** — one knee bent, one leg straight, **hands under the lumbar spine to
   preserve the arch**, head and shoulders **2–3 cm** off the floor, **hold 10 seconds.**
3. **Add the side plank and the bird dog** — the three together cover all three planes of resistance.
4. **6–8 reps of 10-second holds per exercise, one set each, daily. About 8 minutes.**
5. **Do not substitute high-volume crunches** — lower force at higher cycle counts can deliver the same
   cumulative fatigue.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- Sudden inability to stand straight, with pain radiating down the leg `[10:10]`.
- Herniation that "appeared without warning" after decades of daily sit-ups `[11:12]`.
- The abdominal burn that intensifies past 30° and feels most productive `[13:14]`.
- **Absence of back pain despite years of sit-ups — explicitly not evidence of absence of damage**
  `[25:28]`.
