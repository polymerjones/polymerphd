# Why Stroke Recovery Feels Like an Altered State

> **Format note:** this video answers a named viewer's comment. "Sue left a comment under the vagus nerve video that stopped me. She wrote that she is recovering from a stroke and she asked about the science of stroke recovery and why it feels like an altered state." `[00:00]` The whole video is structured as a reply to her question, which makes it a strong candidate for `08_QUESTIONS_AND_ANSWERS.md`.

## Central claim

"Altered state" is not a metaphor and not a psychological reaction to illness. It is the felt experience of a brain whose entire network topology changed in a single vascular event — and every component of that experience has a name, a mechanism and a measurable physical substrate. `[00:00]` `[23:27]` `[25:29]`

> "The altered state is the felt experience of a brain in the process of remapping. **And a brain that was not remapping would not feel altered. It would feel static.**" `[29:34]`

## The first minutes

**Two types, one fundamental event** `[01:01]` `[02:03]`: approximately **85% ischemic** (a clot blocks flow) and **15% haemorrhagic** (a vessel ruptures and bleeds). In both, "a region of brain tissue loses the oxygen and glucose it requires to maintain its electrical activity."

**Why the brain has no margin** `[02:03]`: 2% of body mass consuming **20% of total metabolic energy**, with essentially no reserves. "Unlike muscle which stores glycogen for sustained activity, neurons operate on moment-to-moment delivery. **They are running on a supply line, not a fuel tank.**"

**The collapse sequence** `[02:03]` `[03:04]`:
1. **Within seconds** — neurons begin losing membrane potential, because the sodium-potassium pumps maintaining it require ATP, and ATP generation halts when oxygen delivery stops
2. **Within minutes** — membrane potential collapses entirely; calcium floods in through voltage-gated channels that can no longer be regulated
3. Calcium triggers a destructive enzyme cascade: **proteases** digesting structural proteins, **lipases** dissolving membrane lipids, **endonucleases** fragmenting DNA — "dismantling the cell from within"
4. At the centre, neurons die within minutes. This is the **infarct core**

**The cost of delay** `[03:04]`: every minute of delayed treatment during an ischemic stroke costs approximately **1.9 million neurons, 13.8 billion synapses, and 12 km of myelinated nerve fibres.**

> "The brain is not slowly declining during a stroke. It is undergoing a rapid, precisely sequenced physical collapse… running on the same thermodynamic physics that governs every cell in the body when its energy supply is cut." `[03:04]`

## The penumbra — the territory that determines recovery

Surrounding the core is the **ischemic penumbra**: a border zone receiving reduced but not absent flow through collateral vessels — arteries interconnect through **anastomoses** that can partially supply a region whose primary vessel is blocked. `[04:04]`

**The critical state** `[04:04]`: penumbral neurons are **electrically silent** — they have lost enough membrane potential to stop firing action potentials, but retain enough ATP to maintain basic cellular integrity. **"They are in suspended function, not death."**

**Two outcomes** `[04:04]` `[05:06]`:

| If flow is restored quickly — thrombolysis (dissolving the clot chemically) or mechanical thrombectomy (removing it physically) | If flow is not restored |
|---|---|
| Penumbral neurons resume function. **"They were off, not dead"** | The penumbra progressively recruits into the infarct core over hours as ATP reserves deplete and the calcium cascade extends outward |

> "The emergency room that delivers thrombolysis within 60 minutes of symptom onset is not merely treating quickly, it is **racing the penumbra.**" `[05:06]`

**Why similar strokes produce different outcomes** `[05:06]` `[06:08]`: the penumbra "is not a fixed boundary. It is a dynamic one, shifting as collateral blood flow varies, as brain swelling changes tissue pressure, as the body's own fibrinolytic mechanisms work to dissolve the clot from the inside."

> "The survivor who recovered more function than expected was, in part, someone whose penumbra remained viable long enough… **This is not luck in the casual sense. It is hemodynamics.**" `[06:08]`

**For the family member** `[06:08]`: the partial return of speech or movement in the days after the first hours "was penumbral neurons coming back online. They were never dead… **The recovery that seemed miraculous was physics.**"

## Diaschisis — the direct answer to Sue's question

**Constantin von Monakow** described it in **1914** and named it **diaschisis**, from the Greek for *shock throughout*. `[07:11]`

**The principle** `[07:11]`: damage to one region causes **functional suppression in anatomically distant but connected regions** — not through direct damage, but through the loss of the electrical signals those distant regions depend on.

**Chain:** every region receives continuous **tonic input** from connected regions through white matter tracts, "the physical cables of the neural network" → this background electrical activity maintains each region's baseline excitability and readiness → when a stroke destroys neurons in one area, the regions they projected to suddenly lose that input → temporary functional shutdown in **physically intact, structurally undamaged** tissue, possibly centimetres or a full hemisphere away. `[07:11]` `[08:12]`

**Confirmed by imaging** `[08:12]`: PET and fMRI in acute stroke consistently show reduced metabolic activity and blood flow remote from the infarct —
- The **cerebellum on the opposite side** from a cortical stroke
- The **thalamus**, connected to virtually every cortical region, after cortical strokes
- The **contralateral cortex** — the mirror-image region — after large unilateral strokes

> "The brain did not only lose the functions of the damaged region, it lost **the electromagnetic scaffold that held the whole network in its normal operating state.**" `[09:13]`

**Why symptoms seem unrelated to stroke location** `[09:13]`: a motor cortex stroke affecting the right hand can produce changes in attention, emotional processing, and the quality of visual experience. "The person is not imagining these changes. The diaschisis is real, and it affects regions that the medical team may not have discussed because they were not directly damaged."

**"Spontaneous recovery" renamed** `[10:14]`: "Diaschisis resolution is what clinicians call spontaneous recovery. **It is not spontaneous.** It is the brain's intact regions coming back online as the electromagnetic environment normalizes. The functions that return first are often the functions that were lost through diaschisis, rather than through direct damage. They return because the neurons that perform them were never destroyed, only silenced."

## Excitotoxicity — the second wave

**Chain** `[10:14]` `[11:14]`:
1. Neurons dying in the core release their contents into the extracellular space, including **glutamate**, the brain's primary excitatory neurotransmitter
2. Glutamate is normally cleared from the synapse within milliseconds by transporter proteins — **but the transporters require ATP**, and in ischemic tissue ATP is depleted
3. Glutamate accumulates and floods **NMDA and AMPA receptors** on neighbouring neurons "that were not designed to be activated at this concentration"
4. Massive overstimulation drives calcium influx into **healthy neurons adjacent to the damaged zone — neurons the original vascular event never reached**
5. The same destructive enzyme cascade that killed the core neurons is triggered

**John Olney** at Washington University coined the term **excitotoxicity** in **1969** after observing this in animal models. `[11:14]` `[12:16]`

> "The brain's own primary signaling molecule, at sufficiently high concentration, becomes the most potent neurotoxin in the brain's immediate environment… **The stroke converts a neurotransmitter into a weapon.**" `[11:14]` `[12:16]`

**Why strokes worsen after onset** `[12:16]`: "The dying tissue poisons the surviving tissue adjacent to it. The penumbra… is under attack from two directions, loss of blood flow from below, glutamate flooding from above. The time window for penumbral salvage is, in part, a race against excitotoxicity."

**The bitter symmetry** `[13:16]`: "the molecule the brain uses for every thought, every movement, every sensation, every memory… is the molecule that extends the damage when the system loses the energy to clear it. **The strength of the signal becomes the weapon.** The more active the region was before the stroke, the more glutamate it stored, and the more excitotoxic damage it can inflict on its neighbors."

## The BDNF window

**Chain** `[13:16]` `[14:17]`: neurons in the **peri-infarct zone** — the region immediately surrounding the permanent damage — begin upregulating **brain-derived neurotrophic factor** at levels exceeding anything seen in the normal adult brain.

**What BDNF does** `[14:17]`: drives growth of new **dendritic spines** (the tiny protrusions where synaptic connections form), strengthens existing synapses, promotes entirely new connections, and activates **TrkB receptors** on surviving neurons, triggering cascades that change gene expression and structural protein synthesis.

**The childhood comparison, stated as literal** `[14:17]` `[15:17]`: researchers describe the resulting window as resembling **the critical periods of early childhood brain development**. "That comparison is not casual. The molecular mechanisms are identical. The BDNF concentrations are comparable. The rate of synaptic formation and elimination… is similarly elevated."

| Childhood plasticity | Post-stroke plasticity |
|---|---|
| Building from a blank template — laying down circuits that never existed, connecting regions that had never communicated | Rebuilding around a permanent loss — routing new connections around dead tissue, reassigning functions to regions built for other purposes, "improvising solutions to problems the developmental program never anticipated" |
| Advantage: a blank canvas | Advantage: an existing network with decades of established connections that can be re-routed |

"Neither advantage is absolute. Both produce results that, measured in synaptic connections formed per day, are comparable." `[15:17]`

**Hebbian physics** `[16:18]`: **Donald Hebb's** principle — *neurons that fire together wire together* — "is the physics of all learning, all memory, all skill acquisition. After a stroke, the identical mechanism operates at an accelerated rate."

Reorganisation is measurable on fMRI **as early as days after the stroke**: regions that never represented certain functions begin showing activation when those functions are attempted. `[16:18]`

**The window is finite** `[16:18]` `[17:18]`: plasticity elevation gradually returns to baseline over weeks to months, "which is why the intensity of rehabilitation effort in the early post-stroke period determines outcomes in ways that effort months later cannot fully replicate."

> "The physics does not care about the patient's mood, their motivation, or their belief in recovery. It cares about **co-activation** — repeated firing of the neurons being recruited, repeated practice of the functions being recovered… Each attempt, however frustrating, however imperfect, physically strengthens the synaptic connections that the attempt activated. **The effort is not psychological, it is molecular.**" `[17:18]`

## Rehabilitation fatigue, explained

**Chain** `[17:18]` `[18:18]`: **synaptogenesis** — the formation of new synaptic connections — is "one of the most metabolically expensive processes the brain performs," and the BDNF-driven plasticity of the post-stroke window consumes glucose and oxygen at rates approaching early childhood development.

> "The survivor who is exhausted after 30 minutes of rehabilitation is not weak. They are running a **construction project in their cortex** that is consuming energy at developmental rates in a brain that is simultaneously managing a damaged network. **The fatigue is proportional to the work the physics is doing.**" `[18:18]`

## Redrawing the map

**The map was never fixed** `[18:18]` `[19:19]`: the motor cortex holds a topographic body map — the **homunculus** first described by **Wilder Penfield**. But the hand representation in a professional pianist is measurably larger than in a non-musician; the reading-finger representation in a Braille reader is expanded relative to the other fingers. "The map has always been activity-dependent, plastic, responsive to demand."

**Chain after stroke** `[19:19]`: surviving adjacent cortex expands into the territory vacated by damage → **driven by activity, not by passive time** → motor attempts, "the effortful and often frustrating attempts to move a limb that rehabilitation demands," generate firing in the peri-infarct motor cortex → Hebbian strengthening of surviving synapses and growth of new connections into the former domain.

**Measured** `[19:19]` `[20:21]`: transcranial magnetic stimulation mapping in stroke survivors shows progressive shifts in cortical representation over weeks of recovery — "the representation moving from the damaged zone into adjacent surviving tissue, **measurably in millimeters**, driven by repeated motor attempts."

> "The brain is not healing the damaged tissue. It is redrawing the map around the damage. The geography changes, the function moves." `[20:21]`

## The mirror neuron route

**Chain** `[20:21]`: watching another person perform a movement activates the observer's motor cortex — specifically the regions that would activate if the observer performed it. This is the **mirror neuron system**, identified by **Giacomo Rizzolatti's** group in Parma.

**Clinical application** `[20:21]` `[21:23]`: a survivor whose motor cortex is damaged can activate the surviving adjacent circuits being recruited for remapping **by watching another person perform the movement.** "Watching is doing at the level of cortical activation."

**Mirror therapy** — placing a mirror between the arms and watching the reflection of the unaffected arm moving while attempting movement with the affected arm — "works through exactly this mechanism. The brain is using visual information about movement as a proxy for the motor command signal it can no longer generate efficiently from the damaged cortex." `[21:23]`

> "Observation and attempt together produce stronger remapping than either alone. The brain responds to the activation itself regardless of whether it was generated by a motor command or by a visual signal." `[21:23]`

## The 101st attempt

> "The map does not move in response to intention. It moves in response to activation. **The trying is the mechanism. The watching adds to it.**" `[22:24]`

> "The movement that fails on the 100th attempt and succeeds on the 101st did not succeed because of perseverance in the motivational sense. It succeeded because the 100 previous attempts had collectively strengthened the synaptic connections in the remapped cortex to the point where the neural command finally exceeded the threshold required to produce the motor output. **The 100 failures were not failures. They were the construction phase.** The success was the moment the construction reached functional capacity. Every attempt before it was load-bearing." `[22:24]`

## Decomposing the altered state

Every component named and mapped `[23:27]` `[24:28]`:

| Felt experience | Physical substrate |
|---|---|
| **The world looks different** | The visual processing stream is receiving changed thalamic input |
| **Thinking feels different** | The prefrontal cortex is operating in a changed electromagnetic environment |
| **The sense of self feels shifted** | The default mode network, most associated with self-referential processing, is receiving altered input from multiple diaschisis-affected regions simultaneously |
| Global strangeness | Diaschisis has suppressed the tonic input holding the network in its normal state; excitotoxicity has altered the chemical environment; the BDNF surge has made the brain "simultaneously more responsive and less stable than normal"; the cortical map is shifting; and the thalamus — connected to essentially every cortical region — is almost certainly in diaschisis, altering signal routing throughout the cortex |

**Resolution is not uniform** `[24:28]` `[25:29]`:

| Timescale | What resolves |
|---|---|
| **Weeks** | Diaschisis-driven suppression of distant regions lifts as the acute inflammatory response subsides and the network's electromagnetic environment stabilises |
| **Months** | Cortical remapping and contralateral recruitment progressively restore lost functions — "not to their original form, but to a reorganized form that may feel slightly different from the pre-stroke experience" |
| **Never fully** | The permanent loss of the infarct core means the network will never return to its exact pre-stroke topology. "The new equilibrium is a new brain organized differently, routing differently, experiencing itself differently" |

> "The altered state does not fully disappear. It evolves into a new normal that the survivor gradually stops experiencing as altered because it becomes the baseline." `[25:29]`

> "The altered state is not confusion. It is not psychological reaction to illness. It is the physics of a brain whose network topology has been suddenly changed experiencing itself from inside the changed network. **There is no other way it could feel.**" `[25:29]`

## The four mechanisms of recovery

Dead neurons do not regenerate in the adult human brain. The infarct is permanent. "What recovery is physically is the reorganization of surviving tissue to take over the functions of what is gone." `[25:29]` `[26:30]`

| Mechanism | What it does | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Diaschisis resolution** | Intact distant regions coming back online as acute suppression lifts | The "spontaneous recovery" of the first weeks |
| **2. Cortical remapping** | Adjacent surviving cortex expanding into new functional territory via Hebbian plasticity and the BDNF window | The recovery that depends on practice and effort |
| **3. Contralateral recruitment** | The undamaged hemisphere contributing increasingly to functions previously dominated by the damaged one, "drawing on latent capacity that the healthy brain never needed to use and the catastrophe revealed" | Bilateral compensation |
| **4. White matter remodelling** | Surviving neurons extending new axonal projections toward regions they are being recruited to serve — "new cables being laid" | Structural reconnection of the reorganised network |

> "None of these mechanisms require the survivor to understand them. All of them require the survivor to **use** the brain… because every mechanism listed runs on neural activity. The plasticity is not passive. It is driven by demand… **The physics of recovery is the physics of use.**" `[27:32]`

## Recovery after the window closes

> "The window represents the period of maximum reorganization *speed*… After the window narrows, plasticity continues at the brain's normal adult rate. Slower, less dramatic, but real. Stroke survivors continue to recover function months and years after the acute period, driven by the same Hebbian mechanisms that produce all learning. The improvement at month 12 is slower than the improvement at week four. **But it is running on identical physics. The brain does not stop reorganizing. It reorganizes at its normal rate rather than its emergency rate. The effort still matters. The practice still builds connections. The map still moves.**" `[27:32]` `[28:32]`

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **The "altered state" itself — the world looking different, thoughts arriving differently, the self feeling shifted** — diaschisis plus changed thalamic routing, prefrontal environment and default mode network input. `[23:27]` `[24:28]`
- **Symptoms unrelated to the stroke's location** (attention, emotional processing, visual quality changes after a motor stroke) — loss of tonic input to connected networks. `[09:13]`
- **Rapid partial return of function in the days after onset** — penumbral neurons resuming, having been silenced rather than killed. `[06:08]`
- **Worsening in the hours after onset despite restored flow** — glutamate excitotoxicity extending damage into surviving tissue. `[12:16]`
- **Profound fatigue disproportionate to the physical effort of therapy** — synaptogenesis running at developmental metabolic rates. `[18:18]`
- **Repeated failed attempts at a movement** — reframed as the load-bearing construction phase, not failure. `[22:24]`
- **The sense that recovery has plateaued after the first months** — plasticity has shifted from emergency rate to normal adult rate, not stopped. `[28:32]`
- **The worry that feeling strange means something is going wrong** — "It is a sign that the reorganization is happening." `[29:34]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Ischemic vs haemorrhagic stroke** — clot-blocked flow (~85%) versus vessel rupture (~15%). `[01:01]`
- **Infarct core** — the central zone of irreversible damage where neurons die within minutes. `[03:04]`
- **Ischemic penumbra** — the border zone receiving reduced flow through collateral vessels; electrically silent but structurally intact. `[04:04]`
- **Anastomoses** — the interconnections between cerebral arteries that supply collateral flow. `[04:04]`
- **Thrombolysis / mechanical thrombectomy** — dissolving the clot chemically versus removing it physically. `[04:04]`
- **Diaschisis** — von Monakow, 1914; functional suppression in distant connected regions from loss of tonic input. `[07:11]`
- **Tonic input** — the continuous background electrical activity from connected regions that maintains baseline excitability. `[07:11]`
- **Excitotoxicity** — Olney, 1969; glutamate at high concentration killing neurons through receptor overstimulation. `[11:14]` `[12:16]`
- **NMDA and AMPA receptors** — the glutamate receptors driving calcium influx when overstimulated. `[11:14]`
- **BDNF / TrkB receptors** — the plasticity signal and its receptor. `[14:17]`
- **Dendritic spines** — the protrusions where synaptic connections form. `[14:17]`
- **Hebbian plasticity** — "neurons that fire together wire together." `[16:18]`
- **Cortical homunculus** — Penfield's topographic body map in the motor cortex. `[18:18]`
- **Transcranial magnetic stimulation** — the technique used to map cortical representation shifts. `[19:19]`
- **Mirror neuron system** — Rizzolatti, Parma; motor cortex activation from observing movement. `[20:21]`
- **Contralateral recruitment** — the undamaged hemisphere taking on functions of the damaged one. `[26:30]`
