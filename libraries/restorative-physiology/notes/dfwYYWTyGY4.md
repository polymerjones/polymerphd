# Why YOUR Strength Doesn't Prevent Falls

## Central claim

**Strength is non-directional; balance correction is directional — and walking maintains only one of four
directional programs** `[00:00]`, `[28:38]`.

> "**The fall does not test strength. It tests direction**" `[28:38]`.

> "**You could squat twice your bodyweight and still fall at the same rate as the person beside you who has
> never lifted a weight**" `[00:00]`.

*(Attributed to Sherrington and colleagues, *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*, 2019 — **108 trials,
nearly 24,000 participants**, confirming **strength training alone does not significantly reduce fall rates
in older adults.**)*

## Why strength does not transfer

`[00:00]`–`[04:03]`

**Every muscle produces force along one fixed anatomical line of pull.**

> "The muscle does not know whether the body was pushed forward, sideways, or backward. **It generates force
> along one anatomical axis.** Strengthening it increases the magnitude of force along that line. **It does
> not improve the body's ability to select the correct muscle for a perturbation arriving from an
> unpractised direction**" `[00:00]`–`[01:00]`.

| Perturbation | The only muscles that can correct it | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Lateral** | **Gluteus medius on the stance leg** | Abduction force **within 200 ms** |
| **Backward** | **Anterior chain — quadriceps and hip flexors** | Pull the centre of mass forward before it passes the recovery point |
| **Rotational** | **Deep hip stabilisers and oblique abdominal chains** | Resist torsional displacement around the vertical axis |

**The squat example** `[02:01]`–`[03:03]`: someone squatting three times weekly with quadriceps 50% above
population norms has gained real, measurable, functional force — **in the sagittal plane only.**

> "**The transfer to fall prevention is near zero, because the falls that injure and kill do not arrive
> along the sagittal axis in the population that already walks daily**" `[02:01]`.

> "**The force production is excellent. The force direction is perpendicular to the perturbation. The
> perpendicular force is mechanically invisible to the balance problem**" `[03:03]`.

**The categorical distinction** `[03:03]`–`[04:03]`:

| | Asks | Answered by |
|---|---|---|
| **Strength training** | Produce maximum force along the line of pull — **an intensity question** | **The sarcomere** |
| **Balance challenge** | Detect a direction, select the correct muscle, fire it at the correct magnitude within the reactive window — **a coordination question** | **The cerebellar circuit** |

> "**The sarcomere and the cerebellar circuit are different structures trained by different stimuli,
> producing different outcomes**" `[04:03]`.

## The geometry of falling

`[04:03]`–`[08:09]`

| Stance | Base of support |
|---|---|
| **Two-legged** | **~30 cm × 25 cm** |
| **Single-leg** (held for **~40% of each walking stride**) | **~10 cm × 25 cm** |

**Supporting a structure ~170 cm tall with the centre of mass at roughly 60% of that height** — at the level
of **the second sacral vertebra, roughly the navel.**

> "**No engineer would design a free-standing structure with these proportions**, yet the human body
> maintains it through continuous active correction, computing and executing postural adjustments **every
> millisecond of standing and walking**" `[04:03]`.

**The reactive window: approximately 200 milliseconds** to push the centre of mass back inside the boundary
before momentum carries the body past the recovery point.

**And this is a timing constraint, not a force constraint** `[05:04]`–`[06:07]`:

> With aging — **proprioceptive receptors thinning, conduction velocity slowing, cerebellar Purkinje cells
> declining at 2.5% per decade** — "**the window narrows independently of muscle strength. A stronger muscle
> that fires 20 milliseconds late is still late.**"

> "**Strength cannot compress the window. Only program readiness** — the calibration accuracy of the
> cerebellar computation for that specific direction — determines whether the corrective response initiates
> within it" `[06:07]`.

## What walking actually trains

`[08:09]`–`[12:16]`

**Walking is a sagittal-plane activity throughout** — the body, the arm swing, and the centre of mass
oscillation are all anteroposterior.

> "**10,000 steps per day is 10,000 anteroposterior balance corrections**, each one an opportunity for the
> cerebellar computation to update its error correction algorithm for the sagittal plane. **The
> anteroposterior program is the best-maintained balance program in any adult who walks**" `[08:09]`.

**But the frontal plane receives almost nothing** `[09:10]`: gluteus medius activates during stance as a
**tonic stabiliser at ~20–25% of maximum voluntary contraction**, preventing pelvic drop.

| | Tonic stabilisation | Reactive correction |
|---|---|---|
| **Level** | **Low, continuous, predictable** — maintains a known position | **High, rapid, unpredictable** — responds to an unknown perturbation |
| **Activation** | **20–25% MVC** | **60–80% MVC** |
| **Motor units** | Low threshold | **High-threshold units that produce peak abduction force — never recruited during walking** |
| **Cerebellar data set** | Its own | **A separate one** |

> "**The gluteus medius is used daily and loaded never**" `[16:20]`.

> "**The gluteus medius can stabilise the pelvis beautifully during every walking stride** — smooth,
> controlled, automatic pelvic levelling that produces no visible gait abnormality — **while simultaneously
> losing its capacity to generate the emergency contraction that catches a lateral stumble**" `[15:19]`.

**A stale program is not an absent one** `[10:12]`: "technically present in the circuitry, technically
executable if called upon, **but practically stale because the error correction data it relies on has not
been updated in months or years.**"

### The neighbourhood walk audit

`[11:13]`–`[12:16]` — **the most concrete demonstration in the video.** A 40-minute walk produced:

| Plane | Challenges |
|---|---|
| **Anteroposterior** | **Thousands** — one per step |
| **Lateral** | **Zero** — straight path, even surface |
| **Backward** | **Zero** — forward locomotion produces no backward perturbation |
| **Rotational** | **Two** — brief, slow corner turns with the head facing the turn, **no vestibulo-visual conflict** |

> "**The walk that felt like comprehensive exercise for my legs was comprehensive exercise for one of four
> directional programs**" `[12:16]`.

**And the training implication** `[13:17]`:
> "Tandem stance, heel-to-toe walking and standard single-leg standing **all primarily challenge
> anteroposterior stability**… **For the audience that already walks daily, the anteroposterior program is
> maintained by walking itself. Adding more anteroposterior challenge reinforces the one plane that is
> already stable** while the three planes that are degrading continue degrading."

> "**Studying the subject you already know while the exam tests four subjects**" `[13:17]`.

**On single-leg stance** `[13:17]`:
> "**Standing on one leg is not the training. It is the platform from which directional training begins**" —
> valuable because it reduces the base of support to the footprint where all four directional corrections
> must operate at higher demand.

## Plane by plane

### Lateral — degrades fastest, breaks hips

`[14:19]`–`[18:23]`

*(Attributed to Hilliard and colleagues, *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation* — **mediolateral
instability predicts future falls more strongly than anteroposterior instability.**)*

**Why the injury is so severe** `[16:20]`–`[17:23]`:
> "**The greater trochanter impacts the ground directly** because the body has no protective mechanism to
> redirect a lateral fall. **No reflexive arm extension reaches sideways fast enough. No torso rotation
> converts the lateral momentum into a forward roll.**"

| Fall direction | Typical injury | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| **Anteroposterior** | Wrist fracture from the outstretched hand, or vertebral compression | "Serious, but survivable" |
| **Lateral** | **Femoral neck fracture** — the narrowest point of the thigh bone | **Highest morbidity and mortality of any fall-related injury over 65** |

> **20% do not survive the following year. Among survivors, 50% do not return to their pre-fracture level of
> independent mobility** `[17:23]`.

> "**The hip fracture is not merely an injury. It is the entry point to a deconditioning cascade** —
> hospitalisation, bed rest, muscle atrophy across all four planes simultaneously, loss of walking
> confidence, reduced activity, accelerated sarcopenia **in the very muscles that should prevent the next
> fall**" `[17:23]`.

### Backward — the emptiest program, and the fastest to fix

`[18:23]`–`[23:32]`

> "**No common daily movement produces backward perturbation.** Walking is forward. Stair climbing is
> upward. Even lateral stepping is sideways. **The cerebellar program for backward reactive correction sits
> unused for years or decades in most adults**" `[18:23]`.

**The muscle/program distinction is sharpest here** `[18:23]`–`[19:25]`:
> "**The anterior chain is strong.** These muscles fire forcefully during every sit-to-stand, every stair
> ascent… **What is not maintained is the reactive programming**… **The muscles await a command the
> cerebellar computation cannot accurately generate because it has no recent calibration data.**"

**Why backward walking produces the most dramatic improvement in clinical trials** `[19:25]`–`[21:28]`:
> "**Not because it strengthens the anterior chain, which is already strong** — but because **it reloads the
> backward cerebellar program with recent error correction data.**"

> "Each backward step generates a small backward perturbation the anterior chain must correct. **Each
> correction provides an error signal — the difference between the cerebellar prediction and the actual
> measured outcome.** The prediction was based on outdated data: muscle forces that have changed,
> proprioceptive thresholds that have shifted, reaction times that have extended. **The error signal updates
> every parameter simultaneously**" `[20:26]`.

> "**The first backward walking session fills an empty database with fresh error correction data the
> cerebellum has been waiting for**" `[21:28]`.

**Timeline** `[21:28]`–`[22:30]`: **within 2 weeks of daily backward walking — even three or four steps
along a kitchen counter — the computation recalibrates**, with **measurable force plate improvement in
mediolateral and backward sway parameters "before any strength gain could possibly occur."**

**Why backward falls are the most dangerous per event** `[22:30]`–`[23:32]`:
> "**Forward falls trigger the arm extension reflex. Lateral falls trigger lateral arm extension. Backward
> falls produce no anticipatory arm extension.** The arms cannot reach behind fast enough, and even if they
> could, the wrist cannot absorb impact from behind… **The head hits the ground with minimal deceleration**
> — and **the occipital bone at the back of the skull is thinner than the frontal bone.**"

**Injuries named: occipital skull fracture, subdural haematoma.**

### Rotational — three converging declines, one irreversible

`[23:32]`–`[27:38]`

| Decline | Detail | Reversible? |
|---|---|---|
| **Vestibular hair cell death** in the semicircular canals | Detect angular head acceleration; **die progressively and do not regenerate** | **No** — "the only plane where part of the degradation is biologically irreversible" |
| **Visual processing slowing** | The cortex needs longer to resolve the shifted scene after a head turn | Partially |
| **Cerebellar Purkinje cell thinning** — **~2.5% per decade after 50** | The cells integrating vestibular and visual input | Partially |

**What happens in the gap** `[24:33]`:
> "The vestibular system says the head rotated 30°, the visual system has not yet confirmed the new
> orientation of the room, and **the cerebellar computation must decide whether to trust the vestibular
> input alone or wait for visual confirmation.**"

| System | Decision |
|---|---|
| **Young, dense Purkinje population** | **Fast** — trusts vestibular input, generates a provisional correction, refines it when visual confirmation arrives 50 ms later |
| **Aging, thinned** | **Slower decision, less accurate provisional correction, later refinement** — "the centre of mass moves further from the base of support during the processing delay" |

> "**The delay that was invisible at 40 becomes the fall at 70**" `[24:33]`.

**Where real falls happen** `[25:35]`:
> "**Real-world falls rarely occur during straight-line walking or quiet standing. They occur during
> transitions** — turning to look behind while walking down a hallway, pivoting at the top of a staircase,
> stepping sideways around furniture, turning a corner while carrying a bag that shifts the centre of mass
> unpredictably."

**The supermarket example** `[26:36]`–`[27:38]`: head turns left, body rotates right around the trolley
handle, visual scene shifts, vestibular system detects angular acceleration, **and the base of support is
itself shifting as the feet reposition.**

> "**The turn fails because the sensory integration required exceeded the degraded system's capacity.** The
> sensors delivered noisy data. The processor needed additional milliseconds. **During those milliseconds
> the centre of mass continued moving outside the base of support without correction**" `[27:38]`.

> "**No common exercise trains this computation.** Static balance holds the head still, eliminating the
> vestibulo-visual conflict. Walking moves forward with the head forward — no angular acceleration from the
> semicircular canals" `[27:38]`.

## The independence rule

`[20:26]`–`[21:28]` — **the organising principle for practice:**

> "**The recalibration is specific to the backward direction. It does not transfer to lateral or rotational
> correction because each directional program operates on its own cerebellar data set.**"

> "**Backward walking reloads the backward program. Lateral taps reload the lateral program. Head-turn
> walking reloads the rotational program. Each direction must be trained independently because each
> direction is computed independently.**"

## Safety instruction — stated by the video

`[27:38]`–`[28:38]`:

> "**Anyone with active vertigo, a recent vestibular disorder, or a fall history in the past 6 months should
> perform all balance exercises near a sturdy support surface — counter, wall, or chair — and progress only
> when each level is stable for two consecutive weeks. Exercises producing dizziness should be stopped and
> discussed with a physician before resuming.**"

## Practices

1. **Train all four directions independently** — training one does not transfer to the others.
2. **Backward walking** — even three or four steps along a kitchen counter, daily. **Highest yield, because
   the gap between muscle readiness and program readiness is widest here.** Measurable improvement within
   two weeks.
3. **Lateral taps** — the frontal plane, which walking never challenges reactively and which predicts falls
   most strongly.
4. **Head-turn walking / looking over the shoulder during lateral taps** — the rotational integration that
   no standard exercise trains and where real falls actually occur.
5. **Use single-leg stance as the platform for directional work**, not as the work itself.
6. **Near a counter, wall or chair; progress only after two stable weeks; stop anything that produces
   dizziness and discuss it with a physician.**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Falling despite regular strength training** `[00:00]`.
- **Sideways wobble, lateral sway, hip drop** — the mediolateral instability that predicts falls `[14:19]`.
- **Stumbling during turns, pivots, and transitions rather than during straight walking** `[25:35]`.
- Feeling unsteady when turning the head while moving `[26:36]`.
- Needing conscious attention to stability during ordinary movement `[13:17]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Base of support** — the polygon between the feet and the ground `[04:03]`.
- **Centre of mass** — at ~60% of height, near the second sacral vertebra `[06:07]`.
- **Reactive window** — the ~200 ms available to correct `[05:04]`.
- **Sagittal / frontal / transverse planes** — the four directional programs `[08:09]`.
- **Tonic stabilisation versus reactive correction** — different activation, motor units, timing and data
  sets `[14:19]`.
- **Cerebellar error signal** — prediction minus measured outcome `[20:26]`.
- **Purkinje cells** — the integrating cells thinning 2.5% per decade `[05:04]`, `[24:33]`.
- **Semicircular canals** — the angular acceleration sensors whose hair cells do not regenerate `[23:32]`.
