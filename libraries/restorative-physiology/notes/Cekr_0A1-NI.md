# What Happens Inside YOUR Muscles When Potassium Drops

## Central claim

**The 3 a.m. cramp and the weakness on the stairs are one ion at two stages of the same depletion — and the
symptoms reverse halfway down** `[00:00]`–`[01:01]`.

> "**The muscles that cramp at mild potassium deficiency go silent at severe deficiency.** The symptoms
> reverse halfway through the decline. **Cramps first, weakness second. Same ion, opposite failures**"
> `[01:01]`.

> "**Without sodium, the cell has no power. Without potassium, the cell has no rest**" `[25:24]`.

**This video is explicitly the second half of a pair** `[01:01]`: the sodium video covered the three sodium
ions pumped out per cycle and the action potential riding on that gradient. **This covers the two potassium
ions pulled in, and the resting voltage they maintain.**

## What potassium does between signals

`[01:01]`–`[04:06]`

**A skeletal muscle action potential lasts ~2 milliseconds. After it passes, the cell must return to
resting voltage before it can fire again — and potassium is how it gets back.**

**Potassium leak channels** are **always open regardless of voltage**, allowing continuous outflow along the
concentration gradient.

> "**Without the leak, the cell would not repolarise. Without repolarisation, no subsequent signal could
> fire**" `[03:04]`.

| Compartment | Concentration |
|---|---|
| **Inside the cell** | **~140 mEq/L** |
| **Outside** | **~4 mEq/L** |
| **Ratio** | **35:1** |

**The Nernst equation converts that ratio directly into a voltage: approximately −90 mV for potassium
alone.** Because the membrane is also slightly permeable to other ions, **the actual resting potential
settles near −70 mV** — a weighted average via the Goldman equation — **but potassium dominates**, since
the leak channels make the membrane far more permeable to it than to anything else at rest.

### The thermostat

`[04:06]`–`[05:06]`

> "**The resting potential is the set point. Potassium leak channels are the mechanism that enforces it.
> When potassium drops, the voltage drifts, the set point moves**… **the muscle becomes progressively
> easier to fire because the gap between where the voltage rests and where the voltage triggers has
> narrowed.**"

## Stage one — mild deficiency, and the cramp

`[05:06]`–`[09:10]`

**Serum 3.0–3.5 mEq/L.**

| Measure | Normal | Mild deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| Extracellular potassium | 4 | **3** |
| Ratio | 35:1 | **~47:1** |
| **Resting potential** | **−70 mV** | **drifts toward −60 mV** |

**Why 10 mV matters** `[05:06]`:
> "**The gap between the resting potential and the firing threshold was only 15 millivolts to begin with.
> Ten millivolts of drift eliminates two-thirds of the safety margin.**"

### The three-state channel

`[05:06]`–`[06:07]` *(attributed to Hille, *Ion Channels of Excitable Membranes*)* — **the concept the whole
video turns on:**

| State | Description |
|---|---|
| **Closed** | **Ready** — waiting at resting potential for the voltage to reach threshold |
| **Open** | **Firing** — sodium rushing in |
| **Inactivated** | **Locked** — shut from the inside after opening, **and unable to reopen until the membrane returns to a sufficiently negative resting potential** |

**At mild deficiency, channels open with smaller stimuli than they should.**

> "Muscle cells that normally require a full nerve impulse to contract begin firing in response to **minor
> mechanical stretching, slight temperature changes, small shifts in blood flow**, or spontaneous
> fluctuations in membrane voltage that would not trigger firing at normal levels. **The cell has become a
> hair trigger**" `[06:07]`.

*(Clinical progression attributed to Gennari, *New England Journal of Medicine* — mild hypokalemia
dominated by muscle irritability, cramps, twitching and fasciculations.)*

### The 3 a.m. cramp, explained

`[07:08]`–`[08:09]`

> "**The muscle fired because its resting potential had drifted close enough to threshold that a normal
> nocturnal shift in posture, blood flow, or temperature provided the small additional depolarisation
> needed to trigger a sustained contraction.**"

**Why it eventually releases** `[08:09]`: "the contraction itself restored enough local ion flux to
temporarily reset the voltage."

**Why it hurts the next day** `[08:09]`: "tissue damage from minutes of involuntary maximal contraction —
**the muscle fibres having fired at full force without the gradual recruitment pattern a voluntary
contraction uses.**"

## Stage two — severe deficiency, and the reversal

`[09:10]`–`[13:15]` — **the paradox, and the video names it as one you could not predict from stage one.**

**Serum below 2.5 mEq/L.** Ratio shifts toward **56:1 or higher**; resting potential drifts past −60 mV
toward **−55 or −50 mV.**

**At first, worse of the same:** the cell sits so close to threshold that **sodium channels begin opening
spontaneously, producing action potentials with no external stimulus.**

**Then the failure inverts** `[10:11]`–`[11:11]`:

> A channel fires and its **inactivation gate** swings closed, locking it. **Normally it recovers when the
> membrane repolarises fully — "the negative voltage resetting the inactivation gate."**

> **But the membrane can no longer get there.** "The potassium leak channels that would normally pull the
> voltage back **are working against a concentration gradient that is too small to generate enough outward
> current.** The membrane voltage stalls at −50 or −55 mV, **a voltage too depolarised to reset the
> inactivation gate.**"

> "**The channel fires once and locks. It cannot reopen because the voltage required to unlock it is no
> longer achievable at the current potassium concentration**" `[11:11]`.

| Deficiency | Channel behaviour | Symptom |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | **Fires too easily** — resting voltage drifted toward threshold | **Cramps, twitching, fasciculations** |
| **Severe** | **Cannot fire at all** — the inactivation gate is locked and cannot be reset | **Weakness, paralysis** |

### The two states that look identical from outside

`[12:13]` — **the sharpest point in the video:**

> "**The locked state looks identical to the ready state from the outside because neither one is conducting
> current, but they are completely different at the molecular level.** The ready state is a channel waiting
> for a signal. The locked state is a channel that **cannot** fire until the voltage resets to a level the
> depleted potassium can no longer provide."

> "**Weakness and rest look identical at the surface of the muscle. At the channel level, they are opposite
> conditions.**"

**And both can happen in one day** `[12:13]`–`[13:15]`: potassium fluctuates with meals, exercise, sweating
and circadian rhythm, so "**the cramp at 3 in the morning and the weakness at 3 in the afternoon may
represent two readings of one concentration measured at different points in the daily fluctuation.**"

## The heart — where the same drift becomes dangerous

`[13:15]`–`[18:19]`

| Tissue | Action potential duration |
|---|---|
| **Skeletal muscle** | **~2 ms** |
| **Cardiac muscle** | **~300 ms — 150 times longer** |

**Why the cardiac one is long:** a **plateau phase** during which **inward calcium current holds the
contraction while outward potassium current gradually repolarises the cell.** The balance sets how long the
cell stays contracted.

**When extracellular potassium drops** `[14:16]`–`[15:17]`:
> "**The outward potassium current weakens** because fewer potassium ions are available to carry positive
> charge out during the plateau. **The balance tips toward calcium. The plateau extends. The cell takes
> longer to return to rest.**"

**On the ECG:** the repolarisation interval lengthens, the main repolarisation wave **flattens and
broadens**, and **a small extra deflection appears after it** — **the U wave.**

> "**Visible only during potassium depletion, absent from every normal recording**… **as specific to
> hypokalemia as any finding in clinical medicine. When potassium is restored, the wave disappears**"
> `[15:17]`.

### The vulnerable window

`[16:17]`–`[17:18]` *(attributed to Weiss and colleagues, *Circulation Research*)*

**Prolonged repolarisation creates a window in which a premature stimulus can initiate a reentrant
circuit.** The mechanism is **spatial**:

> "Some regions have recovered and returned to the resting state, while adjacent regions are still in the
> plateau phase… **A premature impulse encounters a mosaic of recovered and refractory tissue.** The impulse
> propagates through the recovered regions, curves around the refractory regions, and by the time it
> completes the circuit, the previously refractory tissue has recovered and conducts the returning wave."

> "**A spiral of depolarisation rotates through the ventricle**, each rotation producing a contraction too
> weak and too fast to pump blood effectively."

> **What it may feel like** `[16:17]`: "**The palpitation you feel when the rhythm stumbles, the flutter in
> the chest that arrives without exertion**, may be the heart encountering the vulnerable window the
> potassium deficit created." **It can self-terminate — or accelerate into fibrillation.**

> "**Hypokalemia kills through the heart more often than through the skeletal muscles**" `[17:18]`.

*(Clinical significance attributed to Palmer and Clegg, *New England Journal of Medicine* — the changes are
dose-dependent, progressive, and **fully reversible when potassium is restored.**)*

## Thiazides — one drug, both substrates

`[17:18]`–`[20:21]`

**The mechanism** `[17:18]`–`[18:19]`: the thiazide blocks sodium reabsorption in the early distal nephron,
so **more sodium arrives at the collecting duct.** Channels there absorb the excess sodium, and **the
resulting electrical gradient drives potassium secretion outward into the urine.**

> "**More sodium arriving at the collecting duct means more potassium excreted alongside it**… **One drug
> depletes both substrates of the pump simultaneously through coupled transport**" `[18:19]`.

> "Sodium depleted from the power supply side, potassium depleted from the resting voltage side. **Both arms
> of the gradient compressed by one medication, producing symptoms from both halves of the pump**"
> `[19:20]`.

**The symptom list to notice** `[19:20]`–`[20:21]`: "The fatigue that arrives without exertion, the cramps
that arrive without exercise, the weakness that arrives without inactivity, **the palpitations that arrive
without anxiety** — each symptom has a voltage explanation."

**Clinical routing, and it is specific and constructive** `[18:19]`, `[20:21]`:
> "**Potassium monitoring through regular blood tests is standard clinical practice for every patient on
> thiazides.**"

> "**If you are taking a thiazide and experiencing cramps, weakness, fatigue, or palpitations, the physics
> of what the medication does to the potassium gradient is worth discussing with the physician who
> prescribed it.**"

## The banana arithmetic

`[20:21]`–`[22:22]` — **and it demolishes the standard advice.**

| | Potassium |
|---|---|
| **One banana** | **~420 mg** |
| **Daily adequate intake, women** | **2,600 mg** |
| **Daily adequate intake, men** | **3,400 mg** |
| **Coverage from one banana** | **12–16%** |

> "**Seven to eight bananas per day to meet the requirement through bananas alone.** The food most
> associated with potassium… **provides one-eighth of what the body requires**" `[20:21]`.

**Better sources, per 100 g:**

| Food | Potassium |
|---|---|
| **White beans** | **~560 mg** |
| **Baked potato with skin** | **~535 mg** |
| **Salmon** | **~490 mg** |
| **Avocado** | **~485 mg** |
| **Sweet potato** | **~475 mg** |
| **Cooked spinach** | **~466 mg** |

> **The real point** `[21:21]`: these "are consumed in quantities and combinations that contribute more
> meaningfully to the daily total. **A baked potato with skin contains more potassium than a banana and is
> more likely to appear as a full serving in a meal.**"

**Whole versus processed** `[21:21]`–`[22:22]`: **98% of the body's potassium sits inside cells.** Whole
foods deliver it **"inside the cellular package the gut evolved to absorb."** Foods that have been **ground,
pressed, heated in water or chemically treated lose potassium into processing water before reaching you.**

> "**The difference between a baked potato and a bag of potato crisps is partly a potassium story**"
> `[22:22]`.

## Why it must be replaced every day

`[22:22]`–`[25:24]` — **the asymmetry with sodium.**

| Ion | Renal conservation during deficiency |
|---|---|
| **Sodium** | **Near 100% reabsorption** — urinary sodium falls to nearly zero via the aldosterone cascade. **"The kidney fights for sodium with everything it has"** |
| **Potassium** | **Obligatory loss of ~10–15 mEq/day continues even during severe deficiency** — because the secretion is driven by an electrical gradient tied to sodium reabsorption, **which the kidney will not reduce below what blood volume requires** |

> "**Sodium has a vault with a sealed door. Potassium has a net with holes that cannot close completely**"
> `[23:23]`.

| Stop eating | Timeline to depletion |
|---|---|
| **Sodium** | **Slow** — the kidney clamps excretion within hours |
| **Potassium** | **Days to weeks** — excretion continues even at zero intake |

**The triple drain** `[24:23]`:
> "**A week of low potassium eating, combined with sweating from exercise or summer heat, and a thiazide
> prescription pulling potassium through the kidney, produces a triple drain** that can move the serum
> concentration from the safe range into the symptomatic range faster than most people expect. **The cramp
> that arrives at the end of that week carries the arithmetic of every day that preceded it.**"

## Practices

1. **Treat potassium as a daily requirement, not a weekly average** — the kidney cannot hoard it the way it
   hoards sodium.
2. **Stop relying on bananas.** One covers 12–16% of the daily target. **Beans, baked potato with skin,
   spinach, sweet potato, avocado and salmon** all deliver comparable or greater amounts per serving in
   forms eaten in meaningful quantities.
3. **Prefer whole foods with intact cellular structure** — processing leaches potassium out before it
   reaches the plate.
4. **Watch the triple-drain weeks** — low intake plus heavy sweating plus a potassium-wasting diuretic.
5. **If on a thiazide with cramps, weakness, fatigue or palpitations, raise it with the prescriber.**
   Regular potassium monitoring is standard practice on these medications.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Potassium leak channels** — always-open pores that set the resting voltage `[02:03]`.
- **Nernst equation** — converting a concentration ratio into a voltage `[03:04]`.
- **Goldman equation** — the weighted average across all permeant ions `[03:04]`.
- **Threshold gap** — the ~15 mV between rest and firing `[05:06]`.
- **Three-state sodium channel** — closed/ready, open/firing, inactivated/locked `[05:06]`.
- **Inactivation gate** — the intracellular structure that locks the channel `[10:11]`.
- **Plateau phase** — the sustained cardiac depolarisation `[14:16]`.
- **U wave** — the ECG deflection specific to hypokalemia `[15:17]`.
- **Reentrant circuit** — a rotating spiral of depolarisation `[16:17]`.
- **Obligatory renal loss** — potassium excreted regardless of status `[23:23]`.
