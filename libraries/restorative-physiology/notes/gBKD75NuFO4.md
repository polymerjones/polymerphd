# Why YOUR Knee and Back Hurt at the Same Time

## Central claim

**Two symptoms, two specialists, one unmeasured input.** The knee pain and the back pain ascend from the
same source through a traceable five-joint chain — and neither examiner looked at the shoe `[00:00]`,
`[18:24]`.

> "The orthopedist looked at the knee, the physiotherapist looked at the back. Both were correct in what
> they found. **Neither looked down**" `[17:23]`.

## The measurement nobody takes

`[00:00]`–`[02:01]`

**Heel-toe drop:** place the shoe on a flat surface, measure thickness at the heel, measure at the toe,
subtract. **Ten seconds.**

| Footwear | Typical drop |
|---|---|
| Running shoes | **~12 mm** (10–15 range) |
| Work boots | 15–20 mm |
| Dress shoes | **20–40 mm** |

> "You have been standing on a wedge for 50 years… **The flat shoe you believed was flat carries a
> heel-toe drop that you never measured because the geometry is invisible from above**" `[01:00]`.

**The chain in one sentence:** the wedge tilted the ankle → the tilted ankle changed the knee → the
changed knee shifted the pelvis → the shifted pelvis deepened the lumbar curve. **Five joints altered by
an elevation smaller than a fingertip width.**

## The ankle — where it starts

`[02:01]`–`[05:07]`

**A raised heel holds the ankle at roughly 85–87° instead of a neutral 90** — a plantarflexion of 3–5°,
"small enough that you have never noticed it, sustained for every minute you wear shoes."

**The calf complex sits chronically shortened, and tissue adapts to the position it is held in.** Collagen
in the Achilles rearranges to that length.

> The ankle that should provide **15–20° of dorsiflexion** now provides **5–10**. "The ankle lost half its
> upward range because the shoe held it in a shortened position for 30 years" `[03:03]`.

### Two self-tests

**1. The barefoot squat** `[03:03]`: stand barefoot on a flat surface, keep both heels down, squat fully.
**If your heels lift before your thighs reach parallel, dorsiflexion is restricted.** Most adults over 50
who have worn standard shoes cannot achieve a flat-footed squat.

**2. The kneeling dorsiflexion test** `[04:04]`–`[05:07]` — more precise:
- Kneel on one knee, other foot flat in front, **push the knee forward over the toes keeping the heel
  flat.** Measure how far the knee travels past the toes.

| Result | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **10 cm past the toes** | Adequate |
| **Under 5 cm** | Significant restriction |
| **0 cm — heel lifts immediately** | Severe adaptive shortening, "likely driving compensatory mechanics at the knee and above for years" |

**Which muscle is responsible** `[04:04]`: repeat with the knee bent. **If dorsiflexion improves, the
gastrocnemius is the primary restrictor** (it crosses the knee and slackens when the knee flexes). **If it
stays restricted, the soleus is also involved** (it sits below the knee, so knee position does not change
its length). "The test tells you which muscle the shoe shortened and which stretch protocol targets the
correct tissue."

### Why the restriction forces a compensation
`[05:07]`–`[06:08]`: walking requires the tibia to rotate forward over the planted ankle — that rotation
**is** dorsiflexion, and the stride needs 10–15°. With only 5° available, "the stride cannot complete
through the ankle," so the body reaches the foot further forward using knee extension and hip flexion.

> "**The compensation is involuntary**… You walk normally from your perspective. The force plate and the
> gait analysis camera see the compensation."

## Knee loading pattern 1 — impact

`[06:08]`–`[11:15]`

**The mechanism:** the compensatory stride extension lands the foot **ahead of the body's centre of
mass** — heel strike, with the leg extended, the quadriceps inactive, and **zero muscular cushioning.**

**The impact transient:** a sharp force spike lasting roughly **50 milliseconds**, rising to **1.0–1.5
times body weight** — arriving **before the muscles can activate to absorb it.** For an 80 kg person,
800–1,200 N transmitted through the heel up the tibia to the knee.

**Why cartilage specifically suffers** `[07:10]`–`[08:11]`: articular cartilage is about **70% water** held
in a collagen and proteoglycan matrix. Under compression water is squeezed out, absorbing force; during
unloading, osmotic pressure draws it back.

> "When the loading exceeds the range, **the water is squeezed out faster than the osmotic pressure can
> draw it back**, and the matrix dehydrates progressively, losing the hydraulic cushion that protects the
> underlying bone."

Dehydrated cartilage is stiffer and transmits more force to the bone beneath, which thickens in response
— **"visible on the X-ray years after the cartilage loading pattern began."**

**Why the inside of the knee** `[08:11]`–`[09:12]`: the heel-strike force vector passes **medial to the
knee's mechanical axis**, creating a **varus moment** that concentrates **60–70% of the load on the medial
compartment.** Every step produces the same bias because the geometry is the same every time.

> "The medial compartment thins while the lateral compartment remains relatively preserved… **the early
> pattern of medial compartment osteoarthritis**, the most common form of knee arthritis in the 60-plus
> population."

**The arithmetic** `[09:12]`–`[10:13]`: ~1,200 heel strikes per mile × 3 miles daily × 365 days ≈ **1.3
million impacts per year, 13 million per decade** on the medial compartment.

**And the failure mode is fatigue, not wear** `[10:13]`: no single step exceeds the cartilage's acute
strength, but millions of cycles propagate cumulative damage. **"The X-ray that looks fine but the knee
that hurts is cartilage compression fatigue below imaging resolution."**

> "The cartilage degrades **not because it wears out from friction**, but because the loading pattern
> exceeds the biological maintenance capacity of a tissue that depends on measured compression for its
> survival" `[10:13]`.

*(Impact transient absent from forefoot and midfoot strike — attributed to Lieberman and colleagues,
*Nature*.)*

**The reframe** `[11:15]`: **"The cartilage was fatigued. The knee was not old. The knee was loaded."**

## Knee loading pattern 2 — hyperextension

`[11:15]`–`[13:16]` — **the same ankle restriction loading the opposite side of the joint.**

When the tibia cannot rotate forward, it **drifts backward relative to the femur during stance**,
producing **3–5° of hyperextension** past neutral.

**Two surfaces loaded simultaneously:**

| Structure | Consequence | Symptom |
|---|---|---|
| **Posterior capsule** (back of the knee) | Stretches under chronic hyperextension | **Dull ache behind the knee during prolonged standing** |
| **Patellofemoral joint** (kneecap on the femoral groove) | Compresses as altered mechanics pull the kneecap tighter | **Sharp pain under the kneecap on stairs** |

**And it accumulates with standing time** `[12:15]`: "A job that requires 4 hours of standing per day
produces 4 hours of chronic hyperextension loading on both surfaces daily, for years."

> "**Two different knee complaints, one ankle restriction.** If you report both… you are describing two
> consequences of one mechanical compensation" `[13:16]`.

*(Patellofemoral contact pressure mechanics attributed to Powers, *Journal of Orthopaedic and Sports
Physical Therapy*.)*

## The pelvis and the lumbar spine

`[13:16]`–`[17:23]`

**The elevated heel shifts the centre of mass forward** — "standing on a 3–5° downhill slope
perpetually." To avoid falling forward, **the pelvis tilts anteriorly by 4–8°.**

**A visible self-check** `[14:18]`: stand sideways at a mirror in shoes, then barefoot. **The change in the
lower back curve is visible** — the belly protruding slightly more in shoes is the anterior tilt; the
lower back hollowing more is the lordosis deepening. "The postural change is immediate and reversible."

**The geometry** `[14:18]`–`[15:20]`: **each degree of anterior pelvic tilt produces roughly one degree of
additional lumbar lordosis**, concentrated at **L4–L5 and L5–S1** where the curve is sharpest.

**Why that matters** `[15:20]`–`[16:21]`: increased lordosis compresses the **facet joints** — small paired
joints at the back of each vertebral segment, **designed for intermittent contact during occasional
extension, now loaded continuously.** Their cartilage is only **1–2 mm thick, versus 3–5 mm at the knee**,
so it tolerates less chronic compression before the bone beneath begins remodelling.

**L4–L5 and L5–S1 are named as the two most common sites of non-discogenic lower back pain.**

### The diagnostic clue
`[16:21]` — **genuinely useful pattern recognition:**

> "**If your back aches specifically after standing** — not sitting, not walking, but standing still for
> 15 to 20 minutes — the positional specificity is the diagnostic clue."

| Position | Facet loading |
|---|---|
| **Standing still** | **Loaded** under the lordotic posture |
| Walking | Reduced — dynamic motion cycles the contact |
| Sitting | **Eliminated** — the lordotic posture is gone |

> "The MRI that shows normal discs but the back that still hurts after standing **may be facet compression
> from the postural shift the heel drop produced.**"

**And the contrast with disc pain** `[17:23]`: disc pain is a **flexion** problem — the nucleus pushing
backward when the spine bends forward. **Facet pain is an extension problem.** "Opposite mechanism, same
lumbar segments, both traceable to mechanical causes you can modify."

*(Heel elevation and facet contact pressure attributed to Kerrigan and colleagues, *American Journal of
Physical Medicine*; facet loading mechanics to McGill.)*

## Why nobody catches it

`[17:23]`–`[19:25]`

> "The orthopedist ordering the knee X-ray does not measure heel-toe drop. The physiotherapist assessing
> lumbar mobility does not test ankle dorsiflexion. The podiatrist evaluating the foot does not examine
> the lumbar spine. **Each specialist examines their segment of the chain. The chain connects all five
> segments through the drop none of them measured.**"

**This resolves a specific frustration** — "the sense that the knee and the back are somehow connected but
no one has explained how."

## The transition protocol

`[19:25]`–`[22:26]` — **and the timeline is the important part.**

**Why abrupt change causes injury:** a tendon remodelled to a shortened position, suddenly asked to
sustain full dorsiflexion under body weight, bears forces at angles the tissue cannot accommodate.
**Achilles tendinopathy is a documented risk of sudden minimalist transition in adults over 50.**

### The schedule — 8 to 12 weeks

| Step | Duration |
|---|---|
| Current drop → **8 mm** | 2–3 weeks |
| 8 mm → **4 mm** | 2–3 weeks |
| 4 mm → **0** | — |

**At each step, monitor:** the Achilles for tenderness, the calf for excessive tightness, and **the
dorsiflexion test for the increase that confirms the tissue is lengthening.**

**Supplement with daily calf stretching** — **sustained holds of 30–60 seconds, not bouncing** — "allowing
the tissue to creep to its new length under gentle sustained tension."

### Why 12 weeks and not 6
`[20:26]`–`[21:26]` — **the best detail in the video:**

| Tissue | Adaptation time | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Muscle** | **4–6 weeks** | Myofibrillar protein turns over with a half-life of ~7–15 days, so sarcomeres are added relatively quickly |
| **Tendon** | **8–12 weeks** | Mature cross-linked collagen turns over far more slowly, replaced at a rate measured in months |

> "**The tendon feels ready at week six because the muscle has adapted. The collagen has not yet
> remodelled**, and the 12-week protocol respects the tendon's slower biology" `[21:26]`.

**Remodelling proceeds at roughly 1–2% of total tendon length per week** under sustained new-length
loading. **"Faster transitions exceed the remodelling rate and produce injury."**

**And the chain unwinds in reverse** `[22:26]`: "Each additional degree of dorsiflexion the ankle recovers
is a degree of compensatory hyperextension the knee no longer needs to produce, a degree of medial impact
the stride no longer generates, a degree of anterior tilt the pelvis no longer requires, **a degree of
facet compression the lumbar spine no longer absorbs.**"

## What changes when dorsiflexion returns

`[22:26]`–`[24:30]`

**The stride shortens naturally** and the foot lands under the centre of mass rather than ahead of it —
**midfoot rather than heel strike**, with the ankle in slight plantarflexion, the knee in slight flexion,
the hip in slight flexion. **The whole lower limb preloaded for eccentric deceleration.**

| | Heel strike | Midfoot strike |
|---|---|---|
| Deceleration window | **~50 ms** | **~200 ms — four times longer** |
| Absorbed by | **Passive cartilage** | **Three muscle groups eccentrically** — calf at the ankle, quadriceps at the knee, glutes at the hip |
| Peak impact force | Baseline | **30–50% lower** |

> "The impact is distributed across **active tissue that can absorb, adapt, and recover**, rather than
> concentrated on passive cartilage that can only compress and slowly rebound" `[24:30]`.

**Downstream:** medial compartment loading decreases, patellofemoral compression decreases, posterior
capsule stretch resolves, anterior tilt reduces, lumbar facet loading decreases.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Heel-toe drop** — heel thickness minus toe thickness `[01:00]`.
- **Dorsiflexion** — shin-toward-toes motion, needed for stride `[03:03]`.
- **Impact transient** — the ~50 ms force spike at heel strike `[07:10]`.
- **Varus moment** — the force vector driving load to the inner knee `[08:11]`.
- **Medial compartment** — the inner knee, taking 60–70% of heel strike load `[08:11]`.
- **Anterior pelvic tilt** — front of the pelvis dropping, back rising `[14:18]`.
- **Lumbar lordosis** — the inward curve of the lower back `[15:20]`.
- **Facet joints** — paired posterior spinal joints, cartilage only 1–2 mm thick `[15:20]`.
