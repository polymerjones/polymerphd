# Why people mistake feelings for moral insight | Alex O'Connor

## Central claim

**On emotivism, saying "murder is wrong" doesn't add a fact to "a murder occurred" — it only adds
an expression of the speaker's attitude toward it, meaning moral language is closer to an
exclamation than a claim that can be true or false.** `[23:26]` "If you try to isolate what the
actual difference is [between 'a murder just occurred' and 'it's wrong that that murder just
occurred'], I think it has to just be an attitude. It's an expression... the only thing that can be
added by saying that something is wrong is the expression of that attitude."

## Emotivism vs. subjectivism: expressing, not reporting

`[01:01]`–`[02:03]` O'Connor draws a precise distinction often collapsed in casual conversation:
**subjectivism** treats "murder is wrong" as equivalent to "I don't like murder" — a psychological
report that could be true or false (the speaker could even be lying about their own preference).
**Emotivism** is different in kind: it holds the statement is **literally** an expression of
attitude, "the difference between telling you I don't like murder and literally going, 'Ugh,
murder'" — not a claim with truth value at all, in the same category as a command or an
involuntary "ow."

## Most "moral" debate is actually factual debate

`[04:05]`–`[08:11]` O'Connor's central practical claim: a large share of apparent moral
disagreement (he uses gun policy and speed limits as examples) is really disagreement over
**checkable descriptive facts** — death-rate statistics, causal claims — layered under a shared
moral reaction ("innocent people dying: boo") that both sides actually hold. Once the facts are
settled, he argues, much of the apparent moral conflict dissolves, though he's explicit this doesn't
eliminate the harder case of genuine, foundational value conflict: **"it might genuinely be that if
you actually go deep enough into people's moral emotions, they will ultimately at root be identical
across the human species"** — an optimistic possibility he flags as unprovable, since "that
experiment has not and probably cannot ever be done."

## The incest taboo as a test case

`[14:15]`–`[17:20]` O'Connor uses the near-universal incest taboo to press on whether "wrongness"
is anything beyond disgust. He notes the taboo has a ready **evolutionary explanation**
(inbreeding risk) but is explicit that "evolutionary explanations aren't a justification" — the
same move that would explain, not justify, other evolved biases. Pressing secular ethicists on why
incest (with all confounds like disability risk or power imbalance controlled for) would still be
wrong, he reports most eventually land on **disgust** as the only remaining answer: "we just think
it's disgusting... that's all you've got ultimately when you break it down."

## The strongest objection: the Frege-Geach embedding problem

`[26:28]`–`[30:31]` O'Connor presents what he calls the biggest problem for emotivism directly and
fairly: moral claims embedded in larger logical structures ("I wonder if murder is wrong," or a
syllogism — "if murder is wrong, then murdering James is wrong; murder is wrong; therefore
murdering James is wrong") behave like they have stable truth value, which a pure expression of
emotion shouldn't be able to do. **"That conclusion follows from those premises... but that would
require us being able to conceive of premise two, murder is wrong, as being something which is
true or false"** — a problem O'Connor states he doesn't resolve in this video, pointing instead to
Simon Blackburn's work as the place to go deeper.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Strong moral certainty accompanied by visceral disgust or revulsion ("that's despicable")** —
  treated as evidence for the emotivist claim that the wrongness *is* the feeling, not a separate
  fact the feeling is merely reacting to. `[13:14]`
- **Feeling that a moral debate is intractable or "just people shouting past each other"** —
  reframed as often being a disguised factual disagreement, with a shared underlying moral reaction
  once the facts are sorted out. `[07:10]`
- **A partner's factual-sounding complaint ("you're 10 minutes late") that is really an emotional
  expression in disguise** — used as an everyday, non-philosophical parallel to how moral language
  works. `[20:22]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Emotivism** — the theory that ethical statements are literal expressions of emotion rather than
  true-or-false claims. `[00:00]`
- **Non-cognitivism** — the broader category holding that ethical statements lack truth value;
  emotivism and prescriptivism (ethical statements as disguised commands) are both examples.
  `[03:04]`
- **The Frege-Geach (embedding) problem** — the objection that emotive statements can't
  consistently keep their meaning once embedded in conditionals or syllogisms, which seem to
  require the embedded statement to have a stable truth value. `[26:28]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- "Boo, murder" (A.J. Ayer's own phrase) — the clearest compact illustration of what an emotivist
  claims is actually happening when someone says an act is wrong. `[02:03]`
- Being sad it's raining, then happy on learning it wasn't actually raining — used to show that an
  emotional state can shift purely from new factual information, without the emotion itself ever
  having had a truth value. `[09:12]`
- A partner's disguised complaint ("oh, I thought we could get dinner together") as an emotional
  expression wearing "descriptive clothing" — extended directly to how moral language works.
  `[20:22]`

## Source-stated confidence

O'Connor presents emotivism as a specific, named philosophical position (A.J. Ayer, *Language,
Truth, and Logic*) situated within a broader academic debate (Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism as a
rival non-cognitivist view), and is explicit that logical positivism — the framework that
originally produced emotivism — has "completely fallen out of favor" among philosophers, even
though he still finds emotivism itself compelling. He presents the Frege-Geach problem as a
genuine, unresolved difficulty for his own view rather than something he has an answer to, which
is a notably even-handed hedge for an advocate to include.

## Conflicts with other sources

Connects directly to Massimo Pigliucci's Stoicism videos in this library (`V3_EAZd0-rw` and,
per other forks writing concurrently in this session, `NOH48U5nxt0`/`4EmuBcIlWBs`): the Stoic
"discipline of assent" — using rational judgment to evaluate one's own impressions before acting on
them — is functionally a practical answer to the problem this video raises philosophically, that a
strong emotional/moral reaction is not automatically a reliable signal of truth. Where Pigliucci
treats this as a practice to cultivate, O'Connor treats it as a metaethical fact about what moral
language actually means — the two are compatible framings of a similar underlying caution against
mistaking feeling for insight.
