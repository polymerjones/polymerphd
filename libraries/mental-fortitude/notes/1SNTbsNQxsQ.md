# 12 Things Stoics Do Every Morning (That 95% of People Ignore) | STOICISM

## Central claim

**A deliberate, twelve-part morning practice — not motivation or feeling — is what determines whether a person actually shows up to their own life rather than postponing it indefinitely.** Framed via Seneca: **"You are living as if you have 10,000 years left... You are postponing. You are delaying. You are half showing up to your own life and calling it a busy schedule"** `[00:01]`. The video's closing frames the entire practice as one repeated question: **"Who am I going to be today? That question, asked honestly and answered with action, is the whole practice"** `[34:46]`.

## One: rise with the intention to live well

`[03:04]`–`[06:09]` — **Marcus Aurelius** is quoted from his *Meditations*: **"You are getting up to do the work of a human being, not the work of habit, not the work of reputation"** `[04:04]`. The source states this isn't about feeling motivated — **"The Stoic practice doesn't ask how you feel. It asks what you've decided"** — and prescribes a single silent sentence before getting up: "Today, I choose to live well" `[05:07]`.

## Two: give thanks for the gift of another day

`[06:09]`–`[08:12]` — Distinguished explicitly from gratitude-journal listing: **"Stoic gratitude is closer to a reckoning... that you almost didn't have this morning"** `[06:09]`. The Greek term **eucharistia** is introduced as "closer to noticing, really noticing" than simple thankfulness `[07:10]`. The source states this practice explicitly does not require denying difficulty: **"They hold both, the difficulty and the gift, in the same breath"** `[07:10]`.

## Three: anticipate the trials the day may bring

`[08:12]`–`[11:17]` — **Premeditatio malorum** is introduced with a specific mechanism claim: an unanticipated hardship delivers "two hits" — the difficulty itself, and the shock of it not matching expectations — and the practice removes the second `[09:13]`. **Seneca** is quoted (paraphrased): the wise person anticipates hardship "not to dwell in it, but to meet it first in imagination where it costs nothing" `[09:13]`. The prescribed practice is naming one realistic difficulty and asking "who do I want to be if that happens" — explicitly "rehearsing character, not outcomes" `[10:15]`.

## Four: concern yourself only with what is within your control

`[11:17]`–`[13:18]` — **Epictetus's** dichotomy of control is introduced with an explicit correction: **"Focus on what you can control sounds... like permission to stop caring... That is the opposite of what Epictetus meant"** `[11:17]`. The morning practice is framed as active sorting, not resignation: putting each item of the day into "genuinely up to you" or "not yours," which the source claims frees up energy otherwise spent "gripping something that was never in your hand" `[12:18]`.

## Five: commit yourself to virtue above all else

`[13:18]`–`[15:20]` — Four virtues are named — **wisdom, justice, courage, and self-discipline** — as "daily operating principles," not aspirational ideals `[13:18]`. The claim is that skipping this commitment produces small, cumulative compromises across a day ("you say the comfortable thing instead of the honest thing") that leave "a vague sense of having drifted" by evening `[14:20]`. The prescribed question: "where will I need courage? Where will honesty cost me something?" `[15:20]`.

## Six: envision the person you ought to be

`[15:20]`–`[18:26]` — A worked example (James, in logistics management) illustrates a four-minute morning practice: picturing the day's end and asking what would need to happen to "feel like I actually showed up" `[16:23]`. This is explicitly distinguished from outcome-based visualization: **"Most visualization is about winning. Stoic envisioning is about being"** — rehearsing "staying grounded," "patient," "honest," rather than a successful result `[17:25]`.

## Seven: prepare your mind for the imperfections of others

`[18:26]`–`[20:27]` — **Marcus Aurelius** is quoted: **"Today I will encounter people who are meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and antisocial"** `[18:26]`, reasoned as ignorance rather than malice. The source is explicit about what this isn't: **"This is not a technique for becoming passive... It removes the shock that compounds irritation into suffering"** `[19:26]`, framing the result as **equanimity** — "someone who can't be thrown off because they were never off balance to begin with" `[20:27]`.

## Eight: sit quietly and bring order to your thoughts

`[20:27]`–`[22:31]` — The Greek term **hesychia** ("inner quiet") is introduced as "not empty, not detached, just ordered, clear" `[20:27]`. The prescribed practice is five minutes of sitting without reaching for a phone or content, "let[ting] whatever thoughts are present be present... without immediately trying to solve them" `[21:29]`.

## Nine: seek guidance from wisdom each morning

`[22:31]`–`[25:34]` — **Seneca** is quoted (paraphrased, to Lucilius): "gather and save the thoughts that have helped you in adversity and apply them over and over in practice until they become... instincts you live from" `[23:32]`. The claim is explicitly about repetition, not insight: **"through repetition, not through one powerful insight... until they stop being ideas and start being you"** `[24:33]`.

## Ten: set your duties in order before the day begins

`[25:34]`–`[27:36]` — An explicit distinction is drawn between a **task list** ("about output") and **duty-based thinking** ("starts with the roles you hold... and asks what does each... genuinely require of me today") `[25:34]`. **Seneca** is quoted (paraphrased): people are "extraordinarily busy with things that don't matter and neglectful of things that do" `[27:36]`.

## Eleven: remember the fragility of life

`[27:36]`–`[30:40]` — **Memento mori** is framed explicitly against a misreading: **"not as morbid theater, not as existential dread, but as the cleanest, sharpest tool... for cutting through the nonsense of daily life"** `[27:36]`. **Epictetus** is cited (paraphrased): people "act as if they have 10,000 years ahead of them... treat the irreplaceable as though it can be replaced" `[28:37]`. The reframed question is explicitly not "how much time do I have left" but **"given the time I have, what becomes non-negotiable today?"** `[29:39]`.

## Twelve: step forward and act with discipline

`[30:40]`–`[32:45]` — The source states the preceding eleven practices "mean nothing if you stay in your head": **"Stoicism is not a contemplative tradition. It's an action tradition that uses contemplation as preparation"** `[30:40]`. Discipline is explicitly redefined against a common misconception: **"They think it's something you feel... The Stoics would say that's exactly wrong. Discipline... is a relationship between your values and your actions"** `[31:43]`. **Seneca** is quoted: **"It is not because things are difficult that we do not dare. It is because we do not dare that things become difficult"** `[31:43]`.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **The vague, low-level anxiety of trying to control things that by nature can't be controlled** — named directly as "gripping something that was never in your hand," resolved by sorting practice four `[12:18]`.
- **The shock and disproportionate irritation of being treated badly by someone during the day** — attributed to not having anticipated imperfect behavior in advance, addressed by practice seven `[19:26]`.
- **Discomfort with unfilled silence, reaching for a phone within seconds of quiet** — named directly and addressed by the hesychia practice `[20:27]`.
- **A vague evening sense of "having drifted" or been "slightly less than you could have been"** — attributed to skipping the morning virtue commitment, not a character flaw `[14:20]`.
- **Waiting for motivation before acting** — explicitly named as backwards: "when they don't feel it, they decide today isn't the day," corrected by defining discipline as independent of feeling `[31:43]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Eucharistia** — an ancient Greek term for gratitude as active noticing of existence, rather than a passive positive feeling or checklist `[07:10]`.
- **Hesychia** — inner quiet as an ordered, clear mind at peace with its own contents, not an empty or detached one `[20:27]`.
- **The "second hit"** — the shock and disorientation added on top of a hardship itself when it wasn't anticipated in advance, which premeditatio malorum is framed as removing `[09:13]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Clearing a workspace before doing careful work** — used to frame morning stillness as maintenance, not decoration `[21:29]`.
- **Gripping something that was never in your hand** — used to describe the wasted energy of trying to control what the dichotomy of control places outside one's power `[12:18]`.
