# Thinking too logically can actually hold you back | Dan Shipper

## Central claim

**Rationalism — the demand that true knowledge be explicit and rule-based — has been extraordinarily
successful in physics and engineering but has systematically devalued intuition as a real form of
knowing, and the recent success of neural networks (which learn patterns without any inspectable
list of explicit rules, much like human intuition does) is evidence that intuitive knowledge was
real all along, not a placeholder for rules we hadn't found yet.** `[19:20]` "That way of thinking
about the world has been very successful for us. But it also kind of blinded us to... how important
that idea that stories and personal hands-on experience give us a way of knowing about things that
we may not be able to explicitly talk about but we still know just as much as we know things that
we can explicitly say."

## The Socrates–Protagoras origin point

`[02:01]`–`[04:04]` Shipper traces rationalism's birth to Plato's dialogue *Protagoras*, a debate
over whether excellence (often translated "virtue") can be taught. Protagoras argues everyone has
the capacity for excellence and explains this through myth and story; Socrates demands an explicit,
non-contradictory definition instead, and — in Plato's telling — dismantles Protagoras for failing
to produce one, with the implication that an inability to define something explicitly means not
truly knowing it. Shipper frames this moment as setting Western thought on a specific path: **"to
identify knowledge, the ability to know something, with whether or not you can really clearly
define it."**

## Where rationalism worked, and where it consistently didn't

`[06:08]`–`[08:10]` The explicit, rule-based approach is credited with essentially all of modern
technology — "smartphones... computers... vaccines... every appliance in your house." But Shipper
names specific domains where the same approach has stalled: psychology, mid a "gigantic replication
crisis" despite a century of research; economics; and — his central case — early artificial
intelligence, which as a field has never produced a single "universal agreed-upon definition" of
its own subject matter.

## Why symbolic AI (explicit rules) broke down

`[10:11]`–`[13:14]` Using the concrete example of email spam filtering, Shipper walks through how
any explicit rule ("if it says 'emergency,' prioritize it") immediately generates exceptions that
require further rules ("...but only from coworkers"), which generate further exceptions ("...but
not coworkers abusing the privilege"), in a spiral that never terminates: **"anytime you try to
create rules to define these things, you always run up against exceptions... it's just too big of
a project."** Early "expert systems" applying this approach to medical diagnosis hit the same wall
— unable to reach 100% explicit accuracy even for something as bounded as "does this person have
measles."

## Neural networks as intuition made technical

`[14:16]`–`[17:18]` Neural networks are presented as the alternative: instead of explicit rules,
they learn to recognize patterns from many examples, and afterward **"you can't go and look inside
of a neural network and find... the entire set of rules that it has"** — no more than a
neuroscientist could locate a discrete rule list by examining a human brain. Shipper draws the
direct parallel: **"human intuition is also trained by thousands and thousands and thousands of
hours of direct experience"** and operates the same inexplicit way — which is why, he argues,
neural networks are "the first technology we've ever invented that works a lot like human
intuition."

## Rationality as emerging from intuition, not replacing it

`[18:19]`–`[19:20]` Shipper's stated resolution isn't to reject rationalism but to reposition it:
**"you can think of rationality as emerging out of intuition. So... we have this sort of squishy
inexplicit intuitive way of understanding what's going on in the world, and our rational thought
comes out of that... but you sort of need both."** He connects this back to Protagoras — arguing
that stories, myth, and lived experience are legitimate ways of knowing that the Socratic demand for
explicit definition made invisible, and that this loop closes only once a technology (neural
networks) began embodying that intuitive mode of knowing itself.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Dismissing a "gut feeling" or developed intuition (about a person, a tool, a situation) as
  inferior to an explicit, statable reason** — directly challenged as undervaluing a real form of
  knowledge. `[20:21]`–`[21:21]`
- **Frustration at being unable to fully explain or justify a correct intuitive judgment** —
  reframed as a limitation of explicit language, not evidence the judgment itself is unfounded.
  `[21:21]`
- **Wanting to "get really precise" and unemotional when thinking through a personal decision** —
  named as a specific, familiar impulse rooted in the broader rationalist tradition, not a neutral
  default. `[01:01]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Symbolic AI** — the original approach to artificial intelligence, built on explicit,
  hand-written logical rules, distinguished from later neural-network approaches. `[09:11]`
- **General problem solver** — an early (pre-computer, originally executed by hand) symbolic AI
  system built by Herbert Simon and Allen Newell, cited as an early success that broke down as
  problems grew more complex. `[09:11]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- Freud's steam-engine model of the mind, followed by the 20th century's computer model — used to
  show how the dominant technology of an era becomes the era's default metaphor for how minds
  (and legitimate thought) are supposed to work. `[17:18]`
- Trying to look inside a neural network for its "rules" compared to trying to find a discrete rule
  list by examining a human brain under a microscope — neither yields an inspectable list, even
  though both systems clearly "know" things. `[16:17]`–`[17:18]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as Shipper's own synthesis of intellectual history (Socrates, Descartes, Newton, Galileo)
and his professional experience building AI products, delivered as confident argument rather than
