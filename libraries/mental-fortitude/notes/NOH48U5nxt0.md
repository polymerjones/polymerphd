# Stoicism has been wildly misinterpreted. Here's the ancient truth: | Massimo Pigliucci

## Central claim

**Popular "little-s stoicism" — emotional suppression, masculine stiff-upper-lip endurance,
detachment from people and things, anti-pleasure asceticism, passive doormat-ism — is a distortion
of "capital-S Stoicism," the actual ancient philosophy, and each distortion traces back to a real
Stoic idea taken out of context or stripped of its nuance.** `[00:00]` "A misconception about
stoicism is that it's about cultivating an attitude of detachment from things and people. It turns
people into doormats... But that's stoicism with the little S, which is not in fact what real
stoicism with a capital S... is about."

## The "broification" of Stoicism, and why the etymology argument fails

`[10:15]`–`[13:20]` Pigliucci names a specific modern distortion he calls **"the broification of
stoicism"** — an interpretation "on the darker side of things" that "tends to emphasize
masculinity," partly justified by the claim that "virtue" derives from Latin *vir* (man). He
rebuts this directly: **"the original was Greek because stoicism ultimately is a Greek philosophy,
not a Roman one. And the original word in Greek is *arete*... it just means excellence. It doesn't
mean manly anything."** He further cites the ancient Stoics themselves as unusually explicit for
their era that "women are rational human beings just like men," attested in the writings of both
Epictetus and Seneca, and frames Stoic justice as treating "any other people, regardless of gender,
race, nationality... with dignity."

## Life hacks versus a coherent philosophy: the Buddhism and CBT analogies

`[13:20]`–`[17:27]` Pigliucci names two specific failure modes for how people misuse Stoicism: **"stoicism by quotations"** (decontextualized quotes, sometimes even misattributed — he notes people quoting Epictetus alongside a picture of Epicurus, "who was most definitely not a stoic"), and treating Stoicism **"as a series of life hacks as opposed to a coherent philosophy of life."** He draws a direct parallel to Buddhism — most practitioners don't actually meditate, yet meditation isn't what makes someone Buddhist; the four noble truths are — and to cognitive behavioral therapy, which he says did originate from Stoic techniques but isn't itself a philosophy of life: **"you can be a very efficient practitioner of cognitive behavioral therapy... and still be a psychopath... That would not make you a stoic."**

## Endurance and emotion, correctly framed

`[08:13]`–`[10:15]` Pigliucci concedes the "grain of truth" behind the stiff-upper-lip caricature: **"endurance is in fact a stoic value"** for things genuinely outside one's control. But he rejects the idea that Stoicism suppresses emotion outright: **"there is no such thing as suppressing emotions... the only way to suppress your emotions is to literally cut off a part of your brain."** The actual Stoic practice, he says, is *modulating* emotion through reason — redirecting destructive anger into "a sense of justice," not eliminating feeling.

## Attachment without indifference: the inn, and figs in winter

`[21:28]`–`[25:35]` Against the misconception that Stoicism demands detachment from people, Pigliucci relays Epictetus's analogy of living **"as if you were visiting an inn or a hotel"** — the room, the lamp, the bed are genuinely yours to use and enjoy while you're there, but you know you're passing through and will eventually check out, "gracefully or ungraciously." He pairs this with Epictetus's **"figs in winter"** story (a student mourning a departed friend is told he is "pining for figs in winter... something that is not possible because it's not the right season") and connects it to his own father's death from cancer: **"I still went on my life as if my father was going to be there forever... I regret that. But I learned this lesson"** — using it since to be actively present with people while they're here.

## Not a doormat: Cato the Younger and the Stoic Opposition

`[25:35]`–`[31:41]` Pigliucci counters the "quietist doormat" misconception with a historical account: a group of Roman senators known as **"the Stoic Opposition"** actively resisted the tyranny of Nero, Vespasian, and Domitian, some at the cost of their lives. His central example is **Cato the Younger**, who raised an army against Julius Caesar's advancing tyranny and, when defeat was certain, took his own life rather than be used by Caesar as a political trophy — described in graphic detail, including Cato tearing open his own wound after a doctor tried to save him. Pigliucci frames this as "inspiration to modern stoics... not necessarily... pick up weapons," but as a mandate to "speak out" and defend "personal autonomy and the autonomy of other people" when they're threatened.

## Revising Stoicism itself: rejecting pantheism and providence

`[32:42]`–`[42:54]` In the video's most personal section, Pigliucci argues Stoicism must adapt as knowledge advances — a position he attributes to Seneca directly ("the people that came before us are not our masters. They're just our teachers"). He identifies ancient Stoic **pantheism** (the universe as a single rational, God-equivalent organism) and its associated concept of **providence** as beautiful but untenable: "I don't think of the cosmos as a living organism." He traces the ancient argument for it — an argument from design, comparing the universe's apparent order to a sword fitting its scabbard — and states that both David Hume's philosophical critique and Charles Darwin's evolutionary alternative make that argument indefensible today. His personal replacement is a "neutral forces" view: accepting death and hardship as natural without believing they serve a cosmic plan, while still permitting emotional distress about them — a stance he distinguishes from Epictetus's own more radical claim that a Stoic understanding one's mortality "should not be disturbed" even by the death of a spouse or child.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Reflexively suppressing or hiding emotional reactions to seem strong** — corrected directly: "there is no such thing as suppressing emotions... that will turn you into a psychopath." `[09:14]`
- **Escalating anger after being insulted or criticized** — Stoic emotional modulation is offered as the alternative to both suppression and unchecked reaction, redirecting the underlying impulse (e.g. toward "a sense of justice") rather than eliminating it. `[09:14]`
- **Taking people for granted until they're gone, then regretting it** — named directly via Pigliucci's own account of his father's death, resolved by internalizing the "figs in winter" lesson to actively savor relationships in season. `[24:32]`
- **Distress about death or loss** — explicitly validated as reasonable and not something a modern Stoic (in Pigliucci's own revised view) needs to eliminate, contra the ancient providence-based reading. `[38:48]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Arete** — the Greek term (not the Latin *vir*) that "virtue" actually translates, meaning
  excellence in a gender-neutral sense. `[11:17]`
- **Preferred indifferents** — the Stoic technical term for things (wealth, health, reputation)
  worth preferring when available but that do not determine one's character. `[19:28]`
- **The Stoic Opposition** — the group of Roman senators and Stoic philosophers who actively
  resisted the emperors Nero, Vespasian, and Domitian. `[26:37]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Visiting an inn** (Epictetus) — enjoying what's temporarily yours (people, possessions)
  without gripping it, since you're only passing through. `[21:28]`
- **Figs in winter** (Epictetus) — wanting something out of its proper season, reframed as a
  lesson to savor what's available while it's actually available. `[23:31]`
- **A sword fitting its scabbard** — the ancient argument-from-design analogy for cosmic order,
  which Pigliucci presents and then rejects via Hume and Darwin. `[40:50]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented by a named philosopher of science and evolutionary biologist (co-author of *Beyond
Stoicism*) with a clear, explicit distinction throughout between claims he treats as textually
and historically supported (the gender-inclusive writings of Epictetus and Seneca, the existence
of the Stoic Opposition) and his own personal philosophical position (the rejection of pantheism
and providence), which he flags directly as his own departure from the ancient texts rather than
a claim about what Stoicism "really" says.
