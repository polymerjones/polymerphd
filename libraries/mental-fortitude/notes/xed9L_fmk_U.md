# 4 Stoic Pillars That Fix 99% of Your Problems (Beginner Guide) - RESET YOUR LIFE | STOICISM

## Central claim

**Most suffering comes not from what happens but from how it is judged, and the four Stoic virtues — wisdom, justice, courage, and temperance — are presented as a single connected system for handling that judgment, each one incomplete without the other three.** Stated directly via Epictetus: **"it's not things that disturb us, but our judgments about things... that one idea right there is the seed that all four pillars grow from"** `[03:07]`. The video's closing image makes the interdependence explicit: **"four legs of a table. Remove one and everything becomes unstable regardless of how solid the other three are"** `[29:32]`.

## Pillar one: wisdom

`[04:08]`–`[09:13]` — **Epictetus** is quoted from the opening of the Enchiridion: **"Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion. Things not in our control are body, reputation, and whatever are not our own actions"** `[05:08]`. **Marcus Aurelius** is added: **"If someone is able to show me that what I think or do is not right, I will happily change"** `[08:12]`, offered as evidence that staying correctable is part of wisdom, not just knowing the control dichotomy. The prescribed practice: writing every current worry into two columns, "in my control" and "not in my control," and treating only the first as an actionable list `[06:09]`–`[07:11]`.

## Pillar two: justice

`[09:13]`–`[16:22]` — **Marcus Aurelius** is quoted: **"What injures the hive injures the bee"** `[10:14]`, read as meaning that treating people as tools for advancement damages the person doing it, not just the people used. The video draws a distinction between responding "from wounded ego" (to make someone feel what you felt) versus "from principle" (asking what the situation actually requires) `[12:16]`. A second Marcus Aurelius quote, his morning rehearsal for dealing with difficult people — **"the people I deal with today will be meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, but I have seen the beauty of good... and have recognized that the wrongdoer has a nature related to my own"** `[13:18]` — is offered as preparation without excusing bad behavior. Three concrete tests are given: honesty even when costly, fairness toward people who can't benefit you, and justice toward yourself (not building an identity around being wronged) `[14:19]`–`[15:21]`.

## Pillar three: courage

`[16:22]`–`[24:31]` — **Seneca** is quoted: **"It is not because things are difficult that we do not dare. It is because we do not dare that things are difficult"** `[18:24]`. The video splits courage into **external** (visible acts, having the hard conversation) and **internal** (examining one's own motives honestly, "not for anyone else, just because it's true") `[19:27]`, citing Marcus Aurelius: **"Never esteem anything as of advantage to you that will make you break your word or lose your self-respect"** `[19:27]`. Two practices are named: **premeditatio malorum**, calmly rehearsing difficulty in advance so it is "met prepared rather than cold" `[20:28]`–`[21:29]`, and deliberately choosing the harder of two small daily options to train decision-making "the same logic" as training a muscle `[21:29]`. Closing quote: Marcus Aurelius, **"it is not death that a man should fear, but he should fear never beginning to live"** `[22:29]`.

## Pillar four: temperance

`[24:31]`–`[32:38]` — Named via the Greek **sophrosyne**, translated as **"soundness of mind, balance, proportion"** `[25:31]`, explicitly distinguished from restriction or deprivation: **"temperance to the Stoics was never about removing good things from your life. It was about making sure good things... don't quietly start running your life"** `[24:31]`. **Seneca** is quoted: **"he is a wise man who does not grieve for the things which he has not, but rejoices for those which he has"** `[25:31]`. **Marcus Aurelius**: **"confine yourself to the present"** `[27:32]`. **Epictetus** closes the section: **"no man is free who is not master of himself"** `[31:36]`. The prescribed practice: when a strong desire arises, pause to simply observe it rather than immediately acting on or suppressing it, creating "a small gap between impulse and action" `[29:32]`, alongside negative visualization — briefly imagining something currently taken for granted as gone, to recalibrate what is actually valued `[30:35]`.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Free-floating anxiety with no clear target** — reframed as energy misdirected toward the "not in my control" column, addressed by writing it out and sorting it `[06:09]`–`[07:11]`.
- **The urge to retaliate or "win" after being wronged** — reframed as a choice between responding from wounded ego versus from principle `[12:16]`.
- **A specific avoided conversation, decision, or truth "quietly draining energy" in the background** — named directly as the target of the courage pillar `[16:22]`–`[17:23]`.
- **Feeling "out of control," compulsively reaching for food, distraction, or validation** — addressed directly as the specific problem temperance is designed to solve `[31:36]`.
- **A vague sense of always living "somewhere ahead of yourself," never satisfied by what's already present** — named explicitly and tied to temperance rather than to a goal or achievement problem `[26:32]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Premeditatio malorum** — calm, deliberate advance rehearsal of a difficulty, meant to produce preparation rather than anxiety `[20:28]`.
- **Sophrosyne** — the Greek term the video gives for temperance: "soundness of mind, balance, proportion," an examined sense of enough rather than self-denial `[25:31]`.
- **Eudaimonia** — the stated result of holding all four virtues together, translated as "flourishing" rather than simple happiness `[33:38]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Pouring a limited daily water supply into soil that can grow something, versus into concrete where it disappears** — used to frame energy spent on what's outside one's control as wasted `[06:09]`.
- **Fog versus a list** — writing worries down doesn't remove them but turns an unmanageable fog into a manageable, sortable list `[07:11]`.
- **Four legs of a table** — the four virtues as interdependent, not separable; removing one destabilizes the rest regardless of how solid they individually are `[29:32]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Built entirely on named quotations from Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius, and Seneca, presented as established fact with no citation of translation or original text, consistent with this channel's typical register. The video does distinguish its own interpretive claims from direct quotation reasonably clearly (e.g., explicitly flagging when it is offering "a completely different reading" of a commonly misunderstood term like temperance). Nothing is presented as uncertain or hedged; the register throughout is instructional and declarative.

## Conflicts with other sources

The closing Marcus Aurelius quote here, **"waste no more time arguing about what a good man should be. Be one"** `[36:42]`, is explicitly attributed to Marcus Aurelius's private journal in this video — which resolves an ambiguity in `uU_0euGi3mk`[25:28], where the same line is introduced only as "the Stoics had a phrase for this" with no named source. The same quotation is reused extensively elsewhere in this library (e.g. `jtAwm8GeXc8`, `rQ02NsGW8mk`, `McjSdV4985Y`, `Lsdm9VkWhaQ`, `LVtZGAFDRjc`), consistent with it being one of this channel's most frequently reused lines.

Pillar one's Epictetus dichotomy-of-control quote `[05:08]` is close to word-for-word the same translation used in `Lsdm9VkWhaQ`[21:37] ("of things, some are in our power and others are not. In our power, our opinion, motivation, desire, aversion, not in our power, our body, reputation, command") — the two videos independently draw on the identical Enchiridion translation.

The Greek term for temperance is given here as **sophrosyne**, attributed generally to "the Stoics" `[25:31]`, while `r_7Wh5At8Xw`[22:36] attributes the same term specifically to Aristotle. This is not a strict contradiction — sophrosyne predates and is shared across Greek philosophical schools — but the two videos differ on whose term it originally was, a distinction neither video itself acknowledges.
