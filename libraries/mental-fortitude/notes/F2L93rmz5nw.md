# The invisible archetype: being physically raised but emotionally abandoned | Nicole LePera

## Central claim

**Trauma, as LePera redefines it, is not primarily about the objective severity of an event but
about whether a child had support to process it — making it far more pervasive than the popular
image of trauma as catastrophic events, and explaining why even a "loving" household with
inconsistent emotional attunement produces the same adult coping patterns as more overt neglect.**
`[05:09]` "I think very few of us were raised with the emotional attunement that we needed... what
we mostly lacked, the large majority of us, is the attunement that our nervous system needed to be
able to cope with big events and even small daily events."

## Same event, different support, different outcome

`[02:03]`–`[03:05]` LePera's illustrating example: two children experiencing the same parental
divorce have "drastically different" outcomes depending only on whether a parent was present to
say "I understand what's happening... this isn't your fault" and allow the child's grief, versus a
child left "to process their grief completely alone." A second example — a distracted parent
telling a bullied child to "just get over it" — is used to show how repeated small, undramatic
moments (not one big event) teach a child "that they are alone in their emotions."

## The six archetypes of childhood trauma

`[10:13]`–`[15:17]` Six parent patterns, each with a stated adult consequence and healing
direction:

| Archetype (parent pattern) | Adult consequence | Healing direction (as stated) |
|---|---|---|
| Denies your reality ("you're overreacting," "too sensitive") | Second-guessing instincts, tolerating what doesn't feel right, not speaking up | Reconnect with instincts; stand up for yourself |
| Doesn't see or hear you (physically present, emotionally absent) | Not speaking up in groups, feeling unheard even when you do speak | Validate your own experience; seek relationships that value your perspective |
| Lives through / molds you (love conditional on performance) | Overworking, perfectionism, avoiding even mild criticism | Embrace imperfection; disconnect worth from output |
| Models no boundaries (relies on the child for emotional support) | Overextending in relationships, guilt over having own needs | Identify and hold boundaries; separate your responsibility from others' |
| Overly focused on appearance | Tying self-worth to how you look; pursuing goals for appearance's sake | Separate worth from appearance; recognize inherent worth |
| Can't regulate their own emotions (erratic, explosive-to-shut-down) | Hypervigilance, overreactivity, difficulty navigating own emotional reactions | Notice flooding; pause and calm the body before responding |

LePera names the last two — a parent who doesn't see/hear you, and a parent who can't regulate
their own emotions — as **the most common and widespread** of the six. `[15:17]`

## Coping versus healing, made concrete

`[18:22]`–`[19:22]` The distinction is illustrated directly: **"say for instance you're in an
argument with your partner. Coping might look like leaving the room, shutting down, or going
silent, where healing might look like noticing the panic rising in your chest, your breath
quickening... slowing down your breathing so that you can... teach yourself a new outcome that
conflict doesn't necessarily mean disconnection or rejection."** LePera adds that healing generally
requires relationship, not solitary work: **"most of our wounds were formed in relationships. So,
to truly heal, we need to be in relationship."**

## A direct message to parents

`[20:22]`–`[22:24]` LePera closes by addressing parents specifically, naming the difficulty of the
role and extending compassion for parents' own unresolved conditioning ("you, too, were impacted by
the parenting that you have had"), while stating the cycle nonetheless "gets carried on" absent new
tools. The practical advice given is to build regulation capacity (sleep, support, a pause before
entering a dysregulated child's room) **outside** of high-stress moments, since attempting to
self-regulate for the first time under pressure "sometimes we quite literally can't."

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Repeating a habit or relationship pattern that clearly doesn't serve you, without knowing
  why** — named as unresolved childhood trauma continuing to drive present behavior. `[06:09]`
- **Feeling "out of character" or unsure who you are in certain situations** — named as a listed
  sign of unaddressed childhood-trauma patterning. `[06:09]`
- **Becoming distant or unable to articulate feelings to a partner, then being told "I don't know
  what you're thinking"** — named as dissociation (a childhood-protective pattern) sabotaging adult
  intimacy. `[08:12]`
- **Feeling anxious or over-responsible when someone else is having an emotion** — named as
  "over-functioning," traced to having had to manage a household's emotional climate as a child.
  `[09:13]`
- **Noticing panic rising in the chest and breath quickening during conflict** — named specifically
  as the physical signal to intervene on with breath regulation, distinguishing healing from mere
  coping. `[19:22]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Coping (as distinguished from healing)** — a strategy that reduces momentary discomfort (e.g.
  leaving the room during conflict) without changing the underlying nervous-system pattern.
  `[18:22]`
- **The six archetypes of childhood trauma** — LePera's own named framework (see table above), not
  a standard clinical diagnostic category. `[10:13]`
