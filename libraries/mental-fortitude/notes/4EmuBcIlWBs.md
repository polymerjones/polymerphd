# 5 Stoic lessons to become your best self in 2026 | Massimo Pigliucci

## Central claim

**Stoic technique is conceptually simple — reframe your thinking before your emotions follow,
journal on your actions, sort what's in your control from what isn't, pause before acting on
instinctive impressions, evaluate your intentions rather than outcomes — and the entire difficulty,
and the entire value, is in repeated practice rather than additional understanding.** `[35:32]`
"This is just too simple. Some things are up to me, other things are not up to me. Yeah, sure,
everybody knows that. So what?... The issue is not that things are simple or not. The issue is are
you going to act on those things?"

## Lesson one: reason and emotion are neurologically fused, not opposed

`[01:02]`–`[07:05]` Pigliucci opens by rejecting the "reason versus emotion" framing directly,
citing the amygdala and frontal/parietal lobes as "massively interconnected" by billions of
neuronal fibers: **"there is no such a thing as an emotional response independent of the way you
think."** The worked example is an insult: rather than escalating ("how dare this person"), the
Stoic move is cognitive reframing first — **"an insult is nothing. It's just air moving around...
either that person is right... or... wrong. In which case that's his problem, not mine"** —
with the claim that emotional de-escalation follows the reframe rather than requiring separate
effort.

## Lesson two: philosophical journaling, modeled on Marcus Aurelius

`[07:05]`–`[14:12]` Distinguished explicitly from a diary: **"it's a journal of reflection,"** with
Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* — never intended for publication — offered as the model, written
"repetitive" and "preachy" because Marcus was talking to himself about recurring problems (his own
anger management). The prescribed practice: pick one incident, describe it as objectively as
possible in the second person ("you did this"), then answer three questions — **"what did I do
wrong? What did I do right? And what could I do next time?"** — explicitly not for "regret and
self-flagellation" but to prepare for the high likelihood that a similar situation (a difficult
colleague, say) will recur.

## Lesson three: the dichotomy of control, and its link to the serenity prayer

`[14:12]`–`[21:22]` Using a job-interview example, Pigliucci walks through building a literal
two-column list — "up to me" versus "not up to me" — noting most concrete outcomes (getting the
job, sleeping well the night before, arriving on time) land in the *not-up-to-me* column, while the
underlying *intentions* (preparing carefully, trying to sleep, trying to leave on time) are what's
actually controllable. He extends the same method to societal-scale anxiety (climate change: voting
and donating are "up to me"; solving the problem is not) and draws an explicit historical line to
the **serenity prayer**, tracing Epictetus's up-to-us/not-up-to-us teaching through medieval
Christian monks' use of his manual as a spiritual-exercise source.

## Lesson four: the discipline of assent — pausing on impressions

`[23:23]`–`[29:26]` Epictetus's faculty of judgment/will — the transcript's caption renders the
Greek term as **"prohiresis"** at this point (almost certainly the standard term *prohairesis*,
though that exact spelling doesn't appear in the available captions) — is named as the most
important human faculty. The mechanism is an **"impression"** — an instinctive judgment fused to a sensory
experience, illustrated with seeing gelato on the way to dinner and instinctively judging it "good
to go by and eat." The trained response is to "talk to your impression" before acting on it. This
extends into evaluating actions by **intention rather than outcome**: volunteering at a soup
kitchen is virtuous if the motivation is genuine care for others, but not virtuous — even though
still outwardly helpful — if the real motivation is padding a résumé, **"because now I'm using
other people as a means to an end."**

## Lesson five: living according to nature — ethics as medicine, and widening the circle

`[29:26]`–`[35:32]` "Living according to nature" is explicitly not about literal wilderness living;
it means studying what makes the human organism flourish, the same way medicine studies the body.
The specific analogy: a craving for sugar and fat is *natural* (evolutionarily adaptive in scarcity)
but not *good for you* in a food-abundant modern environment — and Pigliucci argues **anger is the
psychological equivalent**, a natural response to perceived injustice that is nonetheless
unhealthy to act on unreflectively. The remedy is reasoning through problems and prioritizing
social cooperation, extended via the second-century Stoic **Hierocles's** model of concentric
circles of concern (self, family, friends, acquaintances, humanity) — with the explicit practice of
deliberately shrinking the psychological distance between circles, e.g. addressing strangers as
"brother" or "sister."

## Practice over understanding: the gym analogy, and Pigliucci's own conversion

`[35:32]`–`[46:43]` Pigliucci compares Stoic technique to weightlifting: a trainer's instruction is
conceptually simple, but results come only from repeated practice, not from understanding the
instruction. He then narrates his own path to Stoicism — a midlife crisis compounded by his
father's death, an unexpected divorce, and a cross-country career move, during which he
unsuccessfully tried Buddhism ("it didn't quite click") before a chance tweet about "Stoic Week"
led him to Epictetus specifically: **"who is going to tell you what the right thing to do is...?
Your faculty of judgment... That did it. I almost heard the click."** He reports concrete personal
effects — reduced anger, and applying the dichotomy of control to a lifelong weight struggle by
accepting genetic/developmental factors while acting on diet and exercise, which "sunk in... in a
way that I never did before."

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Escalating anger or a rush of adrenaline in response to an insult** — addressed directly by
  the cognitive-reframing technique in lesson one. `[04:02]`
- **Anxiety about outcomes outside one's control** (a job interview, climate change, political
  events) — addressed by the dichotomy-of-control exercise, redirecting effort toward
  controllable intentions and equanimity toward the rest. `[16:14]`
- **Reflexively acting on an instinctive urge and regretting it afterward** — addressed by the
  discipline of assent, pausing on the "impression" before acting on it. `[25:24]`
- **A pattern of chronic anger** (Pigliucci's own, and Marcus Aurelius's, by his own account) —
  named as a natural but unhealthy response, addressed through repeated cognitive reframing rather
  than suppression. `[44:40]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- No confidently-spelled term is recorded here: Epictetus's term for the human faculty of
  judgment/will (the capacity to assess a situation and act on that assessment) appears in this
  transcript's captions as "prohiresis," "prohesis," and "prohyises" at different points — almost
  certainly auto-caption garbling of the standard Greek term *prohairesis*, but since that spelling
  is not itself present, it is not recorded as a confirmed source-introduced term. `[23:23]`
- **Impression** — a sensory experience combined with an immediate, instinctive judgment, which
  the discipline of assent trains a person to pause on rather than act on automatically. `[25:24]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Ethics as medicine** — natural impulses (sugar/fat cravings, anger) aren't automatically
  healthy ones, just as a doctor distinguishes a natural craving from a medically sound choice.
  `[30:29]`
- **Weightlifting** — a trainer's instruction is conceptually simple, but only repeated practice
  builds the "muscle"; used to explain why Stoic technique's simplicity doesn't make it easy.
  `[35:32]`
- **Hierocles's concentric circles** — self at the center, family, friends, acquaintances, and
  humanity as expanding rings, with the practice of deliberately pulling outer circles inward.
  `[33:30]`
