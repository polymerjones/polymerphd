# 02 — Stoic Techniques and Philosophy

The named, genuinely Stoic concepts and techniques that recur across this collection, gathered in
one place. See `05_CROSS_SOURCE_COMPARISON.md` for how much of this collection's content is *not*
Stoic despite its branding — this file covers what actually is.

---

## The dichotomy of control

Sorting anything a person is worried about into what is genuinely within their power (their own
judgments, effort, and choices) and what is not (other people's actions, outcomes, the past),
directing effort and preparation only toward the first category and practicing acceptance toward the
second. Several sources apply this as a literal daily written exercise — a two-column list applied
to a specific upcoming situation — rather than as an abstract attitude.

## The discipline of assent

Pausing between an "impression" (an automatic, pre-conscious judgment about something that just
happened) and granting that impression belief or turning it into action. Described across sources as
the specific mechanical location of freedom and self-control: not suppressing an emotional reaction,
but examining it before acting on it. Named Greek terms for pieces of this mechanism recur, with
inconsistent transliteration across different videos' auto-generated captions: the faculty of
judgment/choice (variously rendered, likely "prohairesis"), the automatic pre-judgment impression
itself ("phantasia"), curious non-judgmental self-observation ("prosoche"), and the mind's ruling,
interpreting faculty ("hegemonikon").

## Premeditatio malorum (premeditation of adversity)

Briefly and deliberately rehearsing a specific difficulty in advance — a hard conversation, a
setback, an unpleasant person — so that its arrival doesn't carry the additional shock of being
unanticipated. Multiple sources are explicit that this is meant to be brief and bounded, not an
extended anxious rehearsal, and distinguish it from the "disaster rehearsal" of ordinary worry, which
is treated as unproductive by contrast.

## Memento mori (remembering mortality)

Holding the fact of one's own eventual death as a daily, felt reference point rather than an
abstract fact, used across sources to clarify which present activities will matter in retrospect and
to make ordinary decisions feel less deferrable. Consistently reframed as clarifying rather than
morbid.

## The obstacle is the way

Treating whatever blocks a given path as itself the material that builds capability, rather than a
detour to route around — obstacles, difficulty, and friction are reframed as the actual mechanism of
growth rather than an interruption to it.

## Amor fati

Not merely tolerating whatever occurs but treating it as worth affirming — presented as a further
extension of the dichotomy of control, applied to events that have already happened and can no
longer be sorted into "within my power."

## Views on emotion: reframing, not suppression

A recurring, explicit distinction across sources: Stoic emotional technique is not about eliminating
or suppressing feeling, but about creating a pause before acting on it, and about examining the
judgment embedded in a reaction rather than treating the reaction as simple, direct perception of
reality. One source makes this point with an explicit neuroscience gloss (citing the amygdala and
prefrontal cortex as anatomically interconnected, not opposed), used to argue Stoic technique is
better described as cognitive reframing than as emotional suppression.

## The Stoics as sources of practical, repeatable technique — not abstract doctrine

A consistent structural pattern across this collection: sources treat the value of Stoic ideas as
located entirely in repeated practice, explicitly separate from how simple or already-familiar the
underlying idea sounds. More than one source names the gap between intellectually agreeing with an
idea and actually living according to it as the real difficulty — not a lack of new information.
