# 8 Years in the Wilderness with Miriam Lancewood, UYL Podcast Episode

## Central claim

**Miriam Lancewood recounts living eight years (from 2010) in the wild mountains of New Zealand's
South Island with her partner, hunting, gathering, and moving nomadically with no fixed camp, and
attributes to that lifestyle a set of self-reported changes: reawakened senses, reduced long-term
anxiety despite situational fear remaining, physical strength and fitness maintained without
formal exercise, and a repeatable roughly-two-week period of mental "slowing down" on first
entering that way of life.** `[05:07]`–`[06:08]` "We decided that... we would go and give all the
belongings away... and go with our backpack to live in the wilderness... I learned how to hunt with
a bow and arrow and we hunted... gathered and we collected the plants... we have done this since
2010."

## Daily life: nomadic, hunting-based, and unscheduled

`[09:10]`–`[12:14]` A typical day is described as: waking before sunrise, exploring new terrain
(since the couple stayed nomadic rather than settling), hunting on foot by scanning the landscape
for the color or movement of a hare or deer rather than active chasing, returning to camp to cook
and eat organ meat fresh, an afternoon walk following whichever stream or ridge looked interesting,
and sleeping when it got dark. `[12:14]`–`[13:16]` Lancewood explicitly ties this to circadian
rhythm: "we go to bed when it's dark and really sleep when it's dark and wake up when it's light,"
noting she wakes repeatedly on full-moon nights because of the added natural light.

## Senses "coming back": a stated but self-reported reversal

`[08:10]`–`[09:10]` Lancewood reports specific sensory changes she attributes to years of
hunting-dependent living: "my sense of smell is increased enormously because I need it for
hunting... my vision, looking in the far distance... my hearing has improved so much," extending
the claim to her older partner Peter as well. She frames city life as actively suppressing these
senses rather than the senses being lost: **"the city is very desensitizing, but the opposite
happens in nature."**

## The two-week adjustment, corroborated independently

`[14:17]`–`[16:19]` Lancewood describes an initial period of boredom and restlessness lasting "about
[two weeks]" before her mind's pace matched nature's, required "in order for the mind to calm down."
Notably, the podcast host (running an unrelated wilderness-immersion program) interjects that this
matches what they independently observed in their own students without having discussed it with
Lancewood beforehand: "I always say there's gonna be a two-week period where your mind is gonna
have to slow down... it seems to happen almost to each person without fail." This is two
independent (though both informal, non-controlled) sources converging on the same figure, which is
worth noting as stronger than either account alone, while still being anecdotal rather than
measured.

## Fear, anxiety, and an unnoticed cause: noise

`[19:22]`–`[20:24]` Lancewood distinguishes wilderness fear as short-term and situational (real
physical risk — a storm, a fall) from what she describes as the free-floating, long-term anxiety
she associates with city life, reporting that public speaking to large audiences (a new part of her
life after publishing a book) no longer triggers fear for her. `[37:42]`–`[38:42]` She traces one
specific, easy-to-miss cause of that anxiety to background noise, recounting noticing anxiety while
sitting in a field and tracing it to a distant person using a "whipper snipper" (string trimmer):
"this machine noise has bad effects on us and we're not aware of it because it's happening all the
time... I think it has really bad side effects on all our mental health."

## Spirituality: starting pragmatic, ending without a name for it

`[30:36]`–`[31:38]` Lancewood explicitly describes going into the wilderness expecting to
reconnect with animist/spiritual beliefs she grew up with (reincarnation, nature spirits), and
instead becoming "more and more pragmatic" and no longer believing in those specific frameworks —
while separately describing later experiences (she references one involving "connecting with a
plant") that she states plainly she has no vocabulary for: "it doesn't fit in their religion... I
haven't really got words for it." This is a clear, source-stated hedge that should travel with the
claim rather than be resolved into either "she found spirits" or "she found nothing."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Self-reported physical changes: increased strength, thicker hair ("one-third thicker"), and
maintained fitness without formal exercise, attributed to a wild-meat diet and daily walking with a
loaded pack. `[18:20]` Psychological: reduced long-term anxiety and fear, described as a side
effect rather than a deliberately trained outcome.

## Glossary terms introduced

None beyond descriptive phrases used informally in the conversation (e.g. "the art of doing
nothing"), which are conversational framing rather than defined technical terms.

## Analogies worth reusing

- Fear as behaviorally contagious "like sheep" passing panic to lambs, used to argue that humans
  retain the same instinct — "we're so conditioned to think, oh, I'm afraid, what am I afraid of...
  where does that come from? Oh, those people over there, they are afraid." `[33:40]`–`[34:41]`
- A described shared perceptual experience: both Lancewood and her partner reported the world
  looking "very gray and dull" while near a passing stranger, then switching back to normal once he
  left — offered explicitly as a strange, hard-to-explain anecdote about consciousness rather than
  a claimed general phenomenon. `[31:38]`–`[32:39]`

## Source-stated confidence

**Entirely first-person lived experience and self-report, not technique instruction or a
controlled study.** Physical claims (strength, hair thickness, fitness) are Lancewood's own
unverified self-observation. Sensory and psychological claims (heightened senses, reduced anxiety)
are subjective self-report with no measurement. The "two-week adjustment" claim is unusually
corroborated by a second, independent informal source (the podcast host's own program), which
raises its credibility above a single anecdote but still falls short of a measured or
peer-reviewed claim. The spirituality discussion is explicitly hedged by the speaker herself as
something she cannot name or fit into an existing framework — that hedge is preserved above rather
than resolved.

## Conflicts with other sources

Converges with `NlQE1zMJwns` ("What Traditional Sports Couldn't Teach Me") and `EmN2ON_VIXY` ("The
Next Evolution of MovNat") on the broader premise that modern civilization suppresses inherent
human capacities that a return to more natural conditions can restore — but this note is a
first-person "I actually lived this way for years" account, distinct in kind from those two videos'
coaching/organizational framing, and involves no MovNat-style structured technique or method.
`CIShtax3_nI` ("Behind The Scenes with Erwan Le Corre") similarly frames modern life as suppressing
an innate "birthright" capacity, which this account's sensory-reawakening claims independently
echo from a completely different, non-MovNat source — worth noting as two unrelated sources
converging on a similar idea, though neither is a controlled or measured claim.
