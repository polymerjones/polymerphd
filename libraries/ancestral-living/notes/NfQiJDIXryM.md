# 3 Natural Movements for Spinal Mobility

## Central claim

**Spinal mobility is trained here through three natural movements — rotation, flexion (rocking),
and extension (prone lying) — with the source's stated key principle being that consistent, light,
frequent practice combined with body awareness produces better results than infrequent, intense
stretching.** `[15:24]` "The key to success of mobility is more about frequency or consistency than
it is about intensity... lower the intensity, increase the frequency."

## Rotation: sitting elevated, reaching backward in sequence

`[00:00]`–`[06:08]` Sitting on an elevated bolster so the hips are at or above knee height keeps the
spine neutral (rather than already flexed) before rotating. The reach sequence is deliberate — head
turns first, then torso, then the arm — visualized as a segmented pole or ladder twisting evenly
along its length, "from the base of your skull to your lower back," rather than concentrating
rotation in the neck or upper back alone. The final rep of each side is held for 10 seconds using
the person's own muscular tension rather than pulling on the opposite knee, on the stated logic
that self-generated end-range strength is preferable to relying on external leverage.

## Flexion: rocking in a "C" shape

`[06:08]`–`[10:14]` The described key to smooth rocking is spinal flexion itself — "the more our
spine is like a C, the easier it is going to be to rock without needing to throw our arms and limbs
... around." The flexion sequence works upper back, then mid/lower back, then the hips, addressing
a stated common pattern where "the upper back will be rounded but the lower back will be tight,"
producing a jarring rather than smooth rock.

## Extension: prone lying, steering the stretch

`[10:14]`–`[14:23]` From a forearm-supported prone position, pressing up to the wrists while looking
up extends the spine. A diagnostic cue is offered: feeling the stretch concentrated in the lower
back or the neck is described as a sign the mid/upper back "isn't doing its job," and the
practitioner should back off and consciously redirect the extension there instead of pushing
further into the already-mobile segment.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

A common asymmetric stiffness pattern is named — upper back rounded/tight while the lower back is
also tight, producing a "crashing" jarring sensation during rocking rather than a smooth motion.

## Glossary terms introduced

None beyond the descriptive cues above (the "pole"/"ladder" visualization, the spinal "C" shape),
which are teaching metaphors rather than defined technical terms.

## Analogies worth reusing

- The spine visualized as a segmented pole or ladder, twisting or lengthening evenly along its
  length, used as the anchor visualization for the rotation drill. `[01:02]`–`[04:06]`
- A body "C" shape as the target form for smooth spinal rocking during the flexion drill.
