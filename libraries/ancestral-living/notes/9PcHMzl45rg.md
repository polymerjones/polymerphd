# The Power of the Get-Up

## Central claim

**Getting up off the floor without using the hands is presented as a movement-competency
assessment in its own right: because there are only a handful of ways to solve the "get up"
problem, which pattern a person defaults to — and which they avoid — reveals a specific mobility or
strength limitation without needing a full clinical test battery.** `[15:20]` "Movement is a
behavior designed to solve a problem. What better problem to solve than getting up off the floor to
see how well your participant moves?"

## The get-up as diagnostic: pattern avoidance as the signal

`[04:10]`–`[05:10]` The core logic: "when someone is getting off of the ground, they're going to
bias whatever they feel safe doing." If someone always rises via a split squat and avoids a full
squat pattern, the stated question becomes "why are you avoiding the squat" — pointing toward
ankle, hip, or knee mobility as the likely culprit, to be confirmed with a targeted screen rather
than assumed.

## Confirming the hypothesis: ankle and hip screens

`[06:11]`–`[09:14]` Demonstrated on a case example ("Michaela," who consistently avoided the deep
squat): a 5-inch wall/PVC-pipe test screens ankle dorsiflexion in half-kneeling (driving the knee
toward the target without the heel lifting); passive joint-capsule and muscle-tightness checks
follow if the wall test is limited. For the hip, passive external rotation is tested at 90° hip
flexion, then compared against active external rotation from a seated position — **"if her active
range equals her passive range, then it's good. If her passive range is greater than her active
range, then it's a motor coordination problem"** requiring strengthening rather than stretching.

## Natural-movement corrective drills

`[09:14]`–`[13:17]` For a limited ankle/hip pattern, drills demonstrated include: a kneeling
ankle self-mobilization using body weight, a toe-inclusive rocking drill, a seated position sitting
on one shin with the other foot forward and the knee gliding over the toes in a "rainbow" arc, a
tall half-kneel with multidirectional reaching to shift body weight, and a pull-assisted deep squat
with side-to-side weight shifting at the bottom position — each performed slowly, with nasal
breathing and no forced strain, "so that nervous system" doesn't "kick in and start guarding."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None in the sense of injury or illness — the video addresses a movement-competency screening
process (ankle dorsiflexion range, hip external rotation range) rather than symptoms of a health
condition.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Passive vs. active range of motion** — passive range is moved by another person; active range
  is moved by the individual themselves. Passive range exceeding active range at the same joint is
  used here to identify a motor-coordination problem rather than a true tissue restriction.
  `[09:14]`
