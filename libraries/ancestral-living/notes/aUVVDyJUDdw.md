# The Movement Code Hidden in Your DNA – With David Weck

## Central claim

**David Weck argues human movement should be trained around the spine as the origin of force and
coordination — not braced into a "neutral" position and treated as one joint — building an
evolutionary narrative from fish undulation through amphibian, mammalian, and primate movement
strategies to argue that multi-axis spinal mobility, not isolated "mirror muscle" strength, is the
foundation of both power and injury resistance.** `[07:13]` "I start noticing all these people are
coming to work with me who are physical therapists and they're personal trainers and they cannot
move their spine outside of this neutral and then they get hurt all the time." `[07:13]` His stated
reasoning is explicitly evolutionary rather than literature-first: "it's more of this thing of
like, what's the evolutionary perspective — why in the world does the spine have all of these
capabilities if you're not supposed to use them?"

## "Balance begins in the upper body"

`[63:32]`–`[76:48]` Weck states the upper body, not the feet, is where balanced movement is
organized: **"balance begins in the upper body... if my upper body is not correct then it doesn't
matter what my feet do."** His stated mechanism is neurological speed and degrees of freedom:
"it's a lot faster" to send a signal from the cervical spine than the lumbar spine, and the head
"has so many more degrees of freedom to align you precisely than your feet, legs, ankles." He
extends this into a definition of balance itself as coordination: "I define the word balance —
it's coordination."

## Ground-contact time: sprinting versus multidirectional movement

`[79:54]`–`[86:01]` Weck (with Kelley) discusses a specific contrast: sprinters are said to minimize
time on the ground to maximize flight time and stride length, while a named NFL athlete, Tyreek
Hill, is described as spending roughly 80% of his sprint time on the ground — time Weck frames as
valuable precisely because "you have more degrees of freedom to change the behavior of the system"
while still in contact with the ground, versus committing to a ballistic, uncorrectable path in the
air. Straight-line sprinting itself is characterized as a "fifth gear" that was "nonessential for
survival... a contrived thing to race you," since outrunning a predator is described as neither the
evolved strategy nor, per Weck, ever actually attempted: `[87:02]` "the caveman did not run away
from the saber-tooth tiger... you get your buddies, you all talk, you line them up... you point
your sticks at him."

## An evolutionary account of upright movement, offered as a hedge, not a fact

`[08:13]`–`[16:27]` Weck builds a chain of evolutionary speculation — from an ocean-bound
"spinal engine" originating in lateral fish undulation, through amphibians (a locked degrees-of-
freedom "coiling" mechanism), to mammals bringing limbs under the body, to primates entering trees
and developing an upright, throwing-capable shoulder and chest structure. He is explicit that this
chain of reasoning is not settled science: `[08:13]` "at this point it's just a theory... evolution,
it's a theory that is wrong that is useful for the mental gymnastics of understanding yourself."
Kelley separately corrects one specific point in Weck's account, noting current understanding
attributes hominid ground-dwelling more to climate-driven fragmentation of rainforest into savanna
than to social competition in the trees `[15:26]`.

## Movement taxonomy: from Hébert's ten categories to "stick, rope, rock"

`[99:23]` This discussion falls in the conversation's final stretch, which this transcript's
caption track leaves unanchored past this last timestamp. Referencing Georges Hébert's ten
natural-method categories (walk, run, jump, climb, move on all fours, balance, swim, lift, throw,
defend), Weck describes reorganizing them into locomotion, manipulation, interaction, and combat,
and separately distills manipulative-movement training down to three original tools: **"the rod,
the rope, and the rock"** — using stone-lifting and carrying in place of a barbell deadlift on the
stated grounds that a stone "is going to have a little more carry over to what you do" than a gym
implement.

## A personal account: trauma, MDMA therapy, and muscular "tone"

`[44:06]`–`[52:16]` Weck describes struggling with irritable bowel syndrome since adolescence, and
during a period of marital separation, undertaking internal family systems therapy and an
MDMA-assisted therapy session in which he experienced hours of physical shaking he connects to
previously unprocessed trauma from sexual abuse in his early teens. He frames this experience
through his own movement vocabulary — deep postural ("tonic") muscles as a site where unresolved
emotion gets stored, loosely connecting it to Nikolai Bernstein's concept of tone as the most basic
layer of motor control. **This is presented throughout as Weck's own therapeutic and personal
experience, not a scientific or biomechanical claim** — he offers no study connecting muscular tone
to trauma storage, and the note preserves that distinction rather than treating it as established.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None in a first-aid sense. Weck names his own chronic irritable bowel syndrome and describes it, in
his own account, as psychosomatic — "stored the most intense emotions at the deepest layer of the
self" — a personal interpretation rather than a diagnosed mechanism.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Spinal engine** — a term Weck uses (referencing "spinal engine literature," without naming a
  specific author in this transcript) for the spine's role in generating and organizing locomotor
  force, developed evolutionarily from lateral fish movement. `[06:11]`
- **Tone** — the most basic, chronic (non-phasic) level of muscular control, distinguished from
  voluntary muscle synergies and complex coordinated movement; described by Weck as "deeply
  emotional." `[49:13]`–`[50:15]`
- **CCT / RMT** — Weck's own terms for "coiling core training" (staying on the same side while
  shifting weight) versus "rotational movement training" (shifting weight across to the opposite
  side). `[18:31]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- "Pushing on the pull door and wondering why the door is locked" — Weck's image for a body whose
  movement problems come from not knowing better, not from a fixed flaw. Stated at the very start
  of the conversation and repeated again, unanchored, near its close. `[00:00]`
- The Fosbury Flop, adopted for years by only its inventor before being proven decisively superior,
  used as Weck's analogy for entrenched, evidence-resistant orthodoxy in exercise science.
  `[95:18]`

## Source-stated confidence

Explicitly mixed, and unusually self-aware about it within the conversation itself. Weck flags the
evolutionary chain of reasoning as "just a theory... that is wrong that is useful" rather than
settled fact `[08:13]`, and Kelley directly corrects one point in that chain regarding why hominids
came out of the trees `[15:26]`. The personal trauma/MDMA/tone narrative is offered as lived
experience, explicitly novel and unverified even by Weck's own account. By contrast, claims about
nerve conduction speed differences and general biomechanics are delivered with high personal
confidence but no citation to a specific study within this transcript — treat these as Weck's
professional opinion as a biomechanist and inventor, not as sourced findings.
