# She Coaches 16 People at Once and Nobody Gets Left Behind

## Central claim

**Coaching a large, mixed-ability natural-movement group without losing anyone rests on five
stated practices: give simple homework, make progressions bite-sized and achievable, watch body
weight shift closely, personalize each person's progression within the shared group movement, and
make it fun and explicitly tied to real life.** `[24:26]` Summarized directly: "the most important
thing to have as a coach is the ability to progress and regress for your clients within that
program setting" and "the last piece is just make it fun, make it enjoyable, and meet your
participants where they are in the middle."

## Why the gym stopped trying to convert CrossFitters

`[01:00]`–`[03:03]` Elaine Hung describes running MovNat classes for a year out of a CrossFit
facility with low uptake: **"when they recognized that they actually cannot squat as the grass or
like sit and squat... they very quickly just fade away and went back to what they are used to."**
The stated reason is a mindset mismatch — CrossFit culture is described as rewarding pushing "until
you collapse," which conflicts with a mobility-first approach that asks people to slow down. The
gym's resulting positioning: **"we really want to stay in between where we are training mobility...
and also give you strength that is practical for your everyday life... we are not trying to
replace the CrossFit, Hyrox, or the... yoga, Pilates kind of stuff."**

## The class-structure pyramid

`[07:08]`–`[08:08]` Classes are built as a pyramid: **mobility as the base** ("understanding that
you do not have range, and how do you expand the range"), **strength built on that available
range**, then **efficiency** (dynamic, explosive movement — jumps, vaults), with a play-based class
at the top. The stated logic: "if your base of mobility is not strong, it's not wide enough,
whatever you build is just going to be like narrow and small" — a wide base is framed as necessary
to support a large pyramid (i.e., long-term capability) later.

## Five coaching tips for group settings

`[15:16]`–`[21:22]` Asked for top recommendations for coaching groups, Hung lists five in order:
(1) **give simple homework** movements practiced between sessions, since twice-weekly attendance
alone produces slow progress; (2) **make movements accessible** in achievable, bite-sized steps,
since visibly "clunky" early attempts at a hard movement can discourage people from continuing; (3)
**have a sharp eye** for body weight shift and muscle recruitment, correcting even a small angular
error, described as what "sets us apart from every other studio"; (4) **personalize progressions
within the group**, so faster and slower participants both stay safely challenged rather than
either stagnating or risking injury by keeping pace; (5) **make it fun and relatable to life
outside the gym** — connecting a movement to a concrete daily task like picking up a child or
groceries.

## An unreleased stratification concept, extending the get-up assessment

`[11:11]`–`[14:15]` Brian Betancourt describes a still-unreleased project, explicitly flagged as
new information ("you're the first person hearing this... I've actually been working on for a
while, but I haven't presented it"): generalizing the get-up-as-assessment approach from
`9PcHMzl45rg` into a broader competency scale — conceptually "like a belt system in martial arts" —
so a coach can quickly identify a student's level across sitting positions, crawling, and other
developmental-sequence movements, "until you realize that they have the lowest level of
competency." He notes explicitly that this doesn't cover everyone ("you have people who can't get
off the ground but can squat 600 lb. I've seen it"), so it's offered as general guidance rather than
a complete diagnostic.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None in the sense of injury or illness — this video addresses group-coaching methodology and class
programming rather than a health condition. Lack of mobility (inability to "sit and squat" on the
ground) is discussed as a discovered limitation among new CrossFitter attendees, not as a symptom
being treated.

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as a first-person practitioner interview (Elaine Hung's own gym's experience) plus one
speaker's (Brian Betancourt's) explicitly disclosed work-in-progress concept — not a study or
published curriculum. The stratification idea is doubly hedged: offered as personal reasoning
("in my dream world, what I want to create...") and explicitly acknowledged as incomplete for edge
cases.
