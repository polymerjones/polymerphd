# Natural Movement & Parkour with Loren Fjord

## Central claim

**Loren Fjord argues parkour is not "the most extreme" human movement but rather ordinary
movements — walking, balancing, running, jumping — practiced to a high skill level on
unpredictable terrain, and that natural movement training of this kind benefits older adults more
than the young, because a lost or gained capability is far more consequential later in life.**
`[14:17]` "I would actually challenge that definition to say that parkour is actually the most
simple human movements, and then you are witnessing a practitioner that has taken the basic human
movements to the extreme." `[17:20]` "Who benefits most is going to be the elderly, the older
person, the adult. Because as you have more skill and more balance, it is more consequential in
your life what you can and can't do."

## Parkour vs. gymnastics: terrain as the defining difference

`[15:17]`–`[16:19]` Fjord's own stated distinction: "gymnastics is attempting at or building to the
most complex human movements over the most measured and metered and sterile terrain... the exact
same vault whether you're in China or... the US." Parkour, by contrast, is "every scenario, every
handrail... every fire hydrant, retaining wall, wherever you are... at the subjective whims of
Mother Nature or an architect that did not have you in mind." Both disciplines are said to "start
very basic," with elite expression in either looking extreme only at the top.

## The elderly get-up anecdote: confidence over performance

`[19:22]` A specific case is described: an elderly client recovering from a back injury wanted to
play with her grandson but was afraid, telling Fjord "I'm scared that I won't be able to get up off
the floor." After being taught a prone get-up and a tripod get-up, "her whole life shifted at that
moment... she was like, 'This was the most useful thing I've ever done.'" Fjord's own conclusion
from this: **"it's not about performance, it's about confidence."**

## Assessing competency before programming — and what happens when you skip it

`[19:22]`–`[20:23]` Both speakers describe assessing a client's "lowest level of competency" before
programming rather than assuming capability from appearance. A contrasting cautionary story is
given: a trainer put a client who "looked like they were in shape" but lacked coordination straight
onto 18-inch box jumps; the client missed the jump, "went shin first into a wooden box, broke the
shin, went over, broke the oval, dislocated the shoulder."

## The "poop test" and biotensegrity: two proxies for safe, efficient movement

`[11:13]`–`[12:14]` A quality heuristic Fjord attributes to a coach named Michael Carr: "if you're
trying to figure out whether your client's doing it right... the easiest test is the poop test.
Does it look like shit?" — used as an accessible proxy for whether a movement is under control and
therefore safer. Separately, Fjord cites *Biotensegrity* by Graham Scarr to push back on treating
the body as fragile: **"there's like this working assumption that we work like an inanimate object
that has a finite number of uses rather than a living tissue that replaces virtually all the cells
in its body over the course of 7 years."**

## Founder's origin story (context, not a movement-science claim)

`[22:24]`–`[24:27]` Fjord recounts buying a one-way ticket to England 20 years ago after seeing a
parkour video, with no contacts, phone, or return plan, and being immersed in a mixed-skill
community including figures he names as among the sport's originators (Daniel Ilabaca, Phil Doyle,
pre-Storror-crew members). He credits this experience with three lasting effects: physical practice
as a "proxy" for broader life growth, "physical empathy" from training alongside vastly different
skill levels, and a habit of testing internal beliefs against physical reality. The video's final
third (from roughly `[24:27]` onward) is largely a promotional announcement for a 20th-anniversary
retreat event Fjord is organizing — included here for context, not treated as a movement-science or
practice claim in its own right.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None in a clinical sense. The conversation distinguishes training-related physical sensations —
discomfort, fatigue, pain, and tissue damage — as a skill novices often lack, without giving
specific diagnostic criteria for telling them apart beyond experience.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Biotensegrity** — a term from Graham Scarr's book of the same name, describing the body's
  structure "from the cellular level down to the mechanical level" as a tensegrity structure,
  cited here to argue the body is adaptive and self-renewing rather than fragile. `[12:14]`
- **The "poop test"** — an informal quality check (credited to coach Michael Carr) for whether a
  movement looks and sounds controlled, used as a proxy for biomechanical safety and efficiency.
  `[11:13]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- "Big cat philosophy" — a big cat is lazy until it's go time, then commits fully; used to
  distinguish the training mindset (deliberately creating mechanical disadvantage to build
  capacity) from the performance mindset (conserving effort, efficiency) needed at an actual event.
  `[04:03]`
- The body compared to "an inanimate object that has a finite number of uses" (the assumption being
  challenged) versus a living tissue that replaces nearly all its cells over about seven years.
  `[12:14]`

## Source-stated confidence

Mostly personal coaching philosophy and first-person anecdote from a named practitioner, explicitly
offered as such. One claim is attributed to an external source with real specificity — the American
College of Sports Medicine's resistance-training guideline update, described as reviewing "300,000
participants and something like 147 or 157 meta-analyses" and concluding consistency matters more
than any specific tool or method — though the exact figures are recalled from memory in
conversation rather than cited from the document itself, and should be treated as an approximate
recollection, not a verified citation.
