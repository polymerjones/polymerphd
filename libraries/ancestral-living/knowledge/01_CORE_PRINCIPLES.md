# 01 — Core Principles

Five patterns recur across this collection often enough to treat as load-bearing, even though no
single source states them as a numbered list. Read these first — they're the lens the rest of this
package applies to any specific movement, drill, or practice.

---

## 1. Natural movement is framed as the origin sport and exercise are derived from, not the reverse

The recurring claim across this collection's coaching and founder-level sources is that running,
jumping, climbing, throwing, and carrying are the ancestral, general-purpose movement repertoire
humans evolved with — and that specific sports (soccer, tennis, weightlifting) are narrower,
specialized expressions of that same underlying capability, not the other way around. One source
puts it directly: sport and fitness "enable natural movement" only in the sense that natural
movement is what "make[s] any sports... any physical activity... possible" in the first place, since
"our ancestors evolved with these abilities" long before any sport formalized a narrow slice of
them.

## 2. Movement is framed as a behavior that solves a real problem, not a repetition to perform

Several sources draw a sharp line between "exercise" (repetition performed for its own sake,
described in this collection as potentially "mindless") and "practicing movement" (engaging with a
real physical problem — getting up off the floor, carrying a load, crossing an obstacle). This
distinction is used as a diagnostic tool as much as a philosophy: how a person solves the "get up
off the floor" problem, and which solution they avoid, is treated as revealing something concrete
about their mobility or strength that a repetition-based exercise wouldn't surface.

## 3. Technique and structured progression matter even for "natural" movement

More than one source is explicit that natural movement is not the same as unstructured outdoor
play — it's taught as a systematic method with progressions, the same way a martial art is taught
through trained technique rather than improvised fighting. This collection's sources are
consistent that movements which look effortless on video (a barefoot rock-face slide, a smooth
get-up) are effortless *because* of trained technique, not because they require no skill — a point
several sources say surprises people who try to replicate the movements without preparation.

## 4. Capacity has to be built gradually — the body has been "numbed," and rushing causes injury

A specific, repeated caution across barefoot training, spinal mobility, and general movement
coaching in this collection is that skipping a gradual buildup is the direct cause of overuse
injury (hairline fractures, plantar fasciitis, tendinitis are named examples). The stated mechanism
is that years of cushioned, unchallenged movement dull tissue tolerance, so tolerance has to be
rebuilt in small doses rather than assumed to already be present. A closely related, separately
stated principle for mobility work specifically: "frequency or consistency" beats intensity —
light, frequent practice is described as more effective than infrequent, aggressive stretching.

## 5. Awareness and self-generated effort are treated as doing more than the stretch or load itself

Multiple sources in this collection push back against relying on external leverage (pulling on a
limb, using momentum) as the main driver of a mobility gain, instead directing attention to
visualizing the body's internal shape and using the person's own muscular tension to reach an end
range. The stated reasoning is that awareness — recognizing where a limitation actually lives, not
just pushing harder at the point that's already flexible — is what produces lasting change, with
the stretch or the load treated as the occasion for that awareness rather than the mechanism on its
own.
