# The unpopular truth about the concept of the lone genius | David Epstein

## Central claim

**The "lone genius" narrative that credits history-changing breakthroughs to a single isolated
mind is largely a storytelling convention, not an accurate account — the actual pattern, called
"multiple discovery," is that major breakthroughs are reached independently by several people at
nearly the same time, because well-defined problems (not solitary genius) are what actually
channel innovation.** `[01:01]` "Most world-changing breakthroughs are arrived at by multiple
people in almost the same way at almost the same time, even though those people are working
independently... he found that this was the norm with world-changing breakthroughs, not the
exception."

## Mendeleev's periodic table: the real story versus the popular myth

`[02:01]`–`[04:02]` The widely repeated story — Mendeleev falling asleep after three sleepless
days and dreaming the periodic table into existence — is stated flatly to be false, despite being
celebrated by the Royal Society, used in a mattress company's marketing, and cited in Matthew
Walker's *Why We Sleep*. The actual cause: Mendeleev had a publishing contract for a two-volume
chemistry textbook and had only fit 8 of 63 known elements into volume one, forcing him to organize
the remaining 55 by family to save space — and it was in that process that the periodic pattern
became visible. The broader point: **"there were zero periodic tables before 1860. And then there
were six in the next eight years"** — following a specific 1860 conference (Karlsruhe) where a
shared standard for measuring atomic weight let independent labs' work finally be compared.

## Darwin and Wallace: synthesis and a shared "problem setter," not solitary insight

`[07:06]`–`[09:07]` Darwin is reframed as "not so much a creator as... a synthesizer," corresponding
with roughly 240 breeders and gardeners and combining existing observations (marine fossils on
mountains, shared bone structures across species) into a coherent framework, rather than making a
single isolated leap. Alfred Russel Wallace arrived at essentially the same theory independently at
the same time — Darwin's own reaction, quoted: "I have never seen a crazier coincidence." Both had
independently read Thomas Malthus's essay on population, which Epstein calls an example of
**"wrong but seminal"** work: Malthus's population predictions turned out to be incorrect, but the
essay defined the problem (resource scarcity driving competition) so well that it triggered both
Darwin's and Wallace's breakthroughs.

## Einstein's relativity: a well-defined problem, not a thought-experiment leap

`[05:04]`–`[06:05]` Epstein casts doubt on the famous "riding a beam of light" origin story for
special relativity, noting Einstein didn't tell it until decades after it supposedly happened.
What's better documented: the "magnet and wire problem," a well-defined puzzle already circulating
among physicists (why does the physics description differ depending on whether the magnet or the
wire is described as moving), which Einstein resolved by concluding that conceptions of time and
space, not the laws of physics, had to change.

## "Problem setters" as an undervalued role

`[10:08]`–`[16:14]` Malthus is named a **"problem setter"** — likened to a volleyball player who
sets the ball for someone else's spike — someone who defines a problem clearly even if wrong about
its solution. David Hilbert is given as a deliberate, large-scale example: around 1900 he compiled
and precisely defined roughly two dozen open mathematics problems, setting the agenda for 20th-
century mathematics, with most later solved by others. A contemporary parallel: engineer Jayshree
Seth's "mosaic building" process at 3M — interviewing colleagues, identifying a shared underlying
problem across separately-working inventors, then convening them around that defined problem.
DeepMind founder Demis Hassabis is quoted (unattributed to a specific date) calling problem
definition "the most important skill now," especially in the age of AI.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Multiple discovery** — Robert Merton's term for major breakthroughs being reached
  independently by more than one person at nearly the same time. `[00:00]`
- **"Wrong but seminal"** — work that is factually incorrect but defines a problem so effectively
  that it catalyzes other researchers' correct breakthroughs (named after a paper Epstein read by
  that title). `[11:09]`
- **Problem setter** — someone who defines a problem clearly and narrowly, enabling others'
  breakthroughs, regardless of whether they solve it themselves. `[10:08]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as a synthesis of named historical scholarship (Robert Merton's sociology of science,
James Burke, Thomas Kuhn's posthumously published later work) and specific, checkable historical
episodes (the Karlsruhe conference, Hilbert's problem list, the Palm Pilot's origin) — the Mendeleev
"dream" story is explicitly and directly labeled false rather than merely doubted, a stronger
confidence claim than most in this note.
