# Physician: Self-loneliness is real, and most people have it

## Central claim

**Alongside ordinary interpersonal loneliness, the speaker proposes "self-loneliness" — a
measurable pattern of being hostile or removed from one's own body through touch, thought, and
action — and argues that recognizing and correcting it (including through physical touch, not only
willpower or self-talk) has real physiological effects.** `[07:07]` "There's also, I think, another
kind of loneliness which I would depict as like self-loneliness... because of studies that look
into how do you touch yourself, how do you talk to yourself, how do you handle yourself... we
rarely ever talk about these because we don't so much think about having an inner world."

## Wound healing as a model for emotional processing

`[01:00]`–`[03:02]` The speaker describes her own numbness after her grandmother's death — not
matching the expected image of visible grief — and how reading about wound healing reframed it: a
wound is first covered by a numb crust, appearing unchanged for days, while underneath "it is
reorganization... attracting cells that could help fix this." She generalizes this to psychological
healing after loss: **"maybe it is not conscious all the time, and then maybe it doesn't hurt right
away, and maybe it is covered up with something, but it's happening, and it's okay... to let it."**
People feeling numb or unexpectedly unmoved after a major life event (a death, a promotion, a
birth) are explicitly told this reflects complexity, not brokenness.

## The physiological effects of touch, and interpersonal synchronization

`[04:03]`–`[06:05]` Touch is described as having effects comparable to medication, evidenced by
research on **preterm infants** given skin contact after birth (lower stress hormones, improved
metabolic markers, measurable behavioral differences a year later) and benefits reported even in
people who typically find touch overstimulating (cited examples: autism, ADHD), when the touch
comes from someone trusted. A specific experiment is described: one person lying in a scanner tube
with a trusted partner holding their hand outside — their heart rate, skin conductivity, blood
pressure, and stress hormones measurably synchronize toward the calmer person's level, described as
a one-directional transmission of physiological calm.

## Scarring as a metaphor for over- and under-processing emotional injury

`[08:08]`–`[11:11]` Two atypical healing patterns are described and mapped onto emotional patterns:
**hypertrophic scarring** (excess tissue buildup, potentially escalating to non-stop-growing keloid
scars) is likened to overreacting after being hurt — "like someone being burglar[ized] then you
have like five locks on your door," or constant partner-checking after infidelity. **Hypotrophic
scarring** (scars sinking below skin level) is likened to turning inward with self-blame after a
loss — "always going back in the head... was it all my fault." The stated lesson from proper wound
care applies analogously: minimizing outside disturbance, protecting the area, and allowing
"the cells do their thing" — i.e., allowing space and calm rather than either escalating or
self-attacking.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Feeling numb or emotionally flat after a major life event that's expected to provoke strong feeling
(bereavement, promotion, childbirth) — reframed as normal variation, not a sign something is wrong;
self-directed touch and self-talk patterns (rough versus gentle) as an indicator of "self-loneliness."

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Self-loneliness** — the speaker's proposed term for a hostile or disconnected relationship with
  one's own body, assessed through touch, thought, and action, alongside ordinary interpersonal
  loneliness. `[06:05]`
- **Interpersonal synchronization** — the described physiological effect of one person's calmer
  nervous-system state transmitting to another person during physical contact. `[05:05]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- Emotional processing after loss compared directly to a wound healing under a numb crust — an
  unglamorous but active reorganization process, not an absence of change. `[02:00]`
- Hypertrophic and hypotrophic scarring as parallel physical metaphors for over-processing
  (vigilance, mistrust) and under-processing (self-blame) emotional injury. `[09:10]`–`[10:11]`
