# What Creatine Actually Does to Your Body

## Central claim

**Creatine is framed as the most thoroughly studied supplement on the market, with genuinely
strong evidence for some uses (muscle performance, healthy aging) and genuinely early/limited
evidence for others (cognition, Alzheimer's) — and the video is explicit about not blurring those
two tiers together.** `[10:07]` "The science is generally better than the hype most people give it
credit for."

## History: from an 1832 lab curiosity to the 1992 Olympics

`[00:00]`-`[02:03]` — isolated in 1832 by French chemist **Michel Eugène Chevreul** from beef
extract, named from the Greek *kreas* (meat); sat as an obscure biochemical curiosity for roughly
160 years because its function wasn't understood. British sprinter **Linford Christie**'s openly
disclosed creatine use at the **1992 Barcelona Olympics** (passing all drug tests, since creatine
is legal) is credited as the moment it moved from chemistry textbooks into mainstream sports
nutrition.

## The mechanism, step by step

`[02:03]`-`[04:04]` — creatine survives stomach acid largely intact and is absorbed in the small
intestine; a specific transporter (**CreaT**) actively pulls it from blood into muscle and brain
cells, a process **insulin measurably assists** — the basis for the (not "bro science") advice to
take it with carbs or a meal. **~95% ends up in skeletal muscle**, the rest in brain and other
energy-hungry tissue. Inside the cell, the enzyme **creatine kinase** attaches a phosphate group,
forming **phosphocreatine** — described as "a fully charged spare battery" that regenerates ATP
(which runs out in ~10 seconds of maximal effort) on the spot, extending high-intensity output
before the body switches to a slower energy system.

## The five use cases, individually graded

`[04:04]`-`[07:05]`:

| Use case | Evidence tier, as stated |
|---|---|
| **Muscle strength/power, short max-effort work** | "The most studied application in all of sports nutrition, full stop... about as rock solid as sports nutrition gets" |
| **Healthy aging** (combined with resistance training, vs. sarcopenia) | "One of the most consistent things in the whole body of creatine research" — increased lean mass, strength, bone density, and reduced fall-risk data |
| **Cognition/memory in aging brains** | Real per systematic reviews, "especially people who are already dealing with some level of cognitive decline"; effects in young healthy brains "not quite so clear" |
| **Acute sleep deprivation** (high single dose, 0.35 g/kg ≈ 20-30g) | Small, early controlled studies; measurable improvement within hours, lasting most of the day — "genuinely exciting... but it's more recent" |
| **Alzheimer's disease** | Explicitly separated from a viral but unverifiable claim (see below); real evidence is a small 8-week University of Kansas **feasibility/safety pilot**, not an efficacy trial |

**On the Alzheimer's claim specifically:** `[06:04]`-`[07:05]` states directly he could not find the
widely circulated "slows Alzheimer's by 30-50%" claim anywhere in the scientific literature and asks
viewers to send it if they have it. What the actual cited pilot study showed: creatine was well
tolerated, brain creatine levels rose ~11%, and there was "a very small signal of cognitive
improvement on some tests" — explicitly not proof the disease is treated or slowed.

## Dosing: why 5g, and whether loading is necessary

`[07:05]`-`[09:07]` — states the standard 5g dose is mostly a convenient round scoop size, not a
precisely reverse-engineered optimum. **Cites a 1996 study** comparing a 20g/day 6-day loading
protocol against 3g/day from day one: both groups reached identical muscle creatine saturation by
day 28 — the loading group just arrived roughly 3 weeks sooner, at the cost of more GI upset from
dumping 20g into the gut at once. **Recommendation:** 3-5g daily, loading optional and skippable;
the much higher acute dose (20-30g) is a separate, distinct use case for sleep-deprivation cognitive
support, not a daily maintenance practice.

## Safety, addressing the two main myths

`[10:07]`-`[11:09]` — **kidney damage:** doesn't occur in healthy kidneys; the myth is traced
directly to creatine breaking down into creatinine, the same lab marker physicians use to assess
kidney function, causing false alarm at a slightly elevated number. Existing kidney disease is
named as a real reason to consult a doctor first. **Water retention:** real, but described as water
pulled into muscle cells specifically (part of the mechanism, contributing to fuller muscle
appearance), not generalized bloating. **GI upset:** more common at higher/loading doses; resolves
with lower, spread-out dosing.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- Reduced muscle strength/power output in short maximal-effort activity `[04:04]`.
- Age-related muscle loss, falls, and frailty risk (sarcopenia) `[05:04]`.
- Cognitive impairment in older adults, particularly those already experiencing decline `[05:04]`.
- Cognitive slowdown during acute sleep deprivation `[06:04]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Phosphocreatine** — creatine with a phosphate group attached (via creatine kinase), the form
  that directly regenerates ATP during short maximal effort `[03:04]`.
- **CreaT** — the specific transporter that actively pulls creatine from blood into muscle and
  brain cells `[02:03]`.
- **Creatinine** — creatine's breakdown product, used as a kidney-function marker, source of the
  "creatine harms kidneys" myth `[11:09]`.
