# The strange science of placebos | David Linden

## Central claim

**Belief measurably changes body physiology in both directions — it can kill (voodoo death, fatal
misdiagnosis, grief-induced cardiac events) or heal (the placebo effect) — and for at least one
case, pain relief, the exact biological mechanism (endogenous opioid release) is now identified,
which the source argues should motivate serious biological (not just dismissive) treatment of
mind-body medicine.** `[00:00]` "Like a lot of biologists, I've been resistant to the idea that our
mental life affects our body profoundly... In just the last 10 years, our understanding of that
biology has gotten a whole lot better."

## Voodoo death, updated with modern physiology

`[01:01]`–`[04:03]` Linden revisits physiologist Walter Cannon's 1942 documentation of **"voodoo
death"** — death following belief in a curse — and updates Cannon's incomplete mechanism: it's not
just sympathetic nervous system hyperarousal (fight-or-flight overdrive), but **a "one-two punch"**
where sustained, elevated parasympathetic activation follows and, together with the initial
sympathetic surge, shuts down the body. Critically, **the effect requires belief**: "if you don't
have the belief that you can be killed by a hex or curse, then it doesn't work," and a witch doctor
who convincingly lifts the curse can reverse an otherwise fatal course.

## The same mechanism in modern medicine and grief

`[04:03]`–`[08:09]` Linden extends voodoo death beyond "supernatural belief" societies: a
documented 1970s case of a man diagnosed with (and dying on schedule from) liver cancer, whose
autopsy revealed he never had the disease — **"it was his belief that he had a fatal illness that
killed him... in exactly the same way that voodoo death kills people who believe in it."**
Separately, epidemiological data confirms a real "broken heart" effect: mortality (cardiovascular
events, cancer, autoimmune disease) rises measurably after losing a spouse, close friend, sibling,
or even a pet. A specific named mechanism, **takotsubo cardiomyopathy**, is a grief-triggered
cardiac impairment from sympathetic nervous system overactivation, first identified by doctors in
Japan and named for the heart's resulting resemblance to an octopus trap.

## Placebo pain relief: a mechanism, not just a mystery

`[10:11]`–`[13:18]` The placebo effect is presented as occurring across many conditions (pain,
infection clearance, cardiovascular function, surgical recovery, immune modulation), but pain is
singled out as the best-understood case: **naloxone (an opioid-blocking drug) can block the
placebo pain-relief effect**, demonstrating that it's mediated by the brain's own endogenous
opioids (endorphins, enkephalins) — not merely psychological distraction. A separate experiment
cited: pairing a real blood-pressure drug with a lemon-lime flavoring repeatedly can condition the
flavor alone (with no drug) to lower blood pressure, "just like Pavlov's dog" — described as
associative learning, though the underlying biology isn't fully understood.

## Two counterintuitive placebo phenomena

`[13:18]`–`[15:21]` **Geographic/temporal drift:** the placebo effect for pain has measurably
strengthened in recent decades, but — puzzlingly — only in the United States (and possibly New
Zealand), which Linden tentatively ties to the US being nearly alone in permitting direct-to-
consumer prescription drug advertising, explicitly flagged as a guess ("we don't really know, but
the best guess is..."). **Open-label placebo:** even when patients are told outright that a pill is
inert sugar, a placebo effect still occurs — repeatedly replicated across "whole branches of
medicine," which Linden calls "deeply counterintuitive."

## Where this research is heading: drugs and devices, not just behavior

`[16:24]`–`[19:32]` Linden frames mind-body signaling research as informing more than behavioral
advice (meditation, psychotherapy) — it's already produced GLP-1-based drugs (via understanding
body-to-brain signaling) and vagus-nerve-stimulating implants for depression and epilepsy (via
understanding mind-to-body signaling). He names current non-invasive brain stimulation
(transcranial magnetic stimulation, transcranial direct current stimulation) as still "really,
really crude" — stimulating golf-ball-sized brain volumes indiscriminately — with more precise,
genetically-targeted stimulation methods described as still experimental but a future direction for
deliberately exploiting these pathways therapeutically.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Death following belief in a curse or a fatal (even if inaccurate) diagnosis; elevated mortality
risk in the period following bereavement; takotsubo cardiomyopathy's cardiac impairment following
acute grief; pain relief, improved surgical healing, and immune modulation as documented placebo
responses.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Voodoo death** — Walter Cannon's 1942 term for belief-driven death via a curse or hex,
  mechanistically a sympathetic-then-parasympathetic autonomic shutdown. `[01:01]`
- **Takotsubo cardiomyopathy** — grief-triggered cardiac impairment from sympathetic
  overactivation, named for the heart's resulting octopus-trap shape (Japanese: tako = octopus,
  tsubo = trap). `[07:07]`
- **Open-label placebo** — a placebo effect that occurs even when the patient knows the treatment
  is inert. `[15:21]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- The lemon-lime-flavoring/blood-pressure-drug pairing "just like Pavlov's dog" — a concrete,
  reusable illustration of placebo effects as partly associative learning. `[12:17]`
- Voodoo death as "happening in modern medicine with people in white coats just in a slightly
  different setting" — reframes a seemingly exotic phenomenon as continuous with ordinary clinical
  practice. `[06:05]`

## Source-stated confidence

Mixed and explicitly flagged throughout: the naloxone/endorphin mechanism for placebo pain relief
is presented as established science; the US-specific placebo-strengthening trend's explanation is
explicitly called "the best guess," not a confirmed finding; the associative-learning explanation
for classically-conditioned placebo effects is stated as incomplete ("all the details of that are
not yet understood"). Historical cases (the 1970s misdiagnosis death) are presented as documented
in the medical literature but without a specific named citation in this transcript.
