# Young people say their lives feel fake. Here's why | Arthur Brooks

## Central claim

**The explosion in depression and anxiety among young adults is driven by a loss of meaning, not
generational character or economic conditions — and the mechanism is that modern technology
systematically shifts activity toward the brain's left ("how-to") hemisphere and away from the
right ("why"/meaning) hemisphere, which people then experience as a life that "feels fake."**
`[00:00]` "The real reason that we actually find behind the epidemic of depression and anxiety is
the answer to the question, does your life have meaning?... Something happened very recently,
really only in the past 15 years, that made life feel meaningless."

## Meaning has three parts, and most people aren't looking for it

`[03:02]`–`[06:06]` Meaning is defined via psychologists Michael Steger and Frank Martela as
**coherence** (why things happen the way they do), **purpose** (why I do the things I do), and
**significance** (why my life matters). Crossing a "presence" score (how much meaning someone
feels) against a "search" score (how hard they're looking) produces four types: **happy homebodies**
(high presence, not searching), **hopeful wanderers** (low presence, searching hard), **natural
seekers** (high presence, still searching), and **lost in place** (low presence, not searching at
all). The source states the "lost in place" group — typically around 5% of the population — is now
"multiples of that, and it's going up every single year," concentrated among young adults, and
calls this "the number that's actually exploding." A stated diagnostic detail: the first question
Brooks asks someone who says their life feels meaningless is whether they're actually looking for
meaning — and characteristically, they are not.

## The right brain/left brain mechanism

`[07:08]`–`[13:12]` Drawing on hemispheric lateralization, the right hemisphere is described as
handling "the why, the mystery, the meaning, the love and the happiness," while the left hemisphere
handles "the engineering side, the complicated problem side." Brooks argues modern culture adopted
a philosophy that "every problem in life had a technological solution," pushing people into
left-hemisphere-dominant living even for problems — loneliness, meaninglessness — that are
inherently right-hemisphere, relational problems: **"when you try to solve a complex problem with a
complicated solution, it gets worse... every additional hour per day that you spend on social
media, the lonelier you get."**

## Why we can't stand being bored anymore

`[17:15]`–`[21:19]` The default mode network — the brain structure active during undistracted,
bored moments — is named as the substrate for meaning-related thought, and the source argues
constant phone use (cited at 205 checks per day, roughly every 5 minutes for the average American)
has made boredom nearly extinct. A cited lab experiment is used as evidence people actively prefer
discomfort to boredom: given nothing to do for 15 minutes except a button that delivers a painful
electric shock, **"25% of the women actually shocked themselves. Two-thirds of the guys shocked
themselves"** — one participant shocked himself 190 times and was removed from the study. The
source connects this directly to addiction: boredom and anxiety are named as two of the best
predictors of substance and behavioral addiction, producing a **"doom loop"** where boredom triggers
device use, which further erodes the capacity to tolerate boredom.

## Why "more technology" won't fix it

`[23:20]`–`[26:25]` Brooks names the **hedonic treadmill** (via a billionaire anecdote — "it didn't
feel the way I thought it was going to. I guess I need another billion") and applies the same logic
to the idea that better technology (AI partners, AI therapists) will eventually solve loneliness:
**"you can't solve the right brain's problems by getting more left brain solutions."** He explicitly
rejects both "throw your phone in the ocean" abstinence and unlimited-technology utopianism,
positioning the fix as appropriate use — protecting time and attention for relational, mysterious,
meaning-based activity rather than eliminating technology altogether.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

A stated tripling of depression and doubling of anxiety in under-30 adults over 15 years; a
subjective sense of life "feeling fake" or "like a simulation"; chronic low-grade loneliness that
worsens with more social media use; boredom intolerance and reflexive phone-checking as a
measurable behavioral signal (205 checks/day cited as the American average).

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Default mode network** — the brain structures active during undistracted, bored states,
  associated with mind-wandering and meaning-related thought. `[17:15]`
- **The Campbell effect** — gradual decline followed by sudden, seemingly abrupt collapse, named
  for a character in Hemingway's *The Sun Also Rises*. `[22:19]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- Living partly "in the Matrix" — technological mediation between people compared to the simulation
  in the film, used to describe why young people report their lives "feel fake." `[02:01]`
- "If you're in a hole, stop digging" — used against the argument that more of a thing causing a
  problem (more government spending, more alcohol, more technology) will eventually solve it.
  `[24:22]`

## Source-stated confidence

Delivered with confident, direct assertion from Brooks's own stated expertise (professor,
happiness researcher, author of *The Meaning of Your Life*), naming specific psychologists (Steger,
Martela) and a validated research instrument (the meaning in life questionnaire) he states he has
personally administered to students over years. Some figures — the 205-checks-per-day average, the
shock-experiment percentages — are stated without naming the specific study, preserved here as
stated rather than independently verified.
