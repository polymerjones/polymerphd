# Electrocution | Adult First Aid | EMT First Aid Training

## Central claim

**Scene safety comes before treating an electrocution victim — the person may still be "live" and
dangerous to touch, so the first step is always to eliminate the electrical hazard (cutting power
for low voltage, staying far back and calling for help for high voltage) before approaching at
all.** `[02:03]` "Always be aware that the patient may be live if they're still in contact with the
electrical supply... if there's any doubt at all, do not touch them. Always make sure the power is
off."

## Why the danger persists, and how much current matters

`[01:02]` The source explains the underlying mechanism: when a body part contacts or nears a live
source, "the body becomes the link from live to earth, and the electricity transfers through the
body to earth," causing muscles to convulse. A current as small as **10 milliamps** can cause
muscle contraction strong enough that "a hand... can close around the source... and cannot release
it" — meaning a victim may be unable to let go even if conscious. In wet conditions, a current of
just **60 milliamps** "can be fatal," since moisture "reduces the body's resistance" and can disrupt
the heart's electrical activity, causing cardiac arrest.

## High voltage: the arc distance

`[03:05]` For high-voltage sources specifically, the source gives a striking distance figure:
**"electricity can arc 80 meters through the air and through the ground,"** with that distance
potentially increased in wet or foggy weather — the stated instruction is to stay well clear and
call emergency services rather than attempt any approach.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Muscle convulsion at the moment of contact; two separate burn sites (entry and exit) from
electricity passing through the body, described as painful and slow-healing; possible additional
injuries from being thrown back by the shock or from flying debris/exposed wires.

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as direct first-aid instruction alongside UK-specific statistics (about 1,000 reported
electrical accidents annually, 25 fatal) attributed to "a government report by the HSE, the Health
and Safety Executive" — a named, if unlinked, source for the statistics specifically, with the
clinical current-threshold figures given without further citation.
