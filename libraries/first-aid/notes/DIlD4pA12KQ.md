# Drowning, Secondary and Dry | Water Safety | EMT First Aid Training

## Central claim

**Because a drowning collapse is treated as a respiratory event rather than a cardiac one, the
CPR sequence differs from an on-land collapse: give 5 rescue breaths first, then 30 compressions to
2 breaths for a full minute, before leaving to call emergency services if alone — the reverse
priority from a dry-land collapse, where the ambulance is called immediately, before starting
CPR.** `[01:01]` "In drowning cases if the patient is not breathing then you would open the airway
and you'll pinch your nose and you'll deliver five rescue breaths... if not, then you would start
with 30 compressions followed by two breaths, and you continue doing that for one minute."

## Why the sequence is reversed for drowning

`[00:00]`–`[02:01]` The source explains the underlying logic: "the patient has most likely stopped
breathing because of a lack of oxygen, a respiratory issue, not a cardiac issue... it's very, very
similar to the reason why a child or baby would have stopped breathing." Because rescue breaths
alone may restart breathing in a purely respiratory event, the source states one minute of CPR is
attempted before a lone rescuer leaves to call for help — contrasted directly with a dry-land
collapse, "as soon as you know they're not breathing you must call the ambulance straight away."

## Dry drowning vs. secondary/delayed drowning

`[03:03]` **Dry drowning**: water is inhaled through the nose or mouth and triggers "a spasm in the
airway," specifically of the vocal cords, which don't relax — water never actually reaches the
lungs, but the spasm itself blocks breathing. **Secondary/delayed drowning**: water is inhaled into
the lungs and causes inflammation or swelling of the small air sacs, which can develop "hours or
even days later... up to 72 hours afterward," preventing oxygen from reaching the bloodstream.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

For dry or secondary drowning: difficulty breathing, a persistent cough, choking, sudden lethargy
or sleepiness, irritability, or vomiting, appearing at any point after a water incident, including
well after the person has left the water.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Dry drowning** — a vocal-cord spasm following water inhalation that blocks breathing without
  water ever entering the lungs. `[03:03]`
- **Secondary (delayed) drowning** — lung inflammation from inhaled water that can present up to
  72 hours after the initial exposure. `[03:03]`

## Analogies worth reusing

None distinct.

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as direct first-aid instruction, with a WHO-attributed global drowning-mortality figure
(320,000 annual deaths) given without further citation detail, and an explicit statement that dry
and secondary drowning are both very rare — a hedge against over-applying this content to every
water incident.
