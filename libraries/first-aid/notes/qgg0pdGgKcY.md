# Back to School First Aid Special - Medications and Emergencies at School

## Central claim

**A child with an ongoing medical condition is best supported at school through an Individual
Healthcare Plan and by carrying their own medication rather than relying solely on the school's
optional spare-medication stock — the source frames the school's spare inhaler or EpiPen as a
safety net for when a child's own device fails, not a replacement for it.** `[07:09]` "Wherever
possible, children should always be allowed to carry their own medication... they should never be
locked away anywhere." `[08:10]` "It's still important that your child takes their own medication
to school... it just provides an extra layer of support in case of emergency."

## UK policy specifics named

`[05:04]`–`[09:11]` The Children and Families Act (September 2014) is cited as the basis requiring
schools to properly support pupils with medical needs. Since 2014, UK schools have been permitted
to keep a spare reliever ("blue") inhaler on site for emergency use, prompted by a stated figure
that "86% of children at school have been left without an inhaler because they've forgotten to
take it, it's run out, they've lost it, or it's broken." Since October 2017, schools have similarly
been permitted to keep a spare adrenaline auto-injector pen for anaphylaxis — both explicitly
described as optional for a school to provide, not a legal requirement.

## Bringing routine medication into school

`[10:13]`–`[11:14]` For short-term medication like antibiotics, the source's preferred approach is
scheduling doses to avoid the school day entirely (morning, after school, bedtime). If medication
must be brought in: only prescription medication, in original packaging (with a named exception for
insulin pens), accompanied by written parental consent, and only the smallest quantity needed for
that day — "don't take the whole seven days of antibiotics in."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Signs of an emotional/mental-health issue in a child are named for parental awareness rather than
acute treatment: tantrums or rages, tearfulness, sleep disruption, and recurring stomach aches,
alongside a stated general dehydration effect (headaches, tiredness, dizziness, mood swings, poor
concentration).

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Individual Healthcare Plan (IHP)** — a written document specifying what medical support and
  medication administration a school will provide for a specific pupil. `[06:07]`
