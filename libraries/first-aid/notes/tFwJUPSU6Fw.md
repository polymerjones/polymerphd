# Bites and stings from the sea | Adult First Aid | EMT First Aid Training

## Central claim

**Two specific pieces of "common knowledge" about jellyfish stings are directly corrected: fresh
water and urine both risk releasing more venom from unfired stingers rather than helping — the
correct sequence is removing tentacles with a flat object, rinsing with seawater, then soaking in
water as hot as tolerable for at least 30 minutes.** `[02:02]` "Despite the folklore, urinating on a
jellyfish sting may actually cause the stingers to release more venom rather than actually
providing relief... please do not wee over any wound."

## Jellyfish: removal technique and the fresh-water/urine myth

`[01:01]`–`[02:02]` Tentacle removal: "use a flat object such as a credit card... do not use your
fingers... do not rub them either... you can aggravate them, which will set off the poison." The
source gives the same underlying mechanism for both myths it corrects: "fresh water actually
activates the stingers that have not yet released the venom," and urine works the same way,
"despite the folklore." After removal and seawater rinsing, the treatment is heat: "soak the area
in very warm water, as hot as you can possibly tolerate it, for at least 30 minutes."

## Weever fish: a hidden, high-pain hazard

`[02:02]`–`[03:02]` Weever fish "bury themselves in the sand" and sting via a dorsal fin embedding
in the foot when stepped on, "which causes excruciating pain." Treatment: remove the embedded
spines, then immerse the foot in water at "40 degrees Celsius or higher... for no less than 30
minutes." The source explicitly permits deferring to a lifeguard or medical professional instead of
self-treating: "if you do not feel comfortable performing first aid on yourself, please make sure
you seek medical advice immediately."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Jellyfish sting: a painful, red, irritated mark in most cases, rarely progressing to a severe or
life-threatening reaction. Weever fish sting: excruciating localized foot pain from venom injected
via the dorsal fin spines. General coastal hazards: cuts from barnacles/shells, crab pinches.
