# How to treat someone having an Asthma attack | Adult First Aid | EMT First Aid Training

## Central claim

**An asthma attack (airway muscles tightening, lining swelling, mucus narrowing the airway further)
is treated by getting the casualty into a chest-opening position, using their own reliever
inhaler — ideally through a spacer for a more precise dose — one puff at a time, escalating to
emergency services if there's no improvement after five minutes.** `[03:02]` "With the inhaler
without the spacer, we're going to encourage them to push down on it and breathe in and take one
puff every minute. After five minutes, if they're not feeling better, then we're going to call the
emergency medical services."

## Why it's a problem breathing out, not in

`[01:02]` The source frames the mechanism precisely: asthma "is not upon breathing in, it's a
problem breathing out," since narrowed, inflamed airways trap air on exhale. Stated severity
context: roughly 5.4 million people in the UK are in treatment for asthma (about 1.1 million of
them children), and around 2,000 people die of asthma per year in the UK — used to argue that a
common condition still deserves to be "treated seriously."

## Two inhalers, and why using the right one matters

`[02:02]` Preventative inhalers (typically brown, typically a steroid) are for daily use, morning
and night; reliever inhalers (typically blue, often containing salbutamol) are for an active
attack. The source is explicit an asthma sufferer having an attack should use only their own
inhaler, for two reasons: cross-contamination between people's inhalers, and the possibility the
medication inside differs from person to person.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

A distinctive wheeze, struggling to breathe, pale skin color, chest pain — with the source noting
"asthma triggers" that can prompt an attack even in a careful, medicated sufferer: chemicals,
pollen, exercise, cold air, and certain foods.
