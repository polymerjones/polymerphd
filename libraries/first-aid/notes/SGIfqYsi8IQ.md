# Anaphylaxis Explained: Signs, Symptoms & EpiPen Use

## Central claim

**Anaphylaxis is treated as a medical emergency requiring an immediate call to emergency services
and use of the person's own adrenaline auto-injector pen, pressed against the outer thigh through
clothing and held for a consistent 10 seconds regardless of which pen brand is used.** `[02:02]`
"If you suspect that you or someone else has experienced symptoms of anaphylactic reaction, you
must call the emergency medical services instantly, then you're going to get their auto injector
pen for them to use."

## Auto-injector technique, step by step

`[02:02]`–`[04:02]` The cap is described as commonly misunderstood: "the cap is not where the
needle comes from... the cap releases the needle from the opposite end of the pen." The injector is
pushed against the patient's leg until it clicks and held for a count that differs by brand — the
EpiPen's official hold time is 3 seconds, Jext's is 10 — with the source's explicit simplification:
**"I'd always encourage you to hold them for 10 seconds... it saves any confusion."** The pen
cannot be reused after one dose.

## Why two pens, and when a second dose is warranted

`[03:02]`–`[04:02]` The source gives three specific reasons a person with severe allergies should
always carry two pens: the first injection may have missed or been interrupted if the person
moved, the helper may have accidentally injected themselves instead, or the single dose inside one
pen may not be enough while waiting for an ambulance that "can take a while to get there." A second
dose is administered after 5 minutes specifically if the person resumes struggling to breathe.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

General allergic reaction: sneezing, wheezing, coughing, skin rash — varying by how the allergen
was encountered (e.g. airway symptoms from an inhaled allergen like pollen). Anaphylaxis: itchy
skin or raised red rash, swollen eyes/lips/hands/feet, lightheadedness or faintness, wheezy/narrowed
airway, breathing difficulty, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, collapse, and unconsciousness.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Allergen** — any substance that triggers an allergic reaction, with common examples named:
  pollen, house dust mites, mold, pets, nuts, fruits, latex, dairy products. `[00:00]`

## Analogies worth reusing

None distinct beyond the cap/needle clarification above, which functions as a practical
misconception-correction rather than a metaphor.

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as direct procedural instruction, including specific manufacturer-stated timing
(EpiPen 3 seconds, Jext 10 seconds) that the source itself flags as inconsistent and offers a
simplified workaround for — a transparent hedge rather than settled clinical consensus on hold
time.
