# Poisoning | Adult First Aid | EMT First Aid Training

## Central claim

**Across all forms of poisoning covered — swallowed chemicals or drugs, food poisoning, and
plant/fungi poisoning — the shared first-aid response is to identify the substance, stay calm,
avoid inducing vomiting, and call emergency medical services with as much detail as possible.**
`[01:00]` "The treatment firstly is to identify what has actually been taken... how many have they
swallowed, what they swallowed, and try and reassure them and call the emergency medical
services... this information will assist doctors to give the appropriate treatment."

## Why not to induce vomiting, and how to protect yourself during rescue breaths

`[02:02]` The source is explicit that inducing vomiting in a conscious poisoning casualty is
wrong: "vomiting can cause more damage and the chemical... can actually cover the whole of the
respiratory tract, mouth, and also inside the nose, causing more of respiratory issues." If the
casualty stops breathing, the source adds a self-protection step not mentioned in this
collection's other CPR notes: use a face shield, or cover the casualty's mouth and breathe through
their nose instead, to avoid the rescuer's own mouth contacting residual chemical.

## Food poisoning and dehydration

`[03:02]`–`[04:03]` Food poisoning is attributed to bacteria or viruses in contaminated food or
drink, commonly salmonella or E. coli found in meats in the UK, with onset from hours up to a day
after eating. The named serious complication is dehydration from vomiting and diarrhea, "especially
serious for the young and also the old, or if you're living in a hot climate." Treatment is rest,
hydration (naming rehydration powders and isotonic drinks), and seeking medical help if needed —
plus notifying others who may have shared the same food source.

## Plant and fungi poisoning

`[05:05]`–`[06:05]` Named examples: the foxglove plant (also called "Dead Man's Bells," "dog's
fingers," "fairy fingers"), whose toxins "directly affect the heart muscles" and can slow the
heartbeat or cause a heart attack; poisonous berries; and the death cap mushroom, which "causes
severe watery diarrhea between 6 to 12 hours after ingestion... and it can be fatal." The source
frames prevention (knowing what's growing in one's own garden) as the primary defense, since
diagnosis and treatment depend on identifying the exact plant or fungus involved.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Swallowed poison: vomiting (sometimes blood-stained), nausea, diarrhea, impaired consciousness,
pain or burning, and visible empty containers as a scene clue. Food poisoning: nausea, cramping,
abdominal pain, blood-stained diarrhea, headache, fever, shock, impaired consciousness. Plant/fungi
poisoning: nausea, diarrhea, and — for certain mushrooms — hallucinations.
