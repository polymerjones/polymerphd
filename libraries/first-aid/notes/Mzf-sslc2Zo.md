# Heat Exhaustion and Heat Stroke | Adult First Aid | EMT First Aid Training

## Central claim

**Heat exhaustion (body temperature 38°C and above — hot, sweaty, distressed) is managed with
shade, water, and calm; heat stroke (around 40°C, where the "thermostat" has failed and sweating
has stopped) is a medical emergency requiring immediate cooling — whole-body cold-water immersion
preferred, then a cold shower/hose, then ice packs at the neck/armpits/groin — while emergency
services are called.** `[02:01]` "Heat stroke... this is where the body's thermostat fails due to
the extreme temperature. The most obvious sign of heat stroke is they're no longer sweating, the
skin actually becomes dry."

## The cooling-method priority order

`[02:01]` The source gives a specific fallback order for cooling a heat stroke patient while
waiting for help: whole-body immersion from the neck down in water between 1 and 26°C is preferred;
if unavailable, a cold shower or garden hose; if that's also unavailable, large bags of ice wrapped
in a towel placed at the neck, armpits, and groin, or wet towels — with the caveat to swap a wet
towel for a dry one once the patient has cooled, to avoid then overcooling them.

## Febrile convulsions: a heat-related complication in children

`[03:02]`–`[04:03]` The source connects heat regulation failure to a specific childhood
emergency: "approximately three in every 100 children have at least one convulsion in their
lifetime" due to fever, because "a child's brain is not fully developed." A febrile convulsion can
also follow an injection or illness-driven temperature rise, not only outdoor heat exposure. Stated
treatment is identical to any seizure — protect, don't restrain — while also working to lower the
child's temperature, with escalation to emergency services "if ever you're in doubt."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Heat exhaustion: hot, sweaty skin, increased breathing rate, distress. Heat stroke: dry skin (no
longer sweating) as the key escalation sign. Febrile convulsion: seizure-like convulsion in a
child, usually lasting under a minute but potentially up to five, that can look like — but the
source distinguishes from — an epileptic seizure.

## Analogies worth reusing

The body's temperature regulation described as an "automatic thermostat" that adjusts circulation,
heart rate, shivering, and sweating — used as the video's framing device for why heat exhaustion
and heat stroke represent that system failing under extreme conditions. `[00:00]`
