# Sprains and Strains | Adult First Aid | EMT First Aid Training

## Central claim

**A first aider doesn't need to distinguish a sprain from a strain — both are soft-tissue
injuries treated identically with the RICE protocol (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevate) — but should
always recommend professional follow-up in case a fracture is also present.** `[01:01]` "You don't
need to know the difference between a sprain or a strain, you just need to be able to recognize it
as a soft tissue injury and treat it... the treatment is RICE: ice, comfortable compression, and
elevate."

## RICE, step by step

`[02:03]`–`[03:04]` **Rest**: stop the activity, get comfortable. **Ice**: a shake-activated cold
compress can go directly on skin, but ice from a freezer must be wrapped in a towel first ("because
you don't want to put ice directly onto skin... it can burn"), held for a maximum of 20 minutes,
checking circulation past the injury site every 10 minutes. **Compression**: a crepe bandage wrapped
for comfortable, not tight, pressure. **Elevate**: raising the limb to reduce swelling.

## Why follow-up matters even after RICE

`[03:04]` The source is explicit about the limits of first aid here: **"you should always be
encouraging them to go and seek medical help because it might have actually fractured something —
we are not an x-ray machine, we don't actually know what's going on."**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Pain, swelling, difficulty moving the injured part, and bruising (which the source notes "takes
time to actually appear," so its absence early on doesn't rule out injury).

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Ligament vs. tendon** — a ligament attaches bone to bone; a tendon attaches muscle to bone.
  Given as background rather than something the first aider needs to act on differently. `[00:00]`

## Analogies worth reusing

None distinct — RICE is presented as a direct mnemonic rather than a metaphor.

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as direct first-aid instruction, illustrated with the presenter's own hamstring-tear
anecdote (sustained doing "the splits" in a costume) — a personal example, not a cited clinical
source.
