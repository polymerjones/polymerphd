# Adult Choking 2025 Update – First Aid Techniques Explained

## Central claim

**A complete (severe) airway obstruction — silent, struggling, unable to breathe — is treated with
alternating cycles of up to five back slaps and up to five abdominal thrusts, continued until the
blockage clears or the casualty becomes unconscious, at which point the response shifts to checking
breathing and starting CPR.** `[02:01]` "We're going to do up to five back slaps... if that's not
working, we then need to go around the back of the casualty. And this is when we're going to do
abdominal thrusts... we keep going up until two points. One, if it does come out, or two, if they
drop to the floor unconscious."

## Recognizing a complete obstruction, and a hidden-danger scenario

`[01:00]` Signs listed: hands around the throat, gasping without noise, wide/panicked eyes, and a
pale-to-blue skin color as oxygen drops. A specific hidden-risk scenario is flagged: someone
embarrassed at a table may quietly get up and leave "and that's when they might go and collapse and
there's nobody there to go and help them" — the source's stated advice is to follow anyone who
gets up abruptly and silently in this situation.

## Technique detail: back slaps and abdominal thrusts

`[02:01]`–`[03:02]` Back slaps: "up to five back slaps using the heel of your hand aiming between
the casualty's shoulder blades," with the rescuer's other arm braced across the casualty's chest
since the slaps can rock them forward. Abdominal thrusts: thumb tucked inside a closed fist, that
knuckle placed "underneath the person's rib cage, underneath their sternum, and above their belly
muscles," thrusting to push the diaphragm upward and expel the blockage with the escaping air.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Partial obstruction: coughing, ability to make noise. Complete obstruction: silence, gasping
without sound, hands at the throat, panicked wide eyes, skin turning pale or blue.

## Glossary terms introduced

None new — "partial" and "complete" airway obstruction are used descriptively rather than as
defined technical terms.
