# How to help someone having a seizure | Adult First Aid | EMT First Aid Training

## Central claim

**A seizure's first-aid response is to protect rather than intervene physically: stay calm, clear
the area and cushion the head without restraining or holding the person, time the seizure, and once
it ends check breathing and place them in the recovery position — with escalation to an ambulance
governed by specific triggers (unknown history, over five minutes, repeated seizures without
regaining consciousness, or breathing stopping).** `[04:02]` "Never ever restrain the person, do not
put anything in their mouth, do not move them unless they're in danger, do not attempt to bring them
around, and do not give them anything to eat or drink until they are fully recovered."

## What a seizure is, and why "there's not a normal seizure"

`[00:00]` A seizure is described as "a sudden burst of excess electric activity in our brains
causing a temporary disruption in the normal messages passing between our brain cells." The source
is explicit that presentation varies by where in the brain the activity starts and how it spreads:
**"never think there's a normal seizure."** Named causes include head injury, hypo/hyperglycemia in
diabetics, drug or alcohol withdrawal, and — specifically in young children — fever-driven febrile
convulsions.

## The tonic-clonic pattern, as one example among many

`[01:00]`–`[02:00]` The tonic-clonic seizure is presented as the most common generalized type, in
two phases: the **tonic stage** (body goes rigid from strong muscle contraction, consciousness is
lost, the person falls), followed by the **clonic stage** (muscles repeatedly contract and relax,
producing visible shaking) lasting seconds to minutes. Afterward, the person "gradually regain[s]
consciousness but may still be confused" for a variable period, possibly with soreness, headache,
and a need to sleep.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Sudden loss of consciousness, a rigid arched-back body, noisy or difficult breathing, convulsions,
possible loss of bladder control, and post-seizure confusion or deep sleep. A possible pre-seizure
warning symptom is described — odd movements, clothing worn oddly, unusual smells, or a metallic
taste — flagged as inconsistent: "not everybody has these... most seizures commonly occur without
any warning whatsoever." (The caption transcribes the name for this as "orders," which is almost
certainly a mishearing of the clinical term "auras" — see the note on this in Glossary terms.)

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Tonic stage** — the rigid, muscle-contracted phase of a tonic-clonic seizure, preceding loss of
  consciousness and falling. `[01:00]`
- **Clonic stage** — the convulsive phase following the tonic stage, where muscles repeatedly
  contract and relax. `[02:00]`
- **Febrile convulsion** — a seizure in a young child caused by a rapid rise in body temperature
  that "the brain can't deal with... just yet." `[01:00]`
- No new named clinical term is confidently introduced here: the source's caption reads "orders"
