# Snake Bites | Health and Safety | EMT First Aid Training

## Central claim

**Most snake bites are treated by staying calm, keeping the bitten limb still, and getting to
hospital fast — with a long list of things NOT to do (no ice, no tourniquet, no cutting or
sucking, no commercial snake-bite kit) — while a pressure-immobilization bandage is reserved
strictly for a known bite from a neurotoxic/elapid snake, and is never a substitute for or
equivalent to a tourniquet.** `[05:07]` "No first aid is often less harmful than bad first aid,
especially when it comes to snake bites."

## Why the common myths make things worse

`[04:07]`–`[05:07]` The source explains the mechanism behind each prohibited action: ice
"constrict[s]" small blood vessels, which "can produce dramatic tissue damage faster"; a
tourniquet keeps venom "concentrated near the bite," which "rapidly destroy[s] cells," whereas
allowing venom to spread and dilute "will... likely reduce the tissue damage"; aspirin or
ibuprofen "can make the bleeding a lot worse." Chasing, catching, or killing the snake risks "two
bites... to deal with."

## The pressure-immobilization bandage: strictly conditional

`[06:08]`–`[08:10]` This technique is explicitly gated: **"only going to be performed in the event
of a known bite from one of these neurotoxic... species and it should never be performed on snake
bites that are not part of this species, such as the rattlesnake."** Applied from below the bite
upward, over clothing, with sprained-ankle-level pressure, then splinted — and the source stresses
it cannot be removed outside a hospital setting with anti-venom ready, since removal causes "a
massive surge of venom into the bloodstream" and rapid decline. The closing instruction is
explicit: "if you don't understand what I've just said, do not apply — seek medical help quickly."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Two puncture wounds, swelling, redness, and pain at the site; difficulty breathing; vomiting and
nausea; blurred vision; sweating and salivating; numbness in the face and limbs — with a note that
different snake species produce different specific symptom patterns, so recording how the casualty
feels over time (every 30 minutes) helps doctors work out what bit them even without a visual
identification.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Pressure-immobilization bandage** — a broad compression bandage applied from below a bite
  upward, used only for confirmed neurotoxic/elapid snake bites to slow venom spread without
  cutting off circulation like a tourniquet would. `[06:08]`
