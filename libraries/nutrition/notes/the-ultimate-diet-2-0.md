# The Ultimate Diet 2.0

*This is a bodybuilding/physique-preparation text (self-published, 2003) by the same author as
`` `the-stubborn-fat-solution` `` (2008), which repeatedly cites this earlier book for background it
considers already established. Like that book, it is explicit throughout about which claims are
research-backed versus the author's own test-subject-driven design choices (see Source-stated
confidence below). Citations use `[p.N]` against the book's own page numbers.*

## Central claim

**Genetically average, drug-free trainees cannot simultaneously lose fat and build muscle using
standard steady-state diet and training advice, because fat loss and muscle gain require opposite
physiological states — but a carefully timed weekly cycle of glycogen depletion, low-carbohydrate
dieting, and engineered carbohydrate/training refeeds can let such a trainee approximate, for short
periods, what genetically gifted or drug-assisted athletes get for free.** `[p.16]`–`[p.18]` "The
main problem I have with the standard advice is that it's just so standard... only a small portion
of the people who try actually achieve their goals using the standard advice. That tells me that,
standard or not, it's not effective." `[p.9]`

## Why it's hard: evolution, genetics, and drugs

The opening argument is explicitly evolutionary: **the body "hates you" (really: loves you) by
defending fat mass and limiting muscle mass**, because historically both fat storage and modest
muscle were survival-favorable while excess muscle was metabolically wasteful. `[p.11]`–`[p.12]`
Lean individuals reportedly die of starvation in as little as 60 days versus 6+ months for obese
individuals, framed as direct evidence that "extreme leanness is generally incompatible with
survival." `[p.12]` The book is blunt that professional bodybuilders' results are not attainable
advice: **"All professional bodybuilders (and most athletes) use drugs. Anyone who says
differently is lying or trying to sell you something."** `[p.13]` A specific contrast is drawn between
the hormone profile of a natural dieter at 5% body fat (testosterone, thyroid, and IGF-1 all
"bottomed out," cortisol and appetite "through the roof") and a drug-assisted bodybuilder who
replaces each of those systems pharmacologically — "the drug using bodybuilder has completely
shut the door in the face of his normal physiology while the natural bodybuilder is basically
[stuck]." `[p.14]`–`[p.15]`

## Partitioning and the P-ratio

**Partitioning** — where calories go when eaten in excess, or come from when restricted — is
named as the real mechanism behind who succeeds on standard advice and who doesn't. `[p.19]`
The **P-ratio** (the proportion of protein vs. fat gained/lost during over/underfeeding) is
described as roughly fixed per individual and only 15-20% modifiable by diet/training; its
strongest known predictor is starting body fat percentage — leaner individuals lose relatively more
muscle when dieting and gain relatively more muscle when overfeeding, fatter individuals the
reverse. `[p.19]`–`[p.20]` Two mechanisms are given for why leanness itself degrades partitioning:
declining fatty-acid availability forces more reliance on protein for fuel, and **leptin** (produced
by fat cells roughly in proportion to both body fat and recent glucose intake) falls sharply on a
diet, triggering an integrated hypothalamic response (via NPY, CRH, POMC and other
neurochemicals) that lowers thyroid-stimulating hormone, LH/FSH, GH-releasing hormone, and
sympathetic output while raising cortisol and hunger. `[p.21]`–`[p.23]` A summary table `[p.27]`
lists roughly a dozen hormones (testosterone, GH, IGF-1, thyroid, catecholamines, cortisol,
leptin, ghrelin) each moving in opposite directions under overfeeding ("systemically anabolic")
versus underfeeding ("systemically catabolic") — the book's stated rationale for cycling between
the two rather than picking one and holding it constant.

## Fat cell mechanics (shared foundation with the author's later book)

The three-step lipolysis model (mobilization via HSL/cAMP, transport via adipose tissue blood
flow, oxidation via carnitine palmityl transferase) and the alpha-2/beta-2 adrenoceptor mechanism
for why women's hip/thigh fat and men's ab/low-back fat are especially resistant — "women's lower
bodyfat (hips and thighs) have been found to have 9 times as many alpha-2 receptors as beta-2
receptors" `[p.35]` — are presented here in essentially the same form later expanded in
`` `the-stubborn-fat-solution` ``. This book adds that **lowering carbohydrate to ≤20% of calories
naturally inhibits alpha-2 receptors after 3-4 days of elevated blood fatty acids**, without needing
yohimbine `[p.48]`, and frames a low-carbohydrate/ketogenic diet as mimicking fasting's blood-flow
benefits to fat cells specifically because a true fast would cause unacceptable muscle loss.
`[p.48]`

## Ketosis: a side effect, not the goal

In a direct contrast with Duchaine's Bodyopus (which pursued ketosis as fast as possible),
McDonald argues **ketosis itself has little independent benefit** once protein intake is adequate:
claimed protein-sparing and "metabolic inefficiency" effects of ketones are described as real but
minor, and "just hasn't shown up in the real world with lean athletes and bodybuilders." `[p.49]`–
`[p.50]` The diet's carbohydrate floor is set at 50g/day (not zero) specifically because "there
doesn't seem to be any major benefit to going lower" once the goal is reframed as lowering insulin
and raising catecholamines rather than achieving deep ketosis. `[p.54]`

## Muscle growth mechanics

Two forms of hypertrophy are distinguished: **myofibrillar** (growth of the contractile fibers
themselves, driven by high tension/heavy loading) and **sarcoplasmic** (growth of glycogen,
water, and supporting cell machinery, driven by glycogen depletion and repletion). `[p.39]` Growth
is modeled mechanistically: a tension stimulus upregulates messenger RNA and ribosome activity
in the muscle cell, but **the elevated protein synthesis from a single workout is said to decay
within about 36 hours**, offered as support for training a muscle more frequently than the
traditional once-weekly bodybuilding split. `[p.40]`–`[p.41]` Three training "disciplines" are
distinguished: **volume/pump training** (high reps, short rest — depletes glycogen, raises GH via
lactate, mobilizes fat, but rarely builds much muscle in natural trainees) `[p.42]`–`[p.44]`;
**tension training** (moderate reps/rest, near failure — upregulates ribosome activity) `[p.44]`–
`[p.45]`; and **power/strength training** (low reps, long rest, compound lifts — delivers the actual
myofibrillar growth signal once glycogen and ribosome activity are primed) `[p.45]`–`[p.46]`. The
week's structure exists specifically to sequence these three in order.

## The seven-day cycle

| Day | Training | Diet |
|---|---|---|
| Mon/Tue | Full-body high-rep/short-rest depletion workouts (~10-12 sets/bodypart, 60% 1RM) | Low-carb (≤20% cal, 50-100g), calories cut ~50% of maintenance for max fat loss |
| Wed | Rest | Same low-carb/low-cal as Mon/Tue |
| Thu AM/PM | Optional cardio, then evening full-body high-intensity workout (2-4 sets/bodypart) preceded by a 25-30g carb/15g whey "priming" feed | Low-carb until midday (75% of usual calories), then carb-load begins that evening |
| Fri | Rest | Carb-load continues |
| Sat | Full-body heavy "power" workout (3-6 sets of 3-6 reps, compound lifts, alternate-supersetted, long rests) | ~4-5 g/kg lean mass carbs, ~1 g/lb protein, low fat — roughly a standard 60/25/15 bodybuilding diet |
| Sun | Rest (optional late cardio) | Tapering carbs through the day; low-carb by evening to begin the next cycle |

`[p.53]`–`[p.70]` The book gives an explicit, numbered rationale for why the cycle is 7 rather than
the original Ultimate Diet's 10 or Bodyopus's 7-with-different-structure: workweek convenience,
and preserving Friday/Saturday night as normal-eating social time. `[p.51]` Saturday morning is
offered as a built-in diagnostic: waking up "full and lean, with no bloatedness" indicates a
well-executed carb-load, while bloat indicates too much total carbohydrate or too much
sucrose/fructose specifically. `[p.67]`

## Variations for different goals

The book gives modified schedules for: dieters who dislike Thursday's split low/high-carb day
(shifts the whole cycle one day later) `[p.71]`–`[p.72]`; mass gain (higher calories and 100g/day
carbs even on "low-carb" days, a traditional two-way split replacing full-body depletion workouts,
maintenance+10% on weekends) `[p.72]`–`[p.73]`; and endurance athletes, explicitly citing
contemporary "block training" research showing that 2-3 high-intensity days followed by several
easy days (which the weekly structure already resembles) can improve performance. `[p.73]`–
`[p.74]` An 8-day version is offered as the author's preferred structure in the abstract ("I'd be
happier with an 8 day cycle") — 4 strict diet days, 2 carb-load days, 2 normal days, with a full 48
hours between the priming and power workouts — acknowledged as impractical for anyone who
can't decouple training from a Monday-Friday work week. `[p.75]`

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- Nausea, lightheadedness, and muscular burning during the low-carbohydrate high-rep depletion
  workouts, described as expected and even necessary ("if you don't feel all those sensations and
  more, you're not working hard enough") `[p.56]`–`[p.57]`
- Mental fog/"brain dead" feeling from very low carbohydrate intake in those new to the approach,
  with a suggested fallback of raising carbs to 100g/day `[p.54]`
- Feeling "flat, stringy and weak," unable to hold a pump, on the last low-carbohydrate day
  (Thursday), attributed to combined glycogen depletion and dehydration and flagged as the hardest
  day psychologically `[p.59]`
- Loss of libido/erectile function in men and loss of menstrual cycle in women at very low body fat,
  attributed to the same leptin-driven hypothalamic shutdown described above `[p.23]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Partitioning** — where ingested or mobilized calories go (muscle vs. fat), the book's central
  organizing concept. `[p.19]`
- **P-ratio** — the proportion of protein (vs. fat) gained or lost during over/underfeeding. `[p.19]`
- **Myofibrillar hypertrophy** vs. **sarcoplasmic hypertrophy** — growth of contractile fiber
  protein vs. growth of the cell's energetic/supporting machinery (glycogen, water). `[p.39]`
- **Glycogen supercompensation** — the above-normal glycogen (and associated water) storage that
  follows a depletion workout, used here as a deliberate anabolic and fat-mobilization tool.
  `[p.43]`
- **Alpha-2/beta-2 adrenoceptors** and **adipose tissue blood flow (ATBF)** — see the fat-cell
  mechanics shared with `` `the-stubborn-fat-solution` `` above. `[p.34]`–`[p.36]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Your body hates you" (i.e., loves you too well)**: used to frame every homeostatic
  fat-defending mechanism in the book as evolutionarily rational self-preservation rather than
  malfunction. `[p.11]`
- **Genetic elite bodybuilders as a different physiological "species"** — repeatedly contrasted
  against "the genetically average" reader as a way to pre-empt the objection "but pro bodybuilders
  do X." `[p.13]`
- **A one-two "growth punch"**: the Thursday high-intensity workout (priming ribosomes/mRNA) paired
  with the Saturday power workout (delivering the actual tension stimulus once glycogen and
  ribosome activity are elevated) — used to explain why the week can't simply repeat one workout
  style. `[p.51]`

## Source-stated confidence

As in the author's later book, claims here are explicitly graded by evidentiary weight:
- Human-relevant fat cell physiology (adrenoceptor ratios, blood flow differences) is presented as
  established via cited research, while the specific weekly cycle structure is explicitly labeled the
  author's own design, refined "after many years of testing, design and redesign" with test subjects
  rather than validated in a controlled trial. `[p.6]`
- Ketosis's claimed protein-sparing and metabolic-inefficiency benefits are explicitly walked back:
  "I still tend to doubt ketosis has any huge advantages in this regard. If it does, it simply hasn't
  shown up in real world experience." `[p.49]`
- The value of GH-boosting bedtime glutamine is explicitly hedged: "Whether it actually impacts on
  fat loss is unknown... It may do nothing, although it certainly won't hurt anything." `[p.55]`
- The book closes by naming its own approach a "sledgehammer" workaround rather than a genuine
  fix: "it doesn't fundamentally solve [partitioning problems], it just lets the genetically average
  side step them for a brief period." `[p.76]` A product plug (Leptigen, from a company the author
  discloses a friendship with) is flagged with "warning: shameless plug coming up" and "I always
  remain skeptical." `[p.76]`

## Conflicts with other sources

**Strong convergence** with `` `the-stubborn-fat-solution` `` by the same author (published five
years later): both describe the identical three-step lipolysis bottleneck and the same alpha-2/
beta-2 adrenoceptor explanation for stubborn ab/low-back (men) and hip/thigh (women) fat, down to
the same "9 times as many alpha-2 receptors" figure for women's lower-body fat. The later book
explicitly treats this one as prerequisite background ("If you've already read my Ultimate Diet
2.0, some of it will sound familiar") and defers several topics to it by name — the mechanism
behind accelerated end-of-diet muscle loss, and the general rationale for high-rep glycogen-depleting
training. The two books do not appear to contradict each other on any shared claim; where they
differ, it is in scope (this book covers muscle-growth mechanics and a full weekly training/diet
cycle that the later, more narrowly stubborn-fat-focused book does not re-derive) rather than in
substance.
