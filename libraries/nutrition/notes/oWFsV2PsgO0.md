# Are pork & chicken the new seed oils?

## Central claim

**Conventionally fed pork, chicken, and their eggs can carry as much omega-6 linoleic acid as seed
oils, because the animals are fed the same corn-and-soy inputs — and, more provocatively, one
speaker argues that omega-3 fats (fish oil, fatty fish) may be an even bigger version of the same
peroxidation problem.** `[01:00]` "Bacon is better than canola oil, bacon is better than soybean
oil... it's probably not a great thing to have the majority of your fat coming from lard, which is
going to be sometimes 15, 20% linoleic acid."

## Pork and chicken fat as an omega-6 source

`[00:00]`–`[02:02]` — The segment opens from an article the host describes as finding that
"conventional egg consumption increases the susceptibility of LDL to oxidation," attributed to
corn-and-soy chicken feed. He notes the caveat himself: **"a wild chicken is going to eat bugs and
grubs and not as many high-linoleic-acid grains — that egg is going to be much lower in linoleic
acid"** `[00:00]`. The second speaker extends the point to the animals' fat directly: **"chicken and
pork will be just as high in polyunsaturated fat, sometimes 30-plus percent"** as feed-grade seed
oils, since the same oils were originally used "just to fatten up the livestock" before being
marketed to humans `[01:00]`. His practical rule: if eating chicken or pork with the fat on, know
what it was fed; otherwise "stick to the lean ones" `[02:02]`.

## The Byron Bay counter-example

`[02:02]`–`[03:03]` — The host describes visiting a farm outside Byron Bay, Australia (a butcher
named Locavore) with pastured pigs moved daily in a portable house alongside Highland cattle,
"rooting around in the grass... getting bugs... getting worms," fed table scraps and **sprouted
wheat instead of corn and soy — "lower in linoleic acid than corn and soy."** He states this was
"the first pork that I had eaten in years" because of the linoleic-acid concern `[03:03]`.

## Omega-3s as "the next seed oil"

`[03:03]`–`[06:06]` — The second speaker pivots to fish oil, agreeing it is "overconsumed," and
lays out a mechanistic case: omega-3s have more double bonds than omega-6s, making them "more
susceptible to oxidation, not only when they're on the shelf but also when they're in our tissues"
`[04:04]`. He cites unnamed studies showing most fish oil is "already oxidized on the shelf... in
levels beyond what's supposed to be regulatory standards," and that digestion itself further
increases peroxidation, even for "pharmaceutical grade fish oil in the triglyceride form" or with
antioxidants added `[05:05]`. Referencing the "membrane pacemaker theory" of aging, he states that
**animal studies giving higher levels of omega-3s show shortened lifespan, increased lipid
peroxidation, and interference with mitochondrial respiration**, concluding "I think the omega-3s
are next... fish oil is the next seed oil" `[06:06]`. His closing practical guidance: if eating
seafood, lean toward leaner fish, lower in omega-3, over fatty fish `[06:06]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None described as a bodily symptom in this segment — LDL oxidation susceptibility, lipid
peroxidation, and mitochondrial respiration are discussed as lab/mechanistic markers, not felt
symptoms.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Membrane pacemaker theory (of aging)** — invoked to connect a tissue's polyunsaturated fat
  content to its rate of oxidative damage and, by extension, lifespan `[04:04]`–`[05:05]`.
- **"Ioso"** — the source's own (likely caption-garbled) term for the differing signaling
  byproducts that omega-3 and omega-6 fats each produce, offered as the reason the two are
  conventionally viewed as anti-inflammatory versus inflammatory, respectively `[04:04]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None distinct — the argument proceeds by direct percentage comparisons (bacon versus canola oil,
lard's linoleic-acid percentage versus canola's) rather than through a stated analogy.

## Source-stated confidence

The second speaker repeatedly self-qualifies: "I agree that it's overconsumed" `[03:03]`, and
frames the omega-3-shortens-lifespan claim through animal studies specifically, without asserting
the same has been shown in humans `[06:06]`. The host's own linoleic-acid claims about pork and
chicken fat are stated as settled ("that's just as much a source of omega-6s... as the seed oils"),
though the originating article is described rather than named `[00:00]`. The omega-3-as-next-seed-
oil framing is presented as this speaker's own extrapolation ("I think"), not as consensus science
`[06:06]`.
