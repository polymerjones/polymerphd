# How to shop for extra virgin olive oil!

## Central claim

**Citing UC Davis's Olive Center, the host argues most olive oil — both imported (often cut with
seed oils) and California-produced (often rancid or oxidized) — isn't what it claims to be, and
gives four buying criteria for finding a genuine one: organic, cold pressed, a visible harvest
date, and dark glass bottling — while still stating a general preference for animal fats over olive
oil regardless.** `[00:00]` "The UC Davis Center on Olive Oil has actually looked at this and has
found that the vast majority of imported olive oils and olive oils produced in California are not
that healthy."

## Two distinct problems, one study

`[00:00]` — **Imported olive oil**: often "cut with vegetable oils, seed oils like corn, canola,
soybean, sunflower" — adulteration rather than a manufacturing defect. **California olive oil**: a
separate problem — frequently "rancid, oxidized, and essentially spoiled" even without seed-oil
adulteration. Both findings are attributed to the same source, the **UC Davis Center on Olive Oil**
(officially the UC Davis Olive Center), making this a more specifically sourced claim than most in
this library.

## Buying criteria

`[00:00]` — For readers who still want olive oil: **organic, cold pressed, a visible harvest date
on the label, and packaged in dark glass** (to limit light-driven oxidation). The host reiterates
his own preference for **tallow, butter, or ghee** even for something like a salad.

## Source-stated confidence

Moderate — the UC Davis Olive Center is a real, named, checkable institution, which is stronger
sourcing than an unnamed "study." The specific figures behind "vast majority" (what percentage,
what year, what testing method) aren't given in this short.

## Conflicts with other sources

`JpW1utNfQM0` ("Avoid These Olive Oils") covers a related but distinct concern — brand-specific
phthalate contamination from plastic contact during pressing and storage — rather than seed-oil
adulteration or rancidity. The two sources are complementary: this short's UC Davis citation
concerns purity and freshness, while the longer video's independent analysis concerns a different
contaminant (phthalates) entirely. Both agree olive oil quality varies enormously by brand/source
and both name animal fats (tallow, butter, ghee) as the host's preferred alternative.
