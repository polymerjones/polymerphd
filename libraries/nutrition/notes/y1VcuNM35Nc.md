# Are we being deceived about grass-finished labeling?

## Central claim

**Most cheap retail "grass-fed" meat is fed grass-like pellets rather than raised on open pasture,
making the label technically true but practically misleading — the speakers call this
"greenwashing," and describe building their own meat stick around verified, regeneratively raised
beef specifically to avoid it.** `[03:06]` "There is no possible way that these are truly grass,
grass finish, the way people think they are" `[02:05]`.

## What "greenwashing" looks like in practice

`[03:06]`–`[05:08]` — Cheap grass-fed ground meat or meat sticks, sourced from "Brazil... the US...
other countries," are described as "actually the same thing as a grain fed feed lot": cattle are
fed "alfalfa pellets... grasses that have not gone to seed yet, pushed into a pellet, added with
preservatives." A cited but unnamed source ("Stefan van..." — the name is garbled in the
transcript) is said to have found **"synthetic forms of the actual vitamins and minerals" added to
these pellets in tested grass-fed meat**, allowing producers to legally claim "grass-fed" because
the feed is not "technical corn and soy" `[04:08]`. The claim that follows is stated flatly: large
producers are "gaming the system," and "nobody looks into their supply chain" `[04:08]`.

## Verifying supply, the hard way

`[04:08]`–`[06:10]` — The speakers describe asking prospective suppliers two things: information
about farming practices, and permission to visit the farm. **The response rate: "about a 90% no,"**
across repeated requests for a signed affidavit of practices `[05:08]`–`[06:10]`. Two years and
(inconsistently stated as) "50 trials" and "54 Trials" of input sourcing are cited for this specific
product. The same standard is applied to a separate organ-capsule product, where Chilean and
Argentinian organ sources are said to produce lower-quality effects than the sourcing used here —
stated as an observation, without a named comparison study `[06:10]`.

## Air-dried versus cured versus cooked

`[09:13]`–`[12:16]` — Curing (as in salami) is described as requiring "a massive amount of...
different types of salt... curing salt... then different preservatives." Cooking (as in jerky) is
distinguished from both. This product is air-dried for five days at a temperature that "doesn't go
above 78°F," in an air tunnel, explicitly framed as reviving a preservation method "people have
done... for thousands of years." **The product is reported to have passed "accelerated testing" out
to twelve-plus months with no spoilage, rancidity, or microbial growth, avoiding nitrates, nitrites,
celery powder, lactic acid, and black pepper** — the last three named as personal avoidances rather
than food-safety requirements `[10:14]`–`[12:16]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

A personal, explicitly hedged anecdote: eating cured or preserved meat products (particularly while
traveling) is described as producing **"that classic sort of like really thirsty, dry kind of like
high, almost high histamine, aching in the joints feeling"** — a sensitivity the speaker says
intensified "especially after I had mold [exposure]" `[12:16]`. This is offered as personal
pattern-recognition ("I had a radar"), not a general claim, and is paired with an unverified,
secondhand testimonial from a friend reporting "crazy energy" after eating the product
`[13:17]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Greenwashing (applied to meat)** — defined here as "grass pellet fed cows that are called
  grass-fed," as opposed to cattle actually raised on pasture `[04:08]`.
- **Air drying (as distinct from curing and cooking)** — a preservation method the speakers say
  retains "critical enzymes" that stabilize the meat without requiring the salt loads or chemical
  preservatives that curing needs `[10:14]`–`[11:16]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The Tesla Roadster-to-Model-3 analogy** — building the highest-quality, most expensive version
  of a product first, then working toward affordability, rather than starting cheap and
  compromising from the outset `[07:10]`–`[08:11]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Low to moderate, and largely promotional in tone given the speakers' ownership stake in the product
being described. The core "greenwashing" claim is stated with high confidence ("there is no
possible way") `[02:05]` but supported only by an unnamed cited researcher and an internally
reported "90% no" survey with no published methodology given in speech. **The personal
symptom anecdote is the most carefully hedged claim in the video** — framed explicitly as a
self-observed pattern rather than a general health claim `[12:16]`–`[13:17]`.

## Conflicts with other sources

Converges thematically with `2KMaKJqU5Gs` on the underlying concern that a "grass-fed" or
"grass-finished" label does not guarantee the animal was actually raised the way a buyer assumes —
this video's pellet-feeding "greenwashing" claim and `2KMaKJqU5Gs`'s point that most retail beef is
still grain-finished (with associated mold-toxin exposure) both push toward the same practical
advice: verify the farm rather than trust the label. Neither video cites the other, and they focus
on different specific hazards (label-gaming here, mycotoxin exposure there). No other source in
this library discusses air-drying as a preservation method or addresses meat-stick or organ-capsule
sourcing specifically.
