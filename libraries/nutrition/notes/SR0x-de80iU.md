# Bottled Water Brands To AVOID at All Costs (And 2 That Won't Poison You)

## Central claim

**After sending eight popular bottled water brands (five glass, three plastic) to an independent
lab for heavy metals, radioactive elements, and microplastics, two glass waters — Icelandic
Glacial and Voss — tested essentially clean, while Evian tested worst, with the highest uranium in
the panel plus detectable aluminum and BPA** `[00:01]`, `[15:20]`. The source frames the stakes with
a volume argument: **"the average American drinks 365 L of bottled water... per year... over the
course of your life, that's over 10,000 plus liters"** `[02:03]`.

## The lab-tested ranking

`[06:09]`–`[15:20]` — Nine bottles were tested (`[03:05]`), but Smartwater's plastic sample was
dropped from the final ranking mid-video without further explanation ("we don't have a Smartwater
in... we'll just take it out" `[06:09]`), leaving eight ranked places:

| Rank | Brand (container) | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (gold) | Icelandic Glacial (glass) | No heavy metals, no titanium, no uranium, no bromoforms `[10:14]` |
| 2 (silver) | Voss (glass) | Clean on heavy metals; some independent tests suggest radium `[09:13]`–`[10:14]` |
| 3 (bronze) | Mountain Valley (glass) | Clean in this test, but arsenic varied across other reports (1.1 ppb in 2019, 0.16 ppb in 2025) and bromoforms suggest municipal-source mixing `[07:10]`–`[08:12]` |
| 4 | Acqua Panna (glass) | No heavy metals, but 0.9 ppb uranium `[06:09]` |
| 5 | Saratoga (glass) | No heavy metals or uranium, but bromoforms/trihalomethanes suggest municipal mixing `[07:10]` |
| 6 | Deer Park (plastic) | No metals or uranium detected, but plastic packaging brings undetected microplastics `[11:15]` |
| 7 | Fiji (plastic) | Arsenic at 0.9 ppb; the only water in the test with PFAS `[14:19]` |
| 8 (worst) | Evian (plastic) | Highest uranium (1.9 ppb), over 400 ppb aluminum, and detectable BPA `[15:20]` |

## Microplastics and nanoplastics

`[11:15]`–`[13:18]` — The source rebuts a recent study claiming glass bottled water has more
microplastics than plastic: "this study is bunk... it's only looking at large microplastic
fragments" greater than one micrometer `[11:15]`. University research at finer resolution has
found **"up to 250,000 nanoplastics"** per liter in plastic bottled water, particles 50–500
nanometers across `[12:16]`. These carry other contaminants: **"they can carry things that are
endocrine disruptors like PFAS... they can carry BPA and BPS like a Trojan horse"** `[13:18]`.

## Solutions offered

`[17:22]`–`[18:23]` — The source's own practice is reverse osmosis at home, remineralized: **"use a
reverse osmosis filter and remineralize it... probably better than even drinking bottled water"**
`[17:22]`. Remineralization is suggested via commercial mineral salts or "a pinch of sea salt"
`[18:23]`, with a forthcoming separate video testing sea salts for heavy metals referenced but not
detailed here.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

No direct patient symptoms are described; the video instead lists disease associations for each
contaminant tested — lead linked to "neurodevelopmental issues," mercury to "kidney issues,
cancers, hormonal disruption," arsenic as "a known carcinogen," cadmium to "cancers and kidney
issues," aluminum to "increased incidence of dementia," and uranium to cancer `[03:05]`–`[04:07]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Certificate of analysis** — the lab documentation the source says other consumer water-testing
  platforms don't fully publish, contrasted with his own promised full release `[01:02]`.
- **Forever chemicals (PFAS)** — perfluoroalkyl substances described as persistent, accumulating
  endocrine disruptors, found only in the Fiji sample `[13:18]`–`[14:19]`.
- **Nanoplastics versus microplastics** — nanoplastics are defined here as 50–500 nanometers, "20
  times smaller than a micrometer-size microplastic" `[12:16]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

The Olympic-medal framing for the ranking (gold/silver/bronze, and jokingly "the poo medal" for
last place) `[09:13]`, `[16:21]`. The kilogram-quantity argument: **"when you're drinking liters of
water, you're drinking kilograms of a substance"**, contrasted with the much smaller gram
quantities of most solid foods `[02:03]`.

## Source-stated confidence

The core ppb figures come from the source's own lab test, run through his nonprofit (Animal-Based
Nutrition Research Foundation) with certificates of analysis promised at his website `[01:02]`,
which gives those specific numbers a documented basis. Other claims are attributed to unspecified
outside sources without citation — the Mountain Valley arsenic figures and class-action lawsuit
are sourced to "a test from 2019" and "a test from 2025" without naming the testing body `[08:12]`,
and the Voss radium claim to **"some independent tests have suggested"** `[09:13]`, both softer
than the source's own lab numbers.

## Conflicts with other sources

Both this video and `Xr1Wkn1fj58` (2022) praise Mountain Valley's clean heavy-metal profile, but
with different confidence: the earlier video calls it **"pretty darn clean"** with radiologicals
"none detected" and treats it as a top recommendation, while this video's own lab test likewise
finds no heavy metals but ranks it only third of eight, citing arsenic variability in *other*
testing and suspected municipal-source mixing that the 2022 video does not mention — a narrowing of
confidence in the same brand over four years, not a reversal. Both videos are also skeptical of
Fiji, though for different specific reasons: `6tT1RQ_9-l8` and `Xr1Wkn1fj58` flag unspecified
"plastic contaminants," while this video's own lab test finds arsenic and PFAS specifically —
convergent conclusion, different evidence base. On remineralization, this video's default
recommendation to remineralize reverse-osmosis water is a stated shift from `6tT1RQ_9-l8`, where
the source explicitly did not remineralize his own RO water at the time.
