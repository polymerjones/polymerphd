# Why I don't eat fish, chicken or pork!

## Central claim

**The source avoids fish, chicken, and pork not for one shared reason but for a cluster of
distinct ones — PFAS, heavy metals, and microplastics in fish and shellfish, and linoleic-acid
bioaccumulation in corn-and-soy-fed chicken and pigs — and eats beef, bison, and lamb instead as
"the cleanest sources of food for humans on the planet."** `[01:00]`, `[51:45]`

## Reason one for avoiding fish: PFAS and hormones

`[03:03]`–`[06:05]` — The source opens with a 2012 paper in the *Journal of Toxicology and Applied
Pharmacology*, "Fluorochemicals used in food packaging inhibit male sex hormone synthesis," widely
used in food-contact paper and plastic-coated cardboard. In cell culture, these fluorochemicals
**decreased BZRP (benzodiazepine receptor protein) and increased CYP19 gene expression, "leading to
lower androgen[s]... and higher estrogen levels"** — described as mattering for both sexes' sex
drive, and for men's muscle mass and erectile function. The paper also notes fetal and infant
exposure through the placenta and breast milk. PFAS are then named as heavily present in fish and
shellfish specifically, which the source calls "just one reason" he avoids fish.

## Everyday PFAS exposure beyond fish

`[07:06]`–`[14:11]` — A wider PFAS audit follows: microwave popcorn bags, Starbucks and other
plastic-lined coffee cups, takeout paperboard, and nonstick cookware are all named as PFAS sources
per FDA-acknowledged food-contact uses. Citing a Harvard T.H. Chan-linked study (introduced with "who
I generally detest because they are so funded by vegan interests, but nevertheless we will cite them
here"), the source reports **women using Oral-B Glide floss had higher blood levels of PFHxS
(perfluorohexane sulfonic acid)** than non-users. A Consumer Reports test is cited finding PFAS in
several sparkling-water brands, including Topo Chico, Polar, Bubly, and LaCroix — with the caveat
that "all the brands tested were compliant with current standards."

## Fish and PFAS, more directly

`[15:12]`–`[19:16]` — A 2017 *Environmental Research* paper is cited finding **seafood consumption
is the major source of PFAS exposure in the general population**, associated with thyroid disease
and dyslipidemia; the source flags this as "observational epidemiology" but argues the correlation
is "pretty hard to explain away in other ways." A Faroe Islands children's cohort study is cited
showing PFAS levels declining over time in a seafood-eating population, though still elevated
relative to non-seafood populations, because the compounds are "environmentally persistent and
persistent in the body."

## Reason two: heavy metals in fish

`[21:16]`–`[26:21]` — Citing a 2017 review, the source lists iron, manganese, cobalt, nickel,
copper, zinc, chromium, lead, mercury, arsenic, and cadmium as metals found in fish, framing ocean
pollution as making this essentially unavoidable: **"it's basically the equivalent of eating a cow
that is grown in downtown Tokyo."** He cites Tony Robbins's and Joe Rogan's public accounts of
elevated mercury and arsenic from pescetarian diets and sardine consumption respectively, and states
he has personally observed clients' mercury levels rise from eating wild salmon "just a few times a
week." Tuna, mahi, swordfish, and opah are singled out as especially high in mercury. A second cited
review, on heavy metals in Bosnia and Herzegovina seafood, recommends **pregnant women limit tuna and
mackerel specifically.**

## Reason three: microplastics

`[27:21]`–`[32:28]` — A *Nature* review is cited noting microplastics have been found in human
placentas. Two mouse studies are described in detail: one found **polystyrene microplastics caused
"testicular inflammation, disruption of the blood-testis barrier," and reproductive dysfunction**;
another found polyethylene microplastics shifted gut microbiota (increased Staph, decreased
Parabacteroides), raised interleukin-1-alpha, decreased Th17/Treg cell percentages, and produced
visible small-intestine and colon inflammation with elevated TLR4 expression. The source connects
this to leaky-gut mechanisms he has discussed with lectins in prior podcasts. He also tested his own
sea salt options for visible microplastic contamination, reporting a blog's particle counts across
brands (Pacific sea salt highest at 22–51 particles; Redmond Real Salt and Himalayan salt among the
lower counts) and states he personally uses a Kalima salt tested clean under microscope.

## Getting omega-3s and iodine without fish

`[33:29]`–`[38:34]` — The source states he takes **no fish oil**, calling it "absolute [garbage]" in
his opinion, because it is oxidized. He argues most people can convert ALA (alpha-linolenic acid)
into DHA adequately from animal fat (butter, tallow, suet, ghee, occasional egg yolks) **when
polyunsaturated fat intake stays under roughly 3% of energy — consistent, he says, with what's seen
in hunter-gatherer populations like the Hadza.** On iodine, he states egg yolks, dairy, and muscle
meat provide plenty, and that he has "never seen someone eating reasonable amounts of meat get iodine
deficient" absent something actively blocking thyroid uptake — naming isothiocyanates in brassica
vegetables (broccoli, kale, collards, cabbage, chard) as one such blocker.

## Chicken: a "weak bird"

`[38:34]`–`[47:40]` — Quoting his own prior statement made on a podcast appearance (transcribed here
as "Pavel Sausalin," almost certainly the source's own name garbled): **"chicken is a weak bird"** —
his argument being that a hunter-gatherer would never choose to hunt a small, lean bird over a
bison or eland when calorically it makes little sense. He explains chickens and pigs, like humans,
are monogastric and lack the ruminant capacity to "saturate" (remove double bonds from)
polyunsaturated fat, so whatever they eat accumulates in their tissue. He cites a friend, Anthony
Gustin, raising chickens outside Austin who found **"all of the chicken feeds you could find contain
seed oils"** with no widely available alternative. A semi-wild turkey from Rome Ranch, mostly
foraging for bugs with some grain supplementation, is offered as "the closest that I could get to a
monogastric bird that is going to be evolutionarily consistent with his diet." He cites a 2004
*Poultry Science* study and a *Scientific Reports* laying-hen study, both showing that feed
composition directly changes the polyunsaturated fat content of chicken meat, fat, and egg yolks.

## Pork

`[47:40]`–`[51:45]` — The same monogastric logic is applied to pigs, "probably even a bigger problem
than chickens because pigs are fattier." He cites a paper on the Tokelau atolls (natural experiment
comparing saturated-fat intake to vascular disease) whose fatty-acid table shows **"wild" pork and
chicken fat from Tokelau at 2% and 2.5% linoleic acid respectively, versus 15–20%-plus in
conventionally fed pork and chicken today.** A second cited study on dietary PUFA and pig adipose
tissue composition confirms feed directly determines fat composition. He references a prior podcast
with Brad Marshall on low-linoleic-acid pig breeding, and states he will eat wild-killed pork (as he
did on a recent hunt) but not corn-and-soy-fed pork.

## What the source eats, and the scalability caveat

`[51:45]`–`[55:47]` — His stated diet: beef, bison when available, sometimes lamb — "ruminants are I
believe the cleanest sources of food for humans on the planet." On feeding the world this way, he
argues **"we don't have a calorie problem on this planet, we have a nutrient problem,"** citing
prior podcasts on vegan/plant-based diets being nutrient-poor for children, and states he believes
scaling should follow from first understanding what's optimal for individual humans, not the reverse.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Reduced sex drive, erectile dysfunction, and lower muscle mass** attributed to PFAS-driven
  lower androgen levels in both sexes `[04:04]`.
- **Delayed developmental effects and lower infant birth weight** from PFAS exposure via placenta
  and breastmilk `[04:04]`, `[12:09]`.
- **Thyroid disease and dyslipidemia** associated with PFAS from seafood consumption `[15:12]`.
- **Mercury toxicity presenting as elevated blood mercury** in real-world cases (Tony Robbins, the
  source's own clients on wild salmon) `[22:17]`.
- **Testicular inflammation and reproductive dysfunction** from microplastics, shown in mouse
  models `[27:21]`–`[28:24]`.
- **Gut inflammation and altered T-cell populations** from microplastic exposure, shown in mouse
  models `[29:24]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances)** — the "forever chemicals" family tracked throughout
  as coming from food packaging, cookware, floss, and seafood `[03:03]`.
- **BZRP (benzodiazepine receptor protein) and CYP19** — the two gene-expression targets the cited
  2012 study found altered by fluorochemical exposure, linked to lower androgen and higher estrogen
  production `[04:04]`.
- **Monogastric** — used throughout to describe chickens, pigs, and humans as single-stomached
  animals that store rather than metabolize excess dietary polyunsaturated fat, unlike ruminants
  `[41:37]`.
- **Isothiocyanates** — the brassica-vegetable compounds named as inhibiting thyroid iodine uptake
  `[37:34]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"It's basically the equivalent of eating a cow that is grown in downtown Tokyo"** — used to
  frame ocean pollution as making fish contamination essentially unavoidable `[21:16]`.
- **"If you swim with the fish with the current... you end up like all the other fish"** — used to
  argue that following mainstream dietary habits leads to the same poor health outcomes as everyone
  else `[13:11]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Most claims are backed by a named, described study (author or journal given, though rarely a full
citation read aloud). The fish-PFAS correlation is explicitly flagged as **"observational
epidemiology"** rather than causal proof, with the source arguing the correlation is nonetheless
"pretty hard to explain away." The microplastic-mouse studies are stated as showing effects "in
animal models" with the caveat "probably could also happen in human models" left as inference, not
fact. The salt microplastic comparison is sourced to "a blog reporting" test results, explicitly
lower-confidence than the peer-reviewed citations elsewhere in the video.
