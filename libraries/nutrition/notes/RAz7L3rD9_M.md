# The Best Way to Stay Hydrated (Not Water)

## Central claim

**Most people need about 2.3 liters (77 oz) of hydrating fluid a day, and it doesn't have to be
plain water — milk and orange juice are, per the source, "even more hydrating than water" — paired
with roughly 8–9 grams of salt to avoid overhydration.** `[00:00]`–`[01:02]`, `[03:05]`

## The daily fluid target and its timing

`[00:00]`–`[02:02]` — The source states body size doesn't change the target much: **"most humans...
need about 2.3 liters of water per day"**, roughly 77 oz or about 10 eight-ounce glasses `[00:00]`.
He recommends front-loading it: **"this is the amount of fluid that you should probably consume in
the first 10 hours of the day after you wake up"**, because kidneys filter more actively during
that window, reducing nighttime bathroom trips `[02:02]`. After 5 p.m. (for a 7 a.m. wake time), he
suggests limiting intake to "5 to 8 ounces of water before you go to sleep" `[02:02]`.

## Which fluids count

`[02:02]`–`[05:08]` — Milk and orange juice are presented as counting toward, and even exceeding,
plain water's hydration value: **"according to many studies like this one, milk and orange juice
are even more hydrating than water"** `[02:02]`. The source's own daily intake is described as
roughly a liter of raw milk plus a liter of fresh-squeezed orange juice or watermelon juice, with
"maybe only two glasses per day" being actual water `[04:05]`. Coffee and alcohol are excluded from
the hydration total: **"these don't really count the same way towards your intake of fluid because
alcohol and coffee can be dehydrating"** `[05:08]`. Tap water is explicitly avoided: **"I would not
drink water from a tap, from a sink, because of fluoride"** `[05:08]`.

## Salt and hyponatremia

`[06:09]`–`[09:12]` — Drinking the full fluid target without adequate salt risks **hyponatremia**:
**"if you just drink 10 glasses of water per day, you may be hydrated, but you could be
overhydrated"** `[06:09]`, with endurance events like an "Iron Man or a marathon" as the extreme
case. The recommended salt intake is **8–9 grams per day** (not just sodium, total salt), increased
for heavy sweating or a ketogenic diet: **"when you're on a ketogenic diet, your insulin can
actually drop too low and you will not hold on to electrolytes"** `[08:10]`, which the source
connects to his own history of muscle cramps on a long-term carnivore/ketogenic diet.

## Reading your own hydration status

`[10:14]`–`[11:16]` — Suggested markers include urine color (with the caveat that B vitamins can
make urine "fluorescent yellow" independent of hydration `[10:14]`) and urine specific gravity via
test strips, though the source flags disagreement on the threshold: **"sources disagree as to what
level of specific gravity signals dehydration... some sources say any specific gravity greater than
1.01... some sources say... 1.02"** `[11:16]`.

## Exercise hydration (the Galpin equation)

`[11:16]`–`[12:17]` — For exercise, the source cites **"the Galpin equation"** (attributed to Andy
Galpin): **"take your body weight in pounds, divide that by 30, and that is the number of ounces
you should be drinking... every 15 to 20 minutes while you're exercising"** `[11:16]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Nighttime urination interrupting sleep from late fluid intake `[02:02]`; dehydration signs (dark
urine color, skin tenting, urine specific gravity) `[10:14]`–`[11:16]`; hyponatremia from
overhydration during endurance exercise `[06:09]`–`[07:09]`; muscle cramps from poor electrolyte
retention on a long-term ketogenic diet `[08:10]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Hyponatremia** — dangerously low blood sodium from drinking large amounts of water without
  matching electrolyte intake, particularly during long endurance efforts `[06:09]`–`[07:09]`.
- **Galpin equation** — body weight in pounds ÷ 30 = ounces of fluid to drink per 15–20 minutes of
  exercise `[11:16]`.
- **Specific gravity** (as a urine hydration marker) — a test-strip measurement whose dehydration
  threshold the source says is disputed across sources `[11:16]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

Physically measuring out 77 oz / 2.3 L of water into glasses and jugs on camera as a concrete
visual for the daily fluid target `[00:00]`–`[01:02]`.

## Source-stated confidence

The claim that milk and orange juice are more hydrating than water is attributed to unspecified
"studies" without a named citation: **"according to many studies like this one"** `[02:02]`,
`[03:05]`. The urine specific-gravity threshold is explicitly flagged as contested rather than
settled: **"sources disagree as to what level... signals dehydration"** `[11:16]`.
