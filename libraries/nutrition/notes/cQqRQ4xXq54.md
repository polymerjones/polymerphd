# Attack of the Oxalates! A hidden but nasty plant toxin, with Sally K Norton, MPH

## Central claim

**Oxalate — a small, highly reactive molecule concentrated in foods widely assumed to be healthy
(spinach, almonds, sweet potatoes, peanuts) — can accumulate in the body over years and drive joint
pain, tissue pain, kidney stones, and even a documented death, and Norton's own decades of
unexplained arthritis, foot pain, and a severe sleep disorder resolved once she adopted a low-oxalate
diet.** `[03:02]`–`[04:02]` Her own words on the outcome: within six months of "really learning the
oxalate diet and doing it correctly," she went from thirty years unable to go barefoot to being
able to "stand on the very tips of my toes" `[03:02]`.

## What oxalate is, and how it forms crystals in the body

`[11:12]`–`[13:15]` — Oxalic acid is described as "a highly reactive chemical with two negative
charges" that readily bonds with positive minerals, most strongly calcium — "calcium... gets
transferred from being a nutrient to being pretty heavy duty toxin." Norton's analogy for the
mineral's reputation: blaming calcium for oxalate kidney stones is **"like getting in trouble for
marrying an alcoholic."** These crystals form in named shapes (raphides, styloids, druses); the
smallest, nano-sized crystals are compared directly to asbestos — "the literature equates oxalate
nanocrystals to... asbestos type... toxicity to the cells" — disrupting cell membranes and
mitochondrial function and triggering an immune/inflammatory response `[16:20]`–`[17:22]`.

## Why plants make it

`[20:25]`–`[23:29]` — Norton lists several plant uses for oxalate: generating hydrogen peroxide to
fight fungal mildew, "parking" calcium for use during germination, and forming needle-shaped
raphide crystals shot in bundles into the mouths of herbivores as active defense — she describes a
single drop of dumbcane (*Dieffenbachia*) sap causing days of mouth ulcers and swelling severe
enough to threaten breathing. Her framing: **"plants don't want to be eaten,"** and oxalate is one
of their weapons `[23:29]`.

## Which foods, and how much is too much

`[26:32]`–`[30:37]` — Norton names beans, all nuts (especially almonds), seeds (sesame, poppy),
potatoes, sweet potatoes, peanuts/peanut butter, Swiss chard, beet greens, and some kales as the
main dietary sources, while the cabbage family and most lettuces run low. A green smoothie with
three cups of spinach and a tablespoon of almond butter, she calculates, can carry about 1,000
milligrams of oxalate — "somewhere between 10 and at least five times" what may be a safe daily
amount. **Cooking does not neutralize the risk** — "cooking does not... protect you from oxalate" —
since roughly three-quarters of spinach's oxalate is soluble and unaffected by heat, though
extended boiling (broccoli, asparagus) can leach some out `[28:34]`–`[29:36]`. She estimates a
rough safe ceiling of "around 100 milligrams a day" for most people long-term, while flagging
the published lethal range as 3.5 to 30 grams — a Barcelona man reportedly died after eating sorrel
soup calculated at roughly 3.5 grams `[35:42]`, `[38:43]`.

## Accumulation and "oxalate dumping"

`[40:43]`–`[52:58]` — Norton attributes credit to researcher Susan Owens for identifying that when
someone reduces oxalate intake after years of high exposure, **the body begins actively expelling
stored crystals, producing waves of symptoms** — stinging eyes, jaw and tooth pain, sinus pain, and
in one client's case, visible crystal fragments emerging from the mouth. She warns against yo-yoing
back and forth between high- and low-oxalate eating, and against dropping intake abruptly, calling
that "way too much trauma in the body." She describes her own ongoing symptom, styes, managed with
coconut oil on the lash line, still occurring **"five and a half years past this diet."**

## Managing the transition

`[57:02]`–`[60:07]` — Recommended supports include potassium, magnesium, and calcium citrate (the
citrate form specifically, to weaken crystal bonds and allow crumbling), watching for electrolyte
crashes (heart palpitations, even documented emergency-room T-wave inversions), and B-vitamin
repletion via liver and egg yolks — though she cautions that too much nutritional "power" too fast
can accelerate dumping symptoms rather than easing them.

## Endogenous oxalate: collagen, gelatin, and carnivore eating

`[64:13]`–`[67:15]` — Asked directly about collagen/gelatin (hydroxyproline can degrade to
oxalate), Norton cites a researcher she names as "John Knight," who found animal protein overall
does not raise urinary oxalate, but that **around 5–7 grams of gelatin — about a cup of bone
broth's worth — did raise oxalate in urine** in his data, without a rise in blood oxalate levels.
Her practical guidance: **"a half to three-quarters of a cup of gelatin a day"** is probably fine
for most people, with more caution for anyone diabetic, since sugar intake independently raises
oxidative stress and glycated proteins that add to the burden.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Joint and back pain, poor surgical recovery, foot pain lasting 30 years** — Norton's own history,
  resolved within months of a corrected low-oxalate diet `[03:02]`–`[04:02]`.
- **A sleep disorder** — waking "29 times an hour," which she found resolved within 10 days of
  removing Swiss chard and sweet potatoes `[04:02]`.
- **Vulvodynia** — introduced via the Vulvar Pain Foundation, which Norton credits for early public
  oxalate education `[06:04]`–`[07:06]`.
- **Kidney stones**, styes, sinus pain, tooth/jaw pain, and skin crystal expulsion during "dumping"
  `[50:56]`–`[53:59]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Raphide** — a needle-shaped calcium oxalate crystal plants deploy in bundles as a chemical/
  physical defense against being eaten `[14:16]`–`[15:18]`.
- **Oxalate dumping** — the process, credited to researcher Susan Owens, of the body actively
  expelling accumulated oxalate deposits once dietary intake drops, producing transient symptom
  flares `[48:53]`–`[49:55]`.
- **Citrate (as a mineral form)** — potassium/magnesium/calcium bound to citrate, used specifically
  because citrate "lay[s] down on the crystals" and weakens the calcium bonds holding them together
  `[58:04]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Getting in trouble for marrying an alcoholic"** — Norton's image for why calcium gets blamed
  for oxalate kidney stones `[12:13]`.
- **Raphide crystals as "a quiverful of arrows"** shot deliberately into the eater's cells
  `[22:27]`.
- **Oxalate symptoms after removal likened to a mosquito bite's aftermath** — the body's immune
  response to a retained irritant continuing "for a few days" after the initial exposure `[17:22]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Norton repeatedly flags the limits of what's known: **"isn't it... wouldn't it be great if we had a
solid number that really worked for everybody"** before giving her own working estimate of 100
mg/day `[35:42]`; she calls the science of oxalate dumping "a science that needs to get developed"
since "no one's pursuing these questions" in the medical literature, crediting patient-community
crowdsourcing instead `[55:01]`–`[56:02]`. The endogenous-oxalate-from-collagen discussion is
explicitly labeled uncertain by both speakers ("we don't have a lot of really good answers")
`[64:13]`. The 85%-of-thyroids-over-50 statistic is attributed to her own paper without further
sourcing on-screen `[62:09]`.
