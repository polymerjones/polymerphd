# Smoke point and peroxidation index are not the same…

## Central claim

**The host argues smoke point — the temperature at which an oil begins to smoke — is a misleading
way to choose a cooking fat, since it says nothing about how readily the oil oxidizes when heated;
the relevant measure is the peroxidation index, and by that measure canola oil (polyunsaturated
seed oil) oxidizes fastest, olive oil next, and tallow slowest, even though all three share roughly
the same 400-450°F smoke point.** `[00:00]` "Choose fats with a lower peroxidation index like
tallow or butter or ghee for cooking fats and you'll do way better."

## Smoke point versus peroxidation index

`[00:00]` — Canola oil, olive oil, and tallow are cited as sharing roughly the **same smoke point**
(400-450°F), which the host argues makes smoke point useless for distinguishing which oil is
actually safer to cook with. The real differentiator is the **peroxidation index** — how readily an
oil's fat oxidizes under heat. Because canola oil is polyunsaturated, it oxidizes fastest; olive oil
oxidizes faster than tallow; tallow (and by extension butter, ghee) oxidizes slowest, making it the
host's preferred cooking fat despite an unremarkable smoke point.
