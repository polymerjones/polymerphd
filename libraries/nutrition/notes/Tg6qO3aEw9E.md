# Why You Should Avoid Cooking With These Oils

## Central claim

**Smoke point is the wrong metric for choosing a cooking oil — what matters is the peroxidation
index, the oil's propensity to oxidize under heat — and by that measure seed oils are the worst
choice, olive and avocado oil are better but still poor, and saturated animal fats (tallow,
butter, ghee, coconut oil) are the safest.** `[03:01]` "What you care about when you're cooking
with an oil is the peroxidation index... this is pretty much false" regarding smoke point
`[03:01]`.

## Pan choice, before oil

`[00:00]`–`[01:00]` — Stainless steel or cast iron is recommended over nonstick, "the predominant
[reason] being Teflon also known as PTFE, which can aerosolize when you heat the pan," irritating
lungs and potentially "endocrine disrupting" if flakes are ingested. A stainless pan is ready to
cook on, without sticking, once oil dropped on it forms moving beads of water — **called here "the
Mercury ball effect."**

## Seed oils: "toxic... from the moment they end up on a grocery shelf"

`[01:00]`–`[04:01]` — Seed oils are described as high in polyunsaturated fats with "multiple
double bonds," making them prone to oxidation even before cooking, since they arrive "refined
bleached and deodorized" and have already sat in plastic bottles on shelves. **"Humans have never
consumed seed oils without consuming seeds or nuts in our history until the last 110 years."**
Their pre-food history is given as machine and ship-engine lubricant, with canola oil specifically
credited with staying "slippery even when it's wet" in World War II naval use. Cited evidence:
"randomized controlled trials showing seed oils are harmful," and consumption increasing oxidized
LDL and **"a marker of cardiac risk known as LP little a"** `[03:01]`.

## Olive and avocado oil: better than seed oil, still not for the pan

`[04:01]`–`[07:03]` — Both oils are called "much better than seed oils" but not recommended for
cooking. Olive oil runs "10 to 15, sometimes even 20% linoleic acid," an 18-carbon omega-6 fat
that will oxidize on heating, giving it "a lower peroxidation index than seed oils but... much
higher than more saturated fats." A caveat on quality: **most olive oil, when tested, "is cut with
seed oils"** — recommended safeguards are organic, cold-pressed, single-source oil in opaque
glass. Avocado oil gets the same cutting caveat and is high in alpha-linolenic acid (an
omega-3 that also shouldn't be heated). Both oils' can linings are flagged as a separate
concern: **plastic linings containing "endocrine disrupting chemicals" that are lipophilic and
"associate with lipids."**

## The case for saturated animal fats

`[07:03]`–`[10:06]` — Saturated fat is defended against its "bad wrap," with particular praise for
18-carbon stearic acid and the 15- and 17-carbon odd-chain fatty acids (pentadecanoic and
heptadecanoic acid) found in dairy fat, **"consistently and strongly associated with improved
health outcomes in humans."** Animal fats (tallow, butter) are preferred over plant fats
(coconut oil) because grass-fed animals concentrate fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K,
"including vitamin K2, which has been consistently associated with better cardiovascular outcomes."
Tallow is described as rendered fat from perinephric or trim fat; butter is made by churning
cream, with raw (unheated) butter from organic grass-fed milk called the best form. Coconut oil is
called a good third-choice cooking fat but lacking in stearic acid, which is otherwise found
concentrated in tallow, butter, and cacao butter.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Lung irritation, worse for asthmatics** — attributed to aerosolized PTFE from heated nonstick
  pans `[00:00]`.
- No other bodily symptom is named; the oxidized-LDL and Lp(a) claims are framed as cardiac risk
  markers rather than felt symptoms `[03:01]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Peroxidation index** — the propensity of a heated oil to oxidize and turn rancid, presented as
  the correct heat-safety measure in place of smoke point `[03:01]`.
- **Mercury ball effect** — the visual cue (beading, dancing droplets of water) that a stainless
  pan is hot enough to cook on without sticking `[01:00]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Stainless pans and the "Mercury ball effect"** — a concrete, kitchen-testable stand-in for "hot
  enough," used instead of a thermometer or smoke-point chart `[01:00]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Most claims are delivered as settled fact without an on-screen study name — "randomized controlled
trials" and studies on canola-oil trans-fat content are referenced by category, not citation. One
explicit hedge: **"correlation is not causation," attached to the claim linking the last 110 years
of seed-oil exposure to the decline in health** `[02:00]`. The olive-and-avocado-oil adulteration
claim is stated as fact ("majority of olive oils when studies are done are cut with seed oils")
without naming the study.
