# The dangers of additives

## Central claim

**Additives most people assume are inert — anti-caking agents, artificial colors, citric acid,
vitamin C — show up in cheese, supplements, and pharmaceuticals well beyond where anyone would
expect them, and several are argued here to have real physiological effects rather than being
benign fillers** `[00:00]`. The closing framing: read your own labels, because "you will be
astounded what's in there" `[07:29]`.

## Cheese: rennet, soy residue, and silicon dioxide

`[00:00]`–`[01:02]` — Using Parmigiano-Reggiano (milk, salt, rennet) as the example, the speakers
advise checking for animal versus vegetable rennet, "especially if you have autoimmune conditions
or you're quite sensitive," since vegetable rennet "could cause autoimmune issues for people" and
may carry soy residue that triggers allergic reactions in soy-allergic people `[00:00]`. **Grated
cheese specifically is flagged for undisclosed silicon dioxide powder, an anti-caking agent
described as "like powder glass into your intestines"** — said to cause the same "micro colitis"
referenced earlier in the conversation and, over time regularly, to potentially contribute to
inflammatory bowel disease `[00:00]`. The advice is to buy the block, not the grated version, and
to watch for bloating or stomach noises as a sign an ingredient "is not sitting well with your GI
tract," possibly indicating "a serotonin release" `[01:02]`.

## What's really in a supplement

`[01:02]`–`[02:04]` — One speaker describes advising clients to stop all supplements except "a pure
desiccated organ supplement," on the reasoning that ordinary supplements carry excipients like
silicon dioxide, titanium dioxide, talc, and citric acid or vitamin C additives — the latter
"which can be from corn or other problematic things." **The larger point: "supplements are not
benign,"** and for most people eating meat, organs, and fruit already supplies "all the vitamins
you need" without the undisclosed extras `[02:04]`. The commercial driver named is shelf life —
manufacturers "will do anything possible" to make a product last indefinitely on a shelf `[02:04]`.

## Artificial colors and the ADHD question

`[03:05]` — Silicon dioxide, titanium dioxide, and talc are called "present in virtually all
pharmaceutical drugs" taken in tablet or capsule form. On artificial colorings, one dye
(transcribed "Allure") is said to be linked to an unspecified condition, and "several studies
notice that artificial colors are associated with hyperactivity disorder in children, ADHD." An
unnamed doctor's theory is relayed — that some of the ADHD "epidemic" traces to children "ingesting
this candy" full of psychoactive-acting chemicals — alongside an anecdote that genetically related
children who don't eat those candies show "drastically different" outcomes, with the hedge "I'm not
claiming they work" attached to the theory itself `[03:05]`.

## Citric acid and vitamin C as contaminated additives

`[04:05]`–`[05:07]` — Citric acid and vitamin C, added as antioxidants and preservatives especially
in juices (apple, grape, pear), are flagged as often "industrially produced" and "imported from
countries... with less than stellar safety regulation," making contamination likely even when the
compound itself is pure. **The most specific claim: cancer cells "overproduce the enzyme fatty acid
synthase and citrate synthase" and use citric acid, a Krebs-cycle metabolite, "to actually
synthesize fats and promote their own growth"** — supported by an animal study in which injecting
small tumors with citric acid "drastically increased the speed at which the tumors grow and
metastasize" `[05:07]`.

## Reading a label: fillers and a regulatory gap

`[06:29]`–`[07:29]` — The practical check offered: on a supplement label, the "amount per serving"
total should equal the sum of the individual listed amounts; a mismatch means "there's a filler,"
which is "more often than not... not a good thing," though manufacturers aren't always required to
disclose it `[06:29]`. **Since the pandemic, an "emergency restriction amelioration" from the FDA
and USDA is said to let some producers sell products, including foods, without the label actually
matching the ingredients** — the given example: a label could say coconut oil while the product is
"in fact... peanut oil" `[06:29]`. This gap is said to apply "even more so" to supplements, which
ideally should list only ingredients plus "vegetable capsule or gelatin capsule" `[07:29]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Bloating and audible stomach noises are named as signs an ingredient (specifically vegetable
rennet) "is not sitting well" `[01:02]`. Flushing after eating is separately called a sign of a
food reaction, tied to "a serotonin release" `[01:02]`. Hyperactivity in children is raised as a
possible downstream effect of artificial food colorings `[03:05]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Excipient** — the term used throughout for the "inactive" ingredients (silicon dioxide,
  titanium dioxide, talc, citric acid) added to supplements and pharmaceuticals beyond the active
  ingredient `[01:02]`–`[03:05]`.
- **Citrate synthase** — an enzyme said to be overproduced by cancer cells alongside fatty acid
  synthase, used here to explain why citric acid intake is framed as feeding tumor growth
  `[05:07]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Like powder glass into your intestines"** — the description used for silicon dioxide
  anti-caking powder in grated cheese `[00:00]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Mixed, and the format itself (an unlabeled two-speaker conversation) makes attribution of specific
claims to one speaker or a citation difficult. The rennet/autoimmune, silicon-dioxide/colitis, and
ADHD/artificial-color claims are stated with qualifiers ("I think," "he's saying," "I'm not
claiming they work") rather than as settled fact. The citric-acid/cancer mechanism is stated more
confidently and tied to a specific animal experiment, though no study name, author, or year is
given for it in the transcript `[05:07]`. The regulatory claim about a pandemic-era labeling
exemption is stated flatly, again without a named source `[06:29]`.
