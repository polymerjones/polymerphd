# This Invisible Light Restores Your Mitochondria (Science-Backed)

## Central claim

**Modern indoor and screen lighting strips out the infrared component of sunlight while
concentrating blue light, and that imbalance — not just screen time or "too much light" in
general — is framed as a driver of poor sleep, eye strain, headaches, and even mitochondrial
harm.** The host's framing device, stated early and returned to throughout: **"just like food can
be processed food or whole food, light can be whole food light or processed light"** `[00:00]`.
The fix he lands on is concrete rather than abstract: swap blue-heavy LEDs for incandescent or
red-incandescent bulbs, spend more time outdoors, and use accessibility settings to strip blue
light from phones and laptops at night `[18:19]`–`[21:23]`.

## Sunlight as the full-spectrum baseline

`[01:00]`–`[03:01]` — Tested outdoors in shade at roughly **10,000 lux**, sunlight reads as
continuous (**"no flicker... it's a continuous wave"**) and **"over 50% infrared from an energy
perspective."** Tristan states infrared penetrates deep into cells and is **"very restorative for
our mitochondria, helps clean up reactive oxygen species."** The claimed mechanism: infrared
drives **melatonin synthesis inside the mitochondria itself**, which the pair frame as balancing
the oxidative cost of the shorter, higher-energy blue/violet/UV wavelengths sunlight also
carries — **"everything has an oxidative response in our bodies... but the infrared comes in...
and helps clean that up. So it's balanced"** `[03:01]`.

## Indoor light strips out infrared: the window test

`[05:03]`–`[06:03]` — Measuring the same outdoor light through a closed glass sliding door,
Tristan reports **"anything past 850 nanometers pretty much gone,"** attributing the loss to
modern **low-emissivity ("low-e") windows** installed for energy efficiency. His framing: **"when
we're indoors with the windows closed, we're essentially becoming nutrient deficient from good
light."** Their stated fix is simple exposure — get outside even in winter (infrared passes
through clothing) and open windows when possible `[06:03]`.

## Bulb-by-bulb comparison

`[07:04]`–`[17:19]` — The pair test six more light sources on the spectrometer, framed against the
sunlight baseline established above:

| Light source | Infrared | Blue peak (~460 nm) | Flicker |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sunlight (outdoor shade) | **>50% of energy**, into wavelengths past 850 nm `[01:00]` | present, part of full spectrum | none — "a continuous wave" `[01:00]` |
| Candle | "mostly red and infrared... tons of infrared light" | "a tiny bit" | negligible — "nothing that is problematic" `[08:06]`–`[09:07]` |
| Warm white LED | **absent** — "no infrared light... very little red" | yes, "a blue pump... around 460 nanometers" `[10:09]` | 120 Hz, tied to the electrical grid `[12:13]` |
| "Zero flicker" dimmable red LED | narrow band only, ~625 nm — no infrared | none (red-only) | high-frequency switching (worse when dimmed); "wouldn't say that's zero flicker" `[14:15]`–`[15:17]` |
| Red incandescent (filtered) | "tons of red and even more infrared" | none — filtered below ~600 nm | low, similar to plain incandescent `[16:17]` |
| iPhone (post–iPhone 11, OLED) | none | yes, ~460 nm, "three peaks" | ~400 Hz pulse-width modulation `[19:21]`–`[20:22]` |
| 2022 MacBook Pro (LCD) | none | yes, ~460 nm, "three peaks" | better than phone — "tiny oscillations at like 10, 15,000 hertz" `[21:23]`–`[23:25]` |

## The Huberman Lab clip: a mitochondrial concern

`[11:11]`–`[12:13]` — The host inserts a clip of an unnamed researcher on the Huberman Lab
Podcast, who says of short-wavelength LED light on the retina in mouse models: **"we can watch
the mitochondria gently go downhill. Their membrane potentials are coming down. The mitochondria
are not breathing very well,"** and frames the issue as **"on the same level as asbestos... a
public health issue, and it's big."** The host extrapolates from mice to humans without new
evidence of his own: **"if they're actually harming the mitochondria in animals, they're probably
harming the mitochondria in humans as well."**

## Flicker as a second, separate problem

`[12:13]`–`[15:17]` — Beyond spectral imbalance, the pair treat flicker as its own harm mechanism.
Grid-driven LED flicker at 120 Hz is described as invisible but perceptible to "our brain, our
eyes, our nervous system," linked to **"eye strain, headaches, migraines, anxiety"** and, the host
speculates, end-of-day fatigue that people reach for coffee to fix `[13:14]`. A bulb marketed as
"zero flicker" is tested and found to still flicker, just at a higher frequency (roughly 3,000–
5,000 Hz) used to enable dimming — the host calls this **"kind of a shortcut and kind of marketing
misleading"** `[14:15]`.

## Fixes: bulbs, and phone/computer settings

`[18:19]`–`[23:25]` — The stated nighttime solution is incandescent bulbs, amber or red-filtered,
with the host naming his own home setup (a red incandescent "lizard lamp") and noting he links
specific bulb brands in his video description with "no affiliation" `[18:19]`. For screens, he
walks through Apple's Accessibility → Display & Text Size → Color Filters → Color Tint setting,
moved fully right, to strip most blue light from an iPhone; the same accessibility path exists on
a MacBook. Flicker itself is explicitly **not fixable** by this setting on either device
`[20:22]`–`[22:24]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Trouble falling asleep, headaches, and anxiety** — attributed to blue-enriched, flickering
  LED exposure, with incandescent lighting proposed as the fix `[18:19]`.
- **Eye strain, headaches, and migraines** specifically tied to light flicker (both LED bulbs and
  OLED phone screens), independent of spectral color `[13:14]`, `[19:21]`.
- **End-of-day fatigue and reliance on multiple cups of coffee** — speculatively attributed by the
  host to cumulative flicker and spectral-imbalance exposure through the workday `[13:14]`.
- **Macular oxidative stress/damage from blue light unbalanced by infrared** — raised as a
  question by the host and affirmed by Tristan for both LED bulbs and computer screens, though no
  specific study is named `[11:11]`, `[22:24]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Melanopic lux** — a measure of how strongly a light source stimulates the eye's circadian
  (non-visual) response, used to explain why a red-shifted bulb disrupts sleep less than a
  blue-heavy one `[16:17]`.
- **Pulse-width modulation** — the method OLED phone screens use to control brightness by rapidly
  switching the display on and off, which the video treats as the mechanism behind screen flicker
  `[20:22]`.
- **Low-emissivity ("low-e") window** — an energy-efficient glass coating identified as the reason
  indoor sunlight loses most of its infrared content `[05:03]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Light is like food... whole food light" versus "processed light"** — the video's organizing
  frame, applied to sunlight/candle/incandescent (whole) versus LED/screen light (processed)
  `[00:00]`, `[24:26]`.
- **"This is like a lizard cage"** — the host's own description of how his red-incandescent-lit
  house looks to visitors, used to acknowledge the aesthetic cost of the fix he's recommending
  `[17:19]`.

## Source-stated confidence

The spectrometer readings (infrared presence, blue-peak wavelength, flicker frequency) are
presented as direct, on-camera measurement rather than cited literature, and are treated with high
confidence throughout. The physiological claims connecting infrared to mitochondrial melatonin
synthesis and oxidative "balance" are stated as settled mechanism without a named study. The
strongest evidentiary claim — LED light harming mitochondria — rests entirely on one secondhand
podcast clip describing **animal (mouse retina) data**, which the host explicitly extrapolates to
humans himself ("probably harming the mitochondria in humans as well") rather than citing human
evidence; this extrapolation is the host's own inference, not something the quoted researcher
states in the clip as shown.
