# Exposing the "Healthy" Ingredient That's Killing You (Tucker Goodrich and Jeff Nobbs)

## Central claim

**Linoleic acid itself is framed as "probably totally harmless" — the harm is argued to come from
what it becomes in the body (HNE, arachidonic-acid-derived endocannabinoids, oxidized LDL), and
the case against seed oils is built from evolutionary precedent, animal mechanism studies, and a
reinterpretation of human cardiovascular and obesity data rather than from a single decisive human
trial.** `[00:00]` "Linoleic acid itself is probably totally harmless. It's the stuff it turns into
in your body" `[00:00]`.

## The evolutionary and anthropological case

`[10:08]`–`[27:30]` — The argument opens from precedent: **"there's no history of humans eating
[seed oils]," and "any population... that starts eating seed oils inevitably becomes sick and
obese."** The Chimane (Bolivian Amazon) are presented as having "essentially no heart disease,"
attributed to their traditional diet; researchers reportedly found rising obesity correlated first
with motorboat ownership, traced to motorboats enabling trips to buy "Market Foods" — refined
carbohydrates and industrial vegetable oil. This is extended to the "Israeli paradox" (Israel's
high seed-oil intake paired with high heart disease and diabetes), reframed as not paradoxical once
"our understanding of the effect of omega-6 polyunsaturated fats... is wrong." The lone named
exception among hunter-gatherer groups is the **Bushmen/!Kung**, who seasonally eat mongongo nuts
(high in linoleic acid) and, unlike other studied ancestral groups (including the Inuit), fail an
oral glucose tolerance test during that season — a finding both speakers call "pretty interesting"
rather than conclusive, and one host explicitly wonders whether it reflects ancestral diet or
modern "infringement on their hunting lands."

## How much food it actually takes to get this oil

`[28:31]`–`[31:35]` — To make the extraction scale concrete: **"60 to 70, or even 90 ears of corn
for five tablespoons of oil,"** two and a half pounds of sunflower seeds in the hull for the same
volume, and roughly five cups of cooked soybeans for five tablespoons of soybean oil — versus the
five to seven tablespoons of seed oil "the average American eats in a day." The point drawn: "just
because it's from a food that is a real whole food does not mean... it's good for you," since no
plausible amount of whole-food eating reproduces modern seed-oil intake.

## The refining process and mislabeled trans fats

`[38:43]`–`[46:52]` — A "How It's Made" canola-oil clip is described on-screen: screw-press
extraction, hexane solvent extraction of the remaining oil, a sodium hydroxide wash, centrifuging,
and wax-removal cooling — summarized as **"refined, bleached, and deodorized."** Citing unnamed
studies, the hosts state canola oil can run "3.6 to 4.2 percent" trans fat from this high-heat
processing, while FDA/USDA labeling rules allow a "0 grams trans fat" claim under 0.5 grams per
serving — called a legal but misleading gap. A distinction is drawn between these synthetic trans
fats and naturally occurring dairy trans fats like conjugated linoleic acid (CLA), which the
speakers describe as investigated for anti-cancer benefits and present in much smaller absolute
amounts than seed-oil-derived trans fats.

## HNE: the proposed obesogenic toxin

`[51:01]`–`[54:07]` — 4-hydroxynonenal (HNE) is introduced as a breakdown product formed only from
linoleic acid (or, less commonly, arachidonic acid) when it oxidizes. **In early cell-culture
research (reportedly using yeast), the cells "either died or got fat"** `[52:03]`. HNE is said to
alter cellular energy processing and predispose to fat storage; one host connects this to an
epidemiological finding that french fries are "the most fattening food that Americans eat... by
six or seven fold," attributing this to HNE formed when linoleic acid is fried.

## The body's slow clearance, and ketosis as an escape valve

`[55:09]`–`[59:12]` — Humans, unlike some other mammals, lack a route to excrete excess
polyunsaturated fat directly; the body instead converts some into "neutral inert things like
cholesterol or saturated fat." One cited kinetic study puts the half-life of stored omega-6 fat at
**"something like 680 days,"** described as a "bottleneck." Beta-oxidation during ketosis is named
as the main disposal pathway for HNE specifically — offered as a possible partial explanation for
ketogenic-diet benefits — but with a caveat: in human experiments overfeeding polyunsaturated fat
to induce ketosis, participants got nauseous, read as a sign of acute toxicity, and animal studies
of high-PUFA diets showed lean but liver-failing rodents.

## The Swedish muffin study, addressed directly

`[60:13]`–`[67:22]` — This overfeeding study, often cited to defend polyunsaturated fat, is
challenged on two grounds: the diet's only defined component was excess muffins with unknown total
composition, and the comparison fat was **palm oil, not butter** — palm oil's palmitic acid sits
in a different position on the glycerol backbone (outer positions) than in human breast milk
(middle position), a difference the hosts say makes palm oil poorly digested by infants and "not
allowed... in formula" for that reason. Since participants were force-fed to the point of gaining
weight (described as "kind of a strange thing"), the study is called **not "a health study"** by
one host, and its result read as comparing one poorly tolerated fat (palm oil, wrong position) to
another (seed oil) rather than testing a normal human diet.

## The endocannabinoid mechanism for overeating

`[67:22]`–`[78:33]` — Arachidonic acid, made from linoleic acid, is converted in the gut into
endocannabinoids (named: AEA and 2-AG) that activate CB1 receptors — the same receptor pathway THC
activates to cause "the munchies." Increased dietary linoleic acid is said to raise AEA and 2-AG,
increasing CB1 stimulation and hunger, shown "very clearly in rodent studies," including one where
mice fed salmon raised on high-linoleic-acid soy feed gained more weight than mice fed
lower-linoleic-acid salmon. **The CB1-blocking drug rimonabant "nearly eliminate[d] obesity in
animals" and improved human cardiovascular and diabetic markers, but was pulled from market**
because it "made people... want to kill themselves." Roux-en-Y gastric bypass is described as
working via the same pathway — bypassing the gut segment where these endocannabinoids form — with
an ~80% success rate and its own elevated suicide-rate caveat; administering AEA directly is said
to partially reverse the surgery's fat-loss effect in animal studies.

## Chicken, pork, and fish fat as a secondary linoleic-acid source

`[79:33]`–`[82:37]` — Wild chickens and pigs are cited at roughly **4–5% linoleic acid in their
fat versus 16–25% in conventionally corn/soy-fed animals.** One host notes this shapes his own
concern about eating "tons of eggs" or chicken fat today. A partial mitigating factor is raised,
then set aside: some of a conventional chicken's linoleic acid may sit on phospholipids rather
than triglycerides, a form the body may process differently (paralleling fish oil's triglyceride
omega-3 versus whole fish's mixed triglyceride/phospholipid omega-3) — but this is explicitly not
treated as making conventional chicken or pork fat safe, since the salmon-feeding study still
showed weight gain from a high-linoleic-acid diet regardless of form.

## Oxidized LDL and atherosclerosis

`[84:41]`–`[94:50]` — Citing a named consensus paper — **"Low density lipoproteins cause
atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease" from the European Atherosclerosis Society** — the hosts
argue its own first mechanistic step is "the oxidation of linoleic acid in LDL," converting it from
non-atherogenic to atherogenic. They cite Brown and Goldstein's Nobel-winning LDL receptor work as
having first failed to produce foam cells from native LDL in macrophage cultures, and
Steinberg/Witztum's subsequent finding that **oxidized LDL specifically is what macrophages take
up to form foam cells** — the earliest step of arterial plaque. The broader argument: mainstream
lipidology has quietly conceded this oxidation step while still treating LDL level alone as
sufficient grounds for treatment, which the hosts call an unresolved contradiction. One host frames
nutrition science's history as a series of narrowing categories (fat → saturated/unsaturated →
omega-3/omega-6 → oxidized LDL specifically), predicting the field converges on this explanation
eventually.

## A personal case, and the diverticulosis/fiber question

`[97:54]`–`[99:54]` — One host describes 14 years of irritable bowel syndrome resolving within two
days of cutting seed oils, and connects his own acute diverticulitis (which led to a colon
resection) to seed oils and wheat specifically: **"I can reproduce the symptoms for me personally
with wheat and vegetable oils."** He cites an endoscopy study of "over 3,000 patients" finding
higher fiber intake associated with *more* diverticulosis, not less, undercutting the standard
low-fiber explanation, and raises — without concluding — that diverticulosis may have an
autoimmune component `[99:54]`.

## Age-related macular degeneration (closing claim)

Discussed after the transcript's final timestamp, so anchored at `[99:54]`: linoleic acid's link to
age-related macular degeneration is called **"very strong,"** with one host stating that "linoleic
acid is the leading cause of blindness in the United States" would not be an unreasonable thing to
say, since AMD itself holds that title. No specific study is named in speech for this claim.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Overeating / "the munchies"** driven by endocannabinoid (AEA, 2-AG) stimulation of CB1
  receptors from linoleic-acid-derived arachidonic acid `[67:22]`–`[70:27]`.
- **Nausea** reported in human overfeeding experiments with polyunsaturated fat, read as a sign of
  acute toxicity `[58:12]`.
- **Irritable bowel syndrome** resolving within two days of removing seed oils, and **acute
  diverticulitis** reproducible with wheat and vegetable oil, both from personal account
  `[97:54]`–`[98:54]`.
- **Vision loss (age-related macular degeneration)** linked to linoleic acid intake `[99:54]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **HNE (4-hydroxynonenal)** — an oxidation product formed specifically from linoleic acid,
  described as toxic and obesogenic in cell and animal studies `[51:01]`.
- **Endocannabinoids (AEA, 2-AG)** — the body's own CB1-activating molecules, made from
  linoleic-acid-derived arachidonic acid, tied here to appetite stimulation `[67:22]`–`[68:25]`.
- **Rimonabant** — the CB1-blocking drug that reduced obesity and improved cardiometabolic markers
  in animals and humans but was withdrawn for increasing suicidal ideation `[70:27]`.
- **Healthy-user bias** — the observational-study confound where health-conscious people adopt
  seed oils alongside other health behaviors simultaneously, making seed-oil-specific effects hard
  to isolate `[24:27]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"We're all going through another genetic adaptation to a high seed oil diet"** — comparing
  humans' recent, incomplete adjustment to seed oils to earlier human migrations into new climates
  `[36:41]`.
- **Smoking and cigarettes, applied twice** — once to explain why a population-level pattern can
  hold even though some individuals seem to tolerate the exposure (some smokers avoid lung
  cancer), and once for how a single exposure ("a pack of cigarettes") won't cause disease, only
  chronic repetition does `[11:09]`, `[30:35]`.
- **A computer that "started misbehaving"** — used to frame the diagnostic instinct of asking what
  changed in the inputs (i.e., diet) when a well-functioning system (the body) breaks down
  `[94:50]`.

## Source-stated confidence

The hosts are explicit that human randomized-controlled-trial evidence is incomplete: **"we don't
have the studies that we would prefer to have in humans that really put the nail in the coffin
so we have to come at it from different angles"** `[67:22]`. Several claims are stated with more
confidence than their cited backing: the "680-day half-life" figure comes from "one kinetic study,"
the AMD claim from unnamed "lots of evidence," and specific canola trans-fat percentages are
attributed to studies neither host names precisely ("correct me if I'm wrong on those numbers").
Genetic variation in linoleic-acid metabolism (ALDH2-type polymorphisms) is raised explicitly to
undercut confidence in blood-linoleic-acid biomarker studies, since low blood levels could mean
either low intake or fast conversion to more harmful downstream products.
