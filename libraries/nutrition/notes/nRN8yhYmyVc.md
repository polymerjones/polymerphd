# Glyphosate: how it's harming you

## Central claim

**Glyphosate research "in isolation" undersells the risk, because tested herbicide formulations
(Roundup) are repeatedly found more toxic than the glyphosate active ingredient alone, human
glyphosate exposure is widespread and detectable in food and bodies, and the weight of reviewed
evidence points toward real neurological, reproductive, and cancer-adjacent harms even though "the
jury is still out" on some specific claims** `[08:10]`, `[29:34]`. The source states his own
position plainly: "I would be a little bit skeptical, in fact a lot skeptical, about glyphosate"
`[05:06]`.

## Where glyphosate shows up: maps, cereals, beer and wine

`[08:10]`–`[21:27]` — Glyphosate was listed by California in 2017 as a substance "known... to cause
cancer," and by IARC in 2015 as what the source calls a "group two" (elsewhere "Class 2")
carcinogen — a category he notes also includes red meat and acrylamide, adding "I would debate"
some of those classifications `[08:10]`.
General Mills faced lawsuits over glyphosate in Cheerios (2018) and Nature Valley granola bars
(2016) `[09:11]`–`[10:12]`. A USGS glyphosate-use map (2017) is used to argue that where crops
(and therefore meat, fruit, and honey) are produced matters as much as where a person lives
`[10:12]`–`[13:16]`. Environmental Working Group testing of 28 cereal/oat samples found glyphosate
in all of them, with **26 of 28 above EWG's own benchmark of 160 parts per billion** — a benchmark
the source is careful to distinguish from "the EPA legal limit for glyphosate in oats," which is
"30 parts per million," several orders of magnitude higher `[13:16]`–`[14:19]`. Some organic oat
products (Kashi, Whole Foods 365, Bob's Red Mill) showed no detectable glyphosate in this testing,
though not all organic samples were clean `[15:19]`–`[16:20]`. A separate beer-and-wine test found
glyphosate in 19 of 20 samples, with the highest reading in Sutter Home wine at 51 parts per
billion; one product, Peak beer, had no detectable level `[20:26]`.

## The Bruce Ames counter-argument, and where the source parts ways with it

`[21:27]`–`[25:30]` — Bruce Ames's 1980s paper, "Dietary Pesticides (99.9% All Natural)," argued
most dietary pesticide exposure comes from plants' own defense chemicals, not sprays — "Americans
eat 1.5 grams of natural pesticides per person per day, about 10,000 times more than they eat of
synthetic pesticide residues," illustrated with "49 natural pesticides and metabolites found in
cabbage." **The source accepts the data but rejects Ames's implied conclusion: "I look at that a
little differently and think we should probably worry about the pesticides we're spraying on the
plants... because we don't really know what those do in humans"** `[22:27]`. He extends this into a
standing hypothesis that vegetables themselves may "trigger autoimmunity" in some people, based on
anecdotes of symptom improvement after removing vegetables `[25:30]`.

## The organic-food-cancer study, and a caution about healthy-user bias

`[25:30]`–`[27:32]` — A December 2018 NutriNet-Santé cohort study found "a higher frequency of
organic food consumption was associated with a reduced risk of cancer." **The source's explicit
caveat: this is epidemiology confounded by "healthy user bias" — people who eat organic food likely
do other healthy things too — so "we can't know if organic food is better for humans until we do an
interventional study"** `[26:30]`.

## Formulation versus active ingredient: why Roundup may be worse than glyphosate alone

`[27:32]`–`[34:37]` — A cited study testing nine pesticides on three human cell lines found
**"eight formulations out of nine were up to 1,000 times more toxic than their active
principles,"** with Roundup "among the most toxic herbicides and insecticides tested" despite its
"relatively benign reputation" `[28:33]`–`[29:34]`. The study's own conclusion is quoted: this
"challenge[s] the relevance of the acceptable daily intake of pesticides because this norm is
calculated from the toxicity of the active principle alone" `[29:34]`. Separate animal studies are
cited showing POEA (a Roundup adjuvant) and other Roundup-specific compounds caused lung
hemorrhage, lung epithelial damage, and altered swine hemodynamics with piglet deaths — effects not
seen with a glyphosate-sodium-hydroxide preparation lacking POEA `[47:55]`–`[48:57]`.

## Glyphosate in bodies: humans, and grass-fed versus grain-fed cattle

`[30:36]`–`[33:37]` — Citing a review of glyphosate residues in animals and humans, the source
states "the vast majority of us are full of glyphosate," including himself, despite eating mostly
organic in Costa Rica `[30:36]`. **Cows "kept in genetically modified free areas had significantly
lower glyphosate concentrations in urine than conventional husbandry cows,"** offered as one
reason (among several, alongside PFAS and microplastics) to prefer grass-finished beef — with the
explicit caveat that this must be "validated and corroborated individually" per farm, since a cow
can still be fed glyphosate-sprayed hay `[31:36]`–`[33:37]`.

## Reviewed harms: nervous system, immune system, reproduction, and one estrogen study

`[34:37]`–`[54:00]` — A 2022 review states "the weight of the evidence indicates... glyphosate and
Roundup have significant adverse effects on the brain and behavior," including an association with
Parkinson's disease, "some evidence... implicating Roundup in an elevated risk of autism" (flagged
by the source himself as "a very controversial topic"), and disruption of the shikimate pathway
shared by gut bacteria `[34:37]`–`[36:39]`. A 2001 case report describes a 54-year-old man who
developed "a symmetrical parkinsonian syndrome" two years after an intense glyphosate exposure —
the source calls a symmetrical presentation "clinically abnormal" for an otherwise-unexplained
case, while still hedging "we cannot say for sure" `[36:39]`–`[38:42]`. A 2022 case-control study
found thyroid cancer risk associated with agricultural pesticide exposure, glyphosate included, at
"an odds ratio of 1.33," with risk increasing with the number of different pesticides a person was
exposed to `[38:42]`–`[39:44]`. A 2020 review is quoted at length on immune, reproductive, hepatic,
and cardiovascular effects across animal models, including reduced testosterone in male rat
offspring, altered progesterone in human placental cells "only at higher concentrations than ones
causing cell death," and fibrosis in rainbow trout and rats `[42:49]`–`[46:53]`; the source notes an
older (2000) industry-adjacent paper concluding Roundup posed no health risk, and says "I would like
to look at these authors [for] conflicts of interest," given the subsequent 22 years of research
`[46:53]`. **A separate ultra-low-dose (0.1 ppb) two-year rat study found liver and kidney damage,
which the source flags as achievable in humans "without too many problems"** `[48:57]`. A 2013
cell-line study found glyphosate promoted estrogen-receptor-driven breast cancer cell growth, with
an additive effect alongside genistein (the soy/bean compound, separately tied to reduced male
fertility) `[49:58]`–`[52:59]`. An NHL meta-analysis found "b-cell lymphoma was positively
associated with phenoxy herbicides and the organophosphorous herbicide glyphosate," with the
explicit hedge "not all studies have showed this" `[52:59]`–`[54:00]`.

## Kale, thallium, and dacthal — a related digression

`[56:01]`–`[66:16]` — Kale is criticized on three separate axes: isothiocyanates that may affect
thyroid iodine uptake ("you probably would have to eat an excessive amount for this to happen
though" `[57:03]`); dacthal, a pesticide the EPA itself "acknowledge[s]... to have negative
endocrine disrupting effects on the thyroid" `[58:04]`; and thallium, a heavy metal the source
attributes largely to practitioner Ernie Hubbard's clinical work with Doctor's Data testing.
**The source's sharpest claim here: organic kale samples in that testing were higher in thallium
than non-organic ones, "so you're not going to escape it with organic vegetables"** `[65:14]`. He
notes a nutritionist's assessment of Hubbard's work as "good science... preliminary, not [yet]
ready to be extrapolated," rather than confirmed `[63:10]`.

## Actionable steps to lower exposure

`[67:17]`–`[71:24]` — Get a baseline glyphosate level checked (Great Plains Labs); source
grass-fed meat from farms that confirm no hay/herbicide use; buy organic produce; use reverse
osmosis water; avoid oat- and wheat-based grains and processed foods; avoid beans/soy/tofu to limit
genistein; retest periodically; consider the broader GPL-TOX panel for other pesticides and
environmental contaminants; avoid kale to cut thallium and dacthal exposure `[67:17]`–`[70:21]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

Parkinsonian movement symptoms and a symmetrical presentation as a clinical red flag
`[36:39]`–`[38:42]`; anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in animal models, described somewhat
facetiously by the source `[44:51]`–`[45:51]`; chronic fatigue and non-specific symptoms reported
by Ernie Hubbard's patients, later connected to thallium `[59:05]`; autoimmune symptom improvement
after removing vegetables, offered as anecdotal `[25:30]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **POEA** — the Roundup adjuvant named across several cited animal studies as more toxic, in
  combination with glyphosate, than glyphosate alone `[47:55]`–`[48:57]`.
- **Shikimate pathway** — described as "a folate pathway" in plants and some gut bacteria that
  glyphosate is known to inhibit, offered as a proposed mechanism for gut-microbiome disruption
  `[35:38]`.
- **Healthy user bias** — the confound the source raises against the organic-food-cancer cohort
  study, where people who choose organic food may simply do more healthy things generally
  `[26:30]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"2 plus 2 equals 5"** — the source's shorthand throughout for why testing pesticides in
  isolation may understate combined/additive real-world harm `[03:03]`.
- **"Tip of the iceberg"** — used to frame glyphosate as one of many pesticides (alongside dacthal
  and 2,4-D) that deserve the same scrutiny `[21:27]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Deliberately graded throughout. The source repeatedly separates active-ingredient-only studies
from full-formulation studies, and flags animal-study doses that are "massive" and unrealistic for
most humans versus doses he considers achievable `[41:47]`, `[48:57]`. He calls the cancer classification
question unresolved ("I think the jury is still out on glyphosate and cancer") while stating more
confidently that "it doesn't look like glyphosate is good for humans in any way shape or form"
`[08:10]`. The autism association is explicitly flagged as "very controversial" rather than settled
`[35:38]`. The organic-cancer cohort study is explicitly downgraded for healthy-user-bias confounding
`[26:30]`. Notably, the source promotes his own supplement company (Heart and Soil) and a related
nonprofit throughout this video, which is worth naming as a financial interest alongside his
dietary recommendations `[02:03]`, `[64:12]`.
