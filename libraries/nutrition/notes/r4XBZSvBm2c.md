# Clavicular and Paul Saladino FULL Live Stream

## Central claim

**This is an unstructured, in-person grocery-store tour rather than a scripted argument, but Saladino's own stated framework, given near the end, is the closest thing to a throughline: start with macros (roughly 0.8 g protein/lb bodyweight), then food quality, then smaller optimizations like avoiding carrageenan or red 40** `[216:14]`–`[217:15]`. Clav's starting position is nearly the opposite — that swapping a Burger King bun for a whole-food carbohydrate source produces **"no difference"** in how he feels, since for him **"my main... irritability thing would just be whether my dopamine receptors are properly saturated"** rather than food quality itself `[17:22]`. Saladino disagrees directly but doesn't force the point, instead walking the store topic by topic.

## Opening framing: macros vs. food quality

`[07:08]`–`[10:11]` — Saladino opens with red meat, rejecting the mainstream "heart disease, cancer, diabetes" framing as **"baloney"** and stating his message is simply **"do not fear meat... and especially do not fear red meat"** `[08:08]`. He immediately qualifies that ground beef is a fine budget option and that grass-fed is preferred mainly because **conventional cattle are grain-finished on feed that "can be contaminated with pesticides or other toxins like mold,"** not because conventional beef itself is bad `[09:11]`. His stated priority order: total macros first, organic/grass-fed status **"the last thing you worry about"** `[10:11]`.

## LDL cholesterol and fasting insulin

`[11:12]`–`[13:16]` — Saladino frames LDL risk as **context-dependent on insulin sensitivity**, not a fixed danger: **"there's a lot of studies that show LDL cholesterol doesn't have the same association with heart disease when you're insulin sensitive as it does when you're insulin resistant"** `[11:12]`. He describes LDL mechanically as a lipid-bilayer particle carrying cholesterol and triglycerides to tissue including testes and brain, calling it **"crucial for human health"** `[12:16]`. He states his own fasting insulin as **2.5 µIU/mL** ("less than three all the time") as his personal benchmark for metabolic health despite a high-meat, high-honey, high-carbohydrate diet `[13:16]`, and recommends viewers get their own fasting insulin checked before worrying about LDL in isolation.

## Seed oils and frying (first mention)

`[13:16]`–`[14:16]` — Prompted by Clav dismissing seed oils as "the one thing that's the least concern," Saladino disagrees, stating that **heated seed oils generate aldehydes like acrolein that are "linked with cancers" and "triggering inflammation."** He calls heated/deep-fried seed oils specifically **"a problem for humans,"** distinct from the broader (more nuanced) seed-oil conversation he returns to later.

## Oxalates

`[15:21]` — Referencing his own older "plant defense chemical" content, Saladino calls oxalates **"the major precursor for kidney stones... and joint pain, other issues"** for a subset of people, while agreeing with Clav that for most people macros matter more than oxalate content.

## Eggs: a four-tier grading system

`[25:33]`–`[27:33]` — Saladino ranks egg labels from best to worst: **pasture-raised** (legally guaranteed **108 sq ft per bird outdoors**, verified they actually go outside) > **organic pasture-raised** > **free-range** (he calls this **"a tier below pasture"** and jokes it's like being "in prison" — birds get outdoor access but a much smaller, paper-defined footprint) > plain **"outdoor access"** labeling, which he says is probably equivalent to free-range > anything not labeled free-range at all, which he says isn't worth buying `[25:33]`. He offers a visual quality test: crack two eggs side by side and compare **yolk height and white thickness** — a lower-quality egg's white runs noticeably thinner `[26:33]`. He names Vital Farms specifically as a brand he rates well from a prior blind taste-test video, stating **"I have no affiliation with them"** `[26:33]`.

## Potatoes: skin, eyes, and a satiety study

`[29:33]`–`[30:35]` — Saladino says he avoided potatoes for years on strict carnivore but has reintroduced them, with two caveats: the **skin carries some oxalates**, and the **eyes/sprouts on a potato contain alkaloids** he says "can be problematic for humans and cause headaches" — his advice is to skin the potato and remove any eyes before cooking `[29:33]`. He cites (without naming the study directly) a satiety-index comparison of ~35 foods at 100 g portions in which **boiled potatoes ranked as the single most satiating food tested**, with steak also ranking high and cookies/donuts ranking very low `[30:35]`.

## Sugar vs. fruit: a dysbiosis hypothesis

`[30:35]`–`[31:35]` — Saladino's stated hypothesis (not presented as settled): pure sucrose feeds gut bacterial overgrowth with **"nothing in there to prevent"** it, whereas fruit's hundreds of phytochemicals/polyphenols **"actually stop the overgrowth of the bad bacteria in your gut,"** so fruit sugar and table sugar are not physiologically equivalent despite similar glucose/fructose ratios `[31:35]`. He states fruit juices (apple, cherry, orange, grape) are linked in "so many good studies" to improved endothelial function and reduced LDL oxidation and DNA damage, though no specific study is named `[39:42]`.

## Almond milk and plant-milk additives

`[32:35]`–`[35:38]` — Comparing labels in-aisle, Saladino's rule: avoid **carrageenan** and **mono/diglycerides**, citing an unnamed **"2024 study"** (observational, explicitly flagged as such) showing an association with **"multiple cancers... breast and overall"** `[32:35]`. He describes carrageenan as an algae-derived long polysaccharide, **"probably damaging for the gut."** Reading a second almond-milk label together, they flag **"low erucic acid rapeseed oil"** (canola) — Saladino recounts that "canola" is a Canadian trademark acronym (**"Canadian Oil Low Acid"**) and that **erucic acid, the monounsaturated fat canola is bred to minimize, is linked to cardiomyopathy in animal studies** — studies he notes were never done in humans **"because it's not ethical"** `[35:38]`.

## Sunscreen: chemical vs. mineral

`[58:03]` — Saladino says he avoids chemical sunscreens (naming **avobenzone, oxybenzone, homosalate, octocrylene**) because **"one single application... you see those chemicals in the blood, you excrete them in the poop and the pee, and they're known endocrine disruptors,"** hypothesizing an effect at the level of testicular/ovarian LH and FSH signaling. He uses a **tallow-based, non-nano zinc oxide** sunscreen on his face instead.

## Honey

`[58:03]`–`[61:08]` — Saladino says Whole Foods generally isn't his favorite honey source but picks the best available: **glass packaging, organic, raw (unheated)**, and for Manuka honey specifically, a higher **MGO (methylglyoxal) number** indicating stronger antibacterial activity — he points out a 150+, 200+, and 300+ MGO option in-store and explains the number scales with strength, while noting Manuka isn't necessary for most people given its cost `[59:05]`. His central honey claim: table sugar is roughly 50/50 fructose/glucose, but **honey additionally carries "probably 350, 400 other chemicals that affect your gut flora and your body positively,"** and studies show honey **"improves insulin sensitivity"** while sucrose **"worsens those things"** `[60:06]`. Clav pushes back that artificial sweeteners are more efficient for pure leanness goals; Saladino doesn't dispute the calorie math, just states his own preference for honey/maple syrup.

## Peanut butter rancidity

`[64:10]`–`[65:12]` — Saladino says natural peanut butter's separated oil layer becomes **rancid within two to three months**, citing (from his own unspecified research) a peroxide value of **roughly 75+ milliequivalents per liter** in that oil layer, and recommends grinding peanut butter fresh rather than buying jarred natural peanut butter that's been sitting.

## Rice and arsenic

`[50:48]`–`[52:50]` — Saladino calls white rice a fine carbohydrate but flags **brown rice as carrying substantially more arsenic** than white. He contextualizes the risk by comparing it to a recent controversy over arsenic in Mountain Valley bottled water, stating that controversy's arsenic level was **"probably 100 to 200 times lower than rice,"** while rice itself can run **"75 to 150 parts per billion"** — his conclusion is occasional rice is fine but **"probably worth checking your arsenic levels"** for regular eaters `[51:48]`.

## Oats and phytic acid

`[52:50]`–`[53:53]` — Saladino attributes his own history of **stress fractures** as a distance runner to eating **50-lb bags of oats**, hypothesizing the mechanism as **phytic acid** ("robs you of other nutrients") from unfermented oats. He contrasts this with traditional Norwegian preparation, which ferments oats heavily, and draws a parallel to **sourdough bread**, where bacterial fermentation breaks down gluten and phytic acid in a way regular bread and raw oats don't.

## Olive oil and avocado oil selection

`[54:57]`–`[57:01]` — Both put avocado oil's linoleic-acid content at roughly **14-21%**, similar to olive oil, with Saladino saying he prefers olive oil mainly because it's **"more artisanal"** and better-studied over time. His shopping criteria for a "good" olive oil: **glass bottle (opaque), extra virgin, organic, single-source**, comparing it to honey in that **"a lot of low quality avocado or olive oil is just... cut with canola or sunflower"** `[54:57]`. He flags a specific bottle's label claiming **584 mg/L of polyphenols** as a positive marker, reasoning those polyphenols are likely gut-beneficial `[55:58]`.

## Butter, calories, and the hypothalamus

`[48:47]`–`[50:48]` — Saladino says he doesn't consider saturated fat like butter dangerous — **"if you look at the cardiovascular risk profile, it doesn't raise it at all... for a lot of people it improves it"** — but flags it as calorically dense and easy to overeat if satiety signaling is impaired `[48:47]`. His mechanism: in **animal studies** (not human, "because dissecting a human brain is not ethical"), a processed-food diet produces **gliosis and inflammation in the arcuate nucleus** of the hypothalamus, a satiety-regulation center — his inference is that single-ingredient diets let the body's own satiety signaling work properly, so **whole-food overeating self-limits** in a way processed food doesn't `[49:47]`.

## Weston A. Price

`[112:57]`–`[114:57]` — Saladino describes Weston A. Price's 1930s-40s cross-cultural dental surveys (*Nutrition and Physical Degeneration*) as showing indigenous populations eating traditional animal-food-and-fermented-grain diets had **"amazing"** dentition and **wide jaws/palates**, which he frames as a **hypothesis** ("presumably... hypothesis being") connected to fat-soluble nutrient intake during prenatal and childhood development, contrasted with modern narrow palates, sleep apnea, and recessed maxillas `[113:57]`.

## Raw milk

`[114:57]`–`[117:00]` — Saladino acknowledges raw milk carries **"definitely a higher risk... definitely a non-zero risk of contamination,"** comparable to any raw food (he cites a recent lettuce/cyclospora outbreak and cantaloupe as common contamination sources) `[114:57]`. His stated reason for drinking it anyway: **observational studies comparing farm vs. city kids on raw vs. pasteurized milk found lower rates of asthma, eczema, and allergies** in the raw-milk group, with his hypothesis pointing at a heat-sensitive whey protein or bacterial component — he's explicit that **"know your farmer"** is the actual safeguard, not any property of rawness itself `[116:00]`.

## Seed-oil randomized trials, named and critiqued

`[121:04]`–`[123:09]` — This is the most detailed, citation-heavy segment. Saladino states he only weighs **randomized controlled trials**, not observational data, and names two groups: trials he says are **methodologically compromised** — the **Finnish Mental Hospital study** (confounded by more antipsychotic prescriptions, e.g. thioridazine, in the control group) and the **LA Veterans Trial** (more heavy smokers and far less vitamin E in the control group) — versus three trials he says are better-designed and consistently unfavorable to seed oils: **Minnesota Coronary, Sydney Diet Heart, and the Rose Corn Oil Trial**, all of which he says showed **seed oils performing worse than animal fats for cardiovascular health and mortality** `[122:04]`. He's explicit that none of the three "good" trials are perfect either, and that the debate often comes down to **"my study's better than your study."**

## The linoleic-acid accumulation hypothesis

`[124:11]`–`[127:14]` — Saladino's core seed-oil mechanism claim, explicitly labeled his own hypothesis at points: **linoleic acid (an 18-carbon omega-6 PUFA) cannot be synthesized by the human body and is stored, with a body "memory" of roughly 2-4 years** depending which pharmacokinetic studies you credit `[125:12]`. He estimates ancestral diets provided **under ~2%, "probably closer to 1.5%,"** of calories as linoleic acid (mainly nuts in fall), versus **10-15% typical in modern diets** — a **5-10x increase**. His hypothesis, stated as speculative ("again, this is just my hypothesis"): linoleic acid may function as an evolutionary **"winter is coming"** fat-storage signal, and chronic oversupply may stress mitochondrial membranes and contribute to insulin resistance `[126:14]`. He recommends a practical test over theory: a month with little/no seed oils vs. a month eating normally, and noting how you feel.

## Glycine and collagen supplementation

`[137:25]`–`[138:26]` — Saladino states most humans have a **"glycine gap"** of roughly **7-10 g/day** short of stoichiometric need, since glycine is a building block for both **glutathione and collagen**. On collagen supplements specifically, he cites a study (unnamed) showing an ingested **collagen di-peptide** triggered increased collagen production in the body, arguing even fully-digested collagen supplementation is useful **"just [for] giving you more glycine"** to feed the body's own synthesis.

## Retatrutide ("Reda"/"RETA") dosing and a reward-pathway concern

`[62:10]`–`[63:10]`, `[129:14]`–`[130:14]` — Saladino frames retatrutide as **"an amazing antidote to processed food"** for people who eat processed food, but not something a whole-food eater needs `[62:10]`. Clav discloses his own dosing: **10 mg every 5 days** ("a giga dose"), sometimes skipped for over a week with only mild hunger increase and "not much different" subjective effect `[128:14]`. Saladino raises an explicitly unproven concern: GLP-1 receptors sit in **mesolimbic reward pathways**, and while their appetite-suppressing mechanism is established, he wonders **whether chronic use could blunt interest in other reward-seeking behaviors (socializing, romance)** — stating plainly **"my concern, has not been proven yet"** `[129:14]`–`[130:14]`. Clav reports no anhedonia on the drug since 2024, though he separately attributes his own reduced sociability to a different drug, an MAO-B inhibitor referred to in the transcript as "MSAM"/selegiline (the exact acronym is unclear in this whisper transcript and preserved as heard rather than guessed) `[131:14]`.

## Blue zones critique

`[145:47]`–`[147:48]` — Saladino challenges the popular "Blue Zones" longevity narrative (Dan Buettner/National Geographic), stating **four of the five named regions (Okinawa, Sardinia, Ikaria, the Nicoya region of Costa Rica) all include meat-eating populations**, with only Loma Linda, California (Seventh-day Adventist) as a vegetarian exception. His claim: **"there's no good evidence suggesting that longevity is inversely correlated with meat consumption"** and some evidence pointing the other way, though he flags that cross-country meat-consumption data may be confounded by GDP/sanitation `[147:48]`.

## Bryan Johnson critique

His specific claim: a sufficiently calorie-restricted diet without thyroid hormone replacement would show elevated TSH and low thyroid hormone, so **"when he came out and started talking... ## Sunlight: full-spectrum vs. isolated UV

`[182:46]`–`[188:59]` — Saladino's key methodological claim: most **skin-damage studies use isolated UV light**, not full-spectrum sunlight, even though **sunlight itself is "less than 9%" UV** (roughly 0.13% UVB, ~5-6% UVA), with the rest visible and infrared spectrum — and **red/infrared wavelengths are separately protective for collagen and elastin**, raising the open question of whether a "Goldilocks dose" of real sunlight could deliver benefits (nitric oxide, vitamin D, gut microbiome alpha-diversity via skin UV exposure) without the isolated-UV damage seen in lab studies `[182:46]`–`[183:51]`. He personally protects his face (mineral sunscreen, hat) while allowing body sun exposure, and recounts his own **tanning-bed use during a gray Seattle residency** as likely accelerating his own skin aging, since tanning beds are pure UV with none of sunlight's red/infrared component `[185:55]`. On the morning-light routine: he cites a **"lux gap"/"lux cliff"** — indoor light rarely exceeds a few hundred lux versus the **50,000-100,000 lux** needed outdoors to trigger circadian effects — and walks through a specific mechanism chain: morning outdoor light sets the **cortisol awakening response**, suppresses melatonin synthesis (via N-acetyltransferase), and displaces adenosine from brain receptors, crediting **Andrew Huberman** by name for popularizing this `[186:56]`–`[188:59]`.
