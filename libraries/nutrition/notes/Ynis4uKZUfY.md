# How Contaminated Is Your Favorite Salt?

## Central claim

**A brand-by-brand independent analysis found some popular sea salts — Celtic Sea Salt above all
— carry heavy metal loads that, at ordinary daily intake, exceed the California Prop 65 tolerable
limit for lead by multiples, while other brands came back essentially clean.** `[00:00]` The
source describes being "surprised, shocked, and chagrined at the amount of heavy metals"
found.

## Heavy metals in the broader diet, for context

`[00:00]`–`[02:01]` — Aluminum: linked to "increased rates of Alzheimer's, dementia... memory
loss," also found in foil and some deodorants/antiperspirants. Arsenic: found in dirt/earth,
linked to "increased rates of many cancers." Cadmium: concentrated in bottom-feeding shellfish
(shrimp, scallops, lobster), linked to cancers and kidney issues. **Lead**: from historical paint
and gasoline, linked to lower IQ, neurodevelopmental issues, delayed motor/verbal growth,
cardiovascular disease, and kidney/hormone effects.

## Testing your own body

`[03:03]` — Blood tests for lead, mercury, cadmium, and arsenic, done "probably twice a year" by
the source; aluminum is "not usually" tested in blood. Chronic exposure is harder to catch by
blood test alone — provoked urine testing (with a functional medicine practitioner) or hair
testing are named as alternatives, with hair tests flagged as "variable accuracy."

## Brand-by-brand figures

`[04:03]`–`[08:09]`, based on an estimated 8.8 g/day salt intake:

| Brand | Aluminum | Arsenic | Cadmium | Lead | Est. lead/day |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Celtic Sea Salt | 171 ppm | 82 ppb | 1.2 ppb | 553 ppb | ~4.87 mcg |
| Redmond | — | — | — | 252 ppb | ~2.22 mcg |
| Baja Gold | — | — | — | 337 ppb | ~3 mcg |
| Jacobson | 1.69 ppm | ~9.8 (unit unclear in source audio) | small | undetectable (<0.5 ppb) | negligible |
| Diamond Kosher salt flakes | none detected | none detected | none detected | none detected | none |

Celtic Sea Salt is named explicitly as worst, at **"almost 10 times"** the 0.5 mcg Prop 65 daily
limit. Redmond and Baja Gold are both called out despite reputations for purity — Baja Gold
markets its own testing, yet "it has a lot of lead in it." Diamond Kosher salt flakes is
flagged with an explicit note of discomfort about its manufacturer: the source says he doesn't
"feel great about" Cargill but still wanted to "tell you guys about" the clean result
`[08:09]`.

## Context on the Prop 65 number itself

`[06:07]`–`[07:07]` — Prop 65's 0.5 mcg/day lead limit is called "pretty low by a lot of
standards"; the FDA's tolerable upper limit is 12 micrograms of lead per day, which the
source calls "probably a little too high... a little too much tolerance." His own stated
position sits between the two: **"there's really no safe level of lead... but don't get too
stressed out about 0.5 micrograms."**

## What's still untested

`[09:10]`–`[10:11]` — This analysis **did not test for microplastics in salt at all**, flagged
explicitly as an open gap rather than a finding: "hopefully we can get more of that in the
future."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

`[00:00]`–`[02:01]` aluminum: Alzheimer's, dementia, memory loss, cognitive issues. Arsenic:
cancers. Cadmium: cancers, kidney issues. Lead: lower IQ, neurodevelopmental issues, delayed motor
and verbal growth ("lead makes us dumb"), cardiovascular disease, kidney harm, hormone
disruption.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Tolerable upper limit** — applied specifically to lead, with two competing reference numbers
  given (Prop 65's 0.5 mcg/day versus the FDA's 12 mcg/day), and the source explicitly staking a
  position between them rather than treating either as definitive `[06:07]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None distinct.

## Source-stated confidence

More concrete reference points than most videos in this batch — Prop 65 and FDA limits are both
named with numbers — but the brand-level ppm/ppb figures themselves are attributed only to "this
independent analysis," never a named lab or publication. The cleanest result (Diamond Kosher) is
explicitly flagged by the source as needing confirmation: **"this independent analysis, which
probably needs to be repeated"** `[08:09]`, even while he reports the underlying numbers as fact.
Also hedges his own read of the Prop 65 number itself: "that's probably not an absolute upper
threshold, it's just a significantly interesting level" `[06:07]`.

## Conflicts with other sources

Directly corroborated by `9cmx-lt3n1w`, the later salt video in this batch, on the two brands both
name explicitly: **Celtic Sea Salt** is again the worst performer for lead, and Redmond again
shows meaningfully elevated lead — the same two brands failing across two independently described
tests roughly seventeen months apart. Jacobson is clean/low in both. A Diamond-brand salt
is the cleanest in both, though the exact product name differs between videos ("Diamond Kosher
salt flakes" here versus "Diamond Crystal Salt" in `9cmx-lt3n1w`), so this note treats them as
likely, but not confirmed, to be the same product. This video's Baja Gold finding (high lead)
has no counterpart in `9cmx-lt3n1w`, which does not test that brand.
