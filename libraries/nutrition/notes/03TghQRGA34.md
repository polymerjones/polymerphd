# Corn Flakes = Libido Killer

## Central claim

**The host argues corn flakes were invented in 1894 by one of the Kellogg brothers specifically to
suppress libido in patients at the Battle Creek Sanitarium, and that the food remains, by its
essentially nutrient-free composition, plausibly still doing exactly that to unknowing consumers
over a century later.** `[00:00]` "Cornflakes killing libido for more than 100 years is what it
should say on the label."

## The historical claim and the mechanism

`[00:00]` — **History**: developed in 1894 "with the intention of feeding them to patients in a
psychiatric institution to quell their libido." **Mechanism offered for today's consumers**: corn
flakes are "essentially no nutrients... just processed grains, basically like cardboard," and a
diet heavy in this kind of processed corn leads to nutrient deficiency, which the host connects
directly to loss of libido — extending the original intentional-suppression story into an
unintentional modern one, driven by the product's low nutrient density rather than a deliberately
added libido-suppressing ingredient.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Loss of libido** — attributed both to the original historical intent behind the product's
  formulation and to ongoing nutrient deficiency from processed-grain-heavy eating today `[00:00]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Mixed. The 1894/Battle Creek/libido-suppression origin story is specific and independently
corroborated elsewhere in this library (see Conflicts). The modern mechanism — that today's low
nutrient density in corn flakes is itself still suppressing libido via deficiency — is the host's
own extrapolation and isn't tied to a specific study on corn flakes and libido/nutrient status.

## Conflicts with other sources

`pvjeI77-7bA` ("The secret ingredient in corn flakes is a religious agenda," with Gary Fettke, MD)
covers the same origin story in far more depth — naming John Harvey Kellogg specifically, his
Seventh-day Adventist Church ties, and the framing as bland food meant "to quell our lustful
thoughts." `PmObqSKkX1w` ("The Disturbing History of Corn Flakes") independently corroborates the
same Battle Creek Sanitarium origin. All three sources converge tightly on the historical claim;
this short is the only one to add the specific 1894 date and the modern nutrient-deficiency
mechanism as a second, distinct line of argument.
