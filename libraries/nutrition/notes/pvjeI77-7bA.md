# The secret ingredient in corn flakes is a religious agenda. Revisionist history with Gary Fettke, MD

## Central claim

**Gary Fettke, an Australian orthopedic surgeon, was reported to the medical board and told he was
unqualified to give nutritional advice after recommending his diabetic patients cut sugar — and he
and his wife Belinda later found evidence the cereal industry had targeted him directly, which led
them to trace modern low-fat, cereal-based, anti-meat dietary guidelines back to Seventh-day
Adventist Church figures and institutions, including the 1890s origin of Kellogg's corn flakes as a
tool "to quell our lustful thoughts."** `[01:07]`, `[51:56]` Fettke's own framing of the guidelines:
**"effectively we have our dietary guidelines that have been written by the cereal industry based on
the ideology of the Adventist Church"** `[46:52]`.

## Fettke's professional battle

`[09:14]`–`[13:16]` — Fettke describes becoming the surgeon managing diabetic foot complications
for northern Tasmania while hospital patients with diabetes were served **"three ice cream[s] per
day"** alongside sugar-loaded breakfasts and juice. When he pushed back and began publicly
recommending patients cut sugar and processed food, dietitians at his hospital escalated to the
Dietitians Association of Australia, and he was reported to the Medical Board of Australia on three
occasions. He states plainly: "there was never a case of a patient being harmed. There was no
patient complaint... This was completely stirred up by the dietitians" `[23:26]`.

## The cereal industry's briefing document

`[13:16]`–`[16:20]` — Fettke describes a 2014 internal document from the Australian Breakfast Cereal
Manufacturers Forum (naming Nestlé, Kellogg's, Freedom Foods, Sanitarium) stating **"profits are
down in the cereal industry. The concepts of low carb and paleo are to blame,"** naming eight
people to target, including Fettke — "the only doctor... talking preventive health in the public
spectrum." He says this was only discovered "late last year" (i.e., 2018), years after the fact
`[14:17]`. The medical board's expert witness against him, he notes, "was actually working for
Sanitarium, which is a breakfast cereal company" `[17:21]`. A Senate inquiry into the medical review
process followed, and within two hours of Fettke giving adverse testimony against the medical
board, it "handed down a determination against me" barring him from giving nutritional advice
because his medical degree didn't qualify him `[18:23]`–`[19:24]`. He was ultimately cleared after
about two years, when an independent board "threw it out within two weeks" `[21:25]`–`[22:25]`.

## The fructose mechanism, in Fettke's own terms

`[32:34]`–`[38:42]` — Fettke says fructose metabolism was only properly described "in about 2010 by
Luc Tappy," so it postdates most textbooks. He describes fructose as following an aldehyde/alcohol
pathway in the liver, generating a uric acid byproduct that inhibits nitric oxide (a regulator of
circulation and inflammation), and feeding into small dense LDL and insulin resistance; he also
describes a hypothalamic/nucleus accumbens ("addiction center") effect. His clincher concept is the
**polyol pathway: normally about 3% of blood glucose converts to fructose, but in an insulin-
resistant person that rises to roughly 30%** — meaning high-carbohydrate eaters are generating far
more endogenous fructose than the sugar in their diet alone would suggest `[37:40]`–`[38:42]`. He
pairs this with polyunsaturated seed oils to describe a **"perfect storm"** of inflammation
`[38:42]`–`[39:43]`.

## Tracing the dietary guidelines to the Seventh-day Adventist Church

`[44:50]`–`[49:54]` — Belinda Fettke's research, per Gary, found the American Dietetic Association
was founded in October 1917 by Lenna Cooper, a Seventh-day Adventist and vegetarian working for
John Harvey Kellogg at the time; her textbooks became the standard nursing/dietetics texts for
"the next 30 to 40 years." On a 1980s US panel that first accepted vegetarian diets as safe, he
says **"eight of the nine on it were vegans or vegetarians"** and "five of the nine were
Adventists." He also flags a 2018 journal article (in *Religion*) by Joan Sabaté, an Adventist
sitting on the US dietary guidelines review panel, which Fettke says acknowledges this history
without Sabaté disclosing the conflict — though he adds the hedge, "I might be wrong by the time
this goes out" `[49:54]`.

## Corn flakes as an anti-masturbation device

`[51:56]`–`[53:57]` — Fettke states that John Harvey Kellogg, adopted at age 12 into Ellen G.
White's family and set to typesetting her writings — including "a letter to mothers to stop your
children from masturbating" — grew up to invent corn flakes in the 1890s **"to actually develop a
bland food to quell our lustful thoughts."** He connects this to Battle Creek, Michigan, where "101
cereal companies of the world were developed," most founded by Adventists. Saladino summarizes the
throughline: fiber-heavy, nutrient-poor cereal grains as a deliberate "hormonal break" replacing
meat, which the host frames earlier in his own words as a "chemical castrating thing" `[03:09]`.

## Institutional continuity: Ancel Keys, ILSI, and "lifestyle medicine"

`[54:58]`–`[68:14]` — Fettke connects Ancel Keys's starvation studies to Seventh-day Adventist
conscientious objectors ("he was clearly around the Adventists and their low-fat, anti-fat,
anti-meat philosophy"), though he's careful to add "I'm not saying he was the [cause]." He describes
"exercise is medicine" as a trademarked term originating from Coca-Cola funding, tied to the
International Life Sciences Institute (ILSI), and says a "Garden of Eden" plant-based curriculum had
reached **eight US medical schools** as of the interview. Loma Linda University, he notes, was
formerly called the "College of Medical Evangelists" `[84:35]`, and Saladino cites a study he recalls
finding poor sperm quality, motility, and count in vegan/vegetarian men there `[84:35]`–`[85:36]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Diabetic foot ulcers and amputations** — the clinical problem that originally drove Fettke's
  advocacy, described as "completely preventable" `[09:14]`–`[10:14]`.
- **Reduced immune/phagocyte function after high-carbohydrate post-anesthetic meals** — Fettke
  states fructose "shut[s] down the blood vessels... a little bit" and affects immunity, though he
  states the podcast ran out of time to cover this mechanism fully `[60:02]`–`[61:04]`.
- **Poor sperm quality/motility** in a vegan/vegetarian population, raised by Saladino as a study he
  recalls but does not name on-screen `[84:35]`–`[85:36]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Polyol pathway** — Fettke's term for the conversion of excess blood glucose into fructose
  in vivo, rising from roughly 3% to roughly 30% under insulin resistance `[37:40]`–`[38:42]`.
- **Generational education** — Fettke's term for uncritically inherited textbook beliefs: "you
  believe your teachers because that's what they tell you, but if your teachers have believed the
  same thing... over and over, that's continued on" `[47:52]`.
- **The Garden of Eden diet** — the fruit/nuts/seeds diet Fettke says Seventh-day Adventist doctrine
  frames as "the diet for salvation," not a health claim `[49:54]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Fettke is explicit that his gut-microbiome skepticism is unproven: **"I personally think... and I
cannot prove it yet that the whole gut microbiome story is white noise"** `[75:21]`. He similarly
hedges the Sabaté conflict-of-interest claim ("I might be wrong by the time this goes out")
`[49:54]` and the Coca-Cola/Adventist funding link ("that's the only conclusion we can draw," offered
without documentary proof) `[71:17]`–`[72:18]`. By contrast, the professional-persecution narrative
(the briefing document, the medical board rulings) is presented as documented fact, sourced to
"600 pages of internal documents of the cereal industry" `[15:19]`. The corn flakes/masturbation
history is stated with full confidence and no on-screen citation beyond "it's written in
textbooks."
