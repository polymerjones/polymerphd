# Topic Reference 02 — Oils and Fats

Ten videos anchor this file, splitting into three clusters: four take a direct run at seed oils
(`DqHqBWCW3Hs`, `c1kyubykrvA`, `lzazaZLnWNA`, `wrL-i4zy9xU`); four cover buying and cooking with
olive, avocado, and other cooking oils (`d4mYLeFxONQ`, `mV58zXMBco4`, `JpW1utNfQM0`, `Tg6qO3aEw9E`);
one is a dedicated tallow video (`-Wn32snt0YY`); and one turns the same skepticism applied to seed
oils onto fish oil (`AN0M9rUugpI`). The load-bearing finding is that **six of these ten videos,
made by different channels across three years, independently converge on the same heat-stability
ranking** — saturated animal fat over olive/avocado oil over seed oil — without citing each other.
Where the videos disagree or where a claim rests on an unnamed study, that is flagged rather than
smoothed over.

---

# 1. The organizing distinction: smoke point versus peroxidation index

Four sources make the same corrective move before giving any ranking: **smoke point is the wrong
number to be looking at.**

> "What you care about when you're cooking with an oil is the peroxidation index... [smoke point]
> is pretty much false." — `Tg6qO3aEw9E` `[03:01]`

> "Smoke point has nothing to do with how stable an oil is in the pan... what you want to think
> about is peroxidation index." — `-Wn32snt0YY` `[00:00]`–`[01:00]`

> "Don't confuse smoke point with peroxidation index." — `JpW1utNfQM0` `[04:04]`

`DqHqBWCW3Hs` uses the related term **peroxide value** to make the same underlying point: "the more
unsaturated an oil is, the more quickly it's going to be damaged when you heat it" `[05:04]`. The
shared mechanism across all four: more double bonds means faster oxidation under heat, regardless
of the temperature at which the oil visibly starts to smoke.

---

# 2. The convergent ranking: saturated animal fat > olive/avocado oil > seed oil

This ordering is the single most-repeated claim in the cluster. `Tg6qO3aEw9E`'s own note counts the
convergence directly: **"the ranking of seed oil > olive/avocado oil > tallow/butter/ghee by
oxidation risk... matches `d4mYLeFxONQ`, `mV58zXMBco4`, `JpW1utNfQM0`, and `DqHqBWCW3Hs` almost
exactly — five sources in this library now independently land on the same ordering"** `[03:01]`.
Add `-Wn32snt0YY`, which makes the identical argument in tallow-specific terms, and the count is
six.

| Video | Statement of the ranking | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| `Tg6qO3aEw9E` | Seed oils worst; olive/avocado "much better... but still not recommended for cooking"; saturated animal fats safest | `[04:01]`–`[10:06]` |
| `-Wn32snt0YY` | Tallow ~half monounsaturated, just under half saturated; "the more highly saturated a fat is, the more stable it is" when heated | `[02:03]` |
| `d4mYLeFxONQ` | Prefers tallow, butter, ghee over olive oil because they "resist the heat better" — olive oil is "less saturated" | `[01:01]`, `[06:07]` |
| `mV58zXMBco4` | "The more saturated the oil is, the more stable it is for cooking"; order given: tallow, then butter, then ghee | `[03:01]` |
| `JpW1utNfQM0` | Butter, ghee, tallow have a lower (better) peroxidation index than olive/avocado oil, which is lower than seed oils | `[04:04]`–`[05:05]` |
| `DqHqBWCW3Hs` | Refined tallow made more monounsaturated to stay liquid is flagged as a partial downgrade from "pure" (more saturated) tallow | `[05:04]`–`[06:04]` |

`Tg6qO3aEw9E` puts a number on where olive oil sits inside that ranking: **"10 to 15, sometimes
even 20% linoleic acid"** `[04:01]`, giving it "a lower peroxidation index than seed oils but... much
higher than more saturated fats." Across the cluster the stated cooking-fat order, most confident
to least, is consistently **tallow, then butter, then ghee** (`mV58zXMBco4` `[03:01]`; `Tg6qO3aEw9E`
`[07:03]`–`[10:06]`), with ghee's higher smoke tolerance attributed to its milk solids having been
removed.

# 3. Seed oils: the industrial-history narrative

Four videos independently tell a version of the same origin story — seed oil as machine or
military lubricant before it was food — and none of them cites any of the others.

| Video | Version of the history | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| `lzazaZLnWNA` | "There was no such thing as a seed oil before 1870... it wasn't really popularized for human consumption until 1910, 1911 with Crisco," widespread only by "the 1950s" | `[00:00]` |
| `c1kyubykrvA` | Rapeseed oil "originally developed in World War II to lubricate ships and steam engines because the oil sticks to wet metal"; Canada's wartime surplus led to the 1980 genetic modification that produced canola | `[00:00]`–`[01:01]` |
| `Tg6qO3aEw9E` | "Humans have never consumed seed oils without consuming seeds or nuts in our history until the last 110 years"; canola oil specifically credited with staying "slippery even when it's wet" in WWII naval use | `[01:00]`–`[04:01]` |
| `wrL-i4zy9xU` | Shows an actual "How It's Made" canola-refining clip — screw-press extraction, hexane solvent extraction, sodium hydroxide wash, centrifuging, wax removal — the process the other three summarize in words | `[38:43]`–`[46:52]` |

`lzazaZLnWNA` extends the timeline into a claim about disease trends, explicitly flagged by its own
speaker as unproven:

> "This is all correlation... we can only draw a hypothesis from this which we must test."
> — `lzazaZLnWNA` `[02:01]`

## The LDL / cholesterol-hypothesis disagreement, and where it actually diverges

`lzazaZLnWNA` ties the seed-oil-as-health-food narrative to a broader rejection of the cholesterol
hypothesis: saturated fat raises LDL, seed oil lowers it, and if LDL is treated as "a pure cause" of
atherosclerosis this makes animal fat look dangerous by comparison. The speaker calls that
framework **"totally wrong"** and extends the claim to all ApoB-containing lipoproteins (LDL, VLDL,
chylomicrons): "I do not believe that this particle... is causative of atherosclerosis" `[04:02]`–
`[08:06]` — stated as his own position, not settled consensus.

`wrL-i4zy9xU` makes a related but narrower argument, citing a named paper — **"Low density
lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease," from the European Atherosclerosis
Society** — and Steinberg and Witztum's finding that oxidized LDL specifically, not native LDL, is
what macrophages take up to form foam cells, the first step of arterial plaque `[84:41]`–`[94:50]`.
Its own note is explicit that these are compatible but not identical claims: **"`lzazaZLnWNA`'s
speaker rejects ApoB-containing lipoproteins as causative of atherosclerosis in general, while this
video's argument is narrower — that oxidized LDL specifically... is the required step"** — and
neither video cites the other.

# 4. Refining-process contaminants: benzene, phthalates, heavy metals, acrolein

`DqHqBWCW3Hs` and `c1kyubykrvA` converge on naming **benzene** specifically as a carcinogenic
solvent-extraction residue:

> "There have been studies... [finding] trace amounts of benzene, which is a known carcinogen [in
> solvent-extracted seed oils] — all seed oils are solvent extracted." — `DqHqBWCW3Hs` `[00:00]`

> "[Oil extraction is] exposed to many extracting agents that are carcinogenic like benzene [and]
> hexane, and heated to high temperatures leading to oxidation of these fragile oils." —
> `c1kyubykrvA` `[01:01]`

`DqHqBWCW3Hs` goes further into named heavy metals — antimony migrating from polyethylene
containers — and gives the sharpest phthalate figure in the whole set: a cited paper's calculated
**"average estrogenic equivalent of 2.7 to 958.1 nanograms of estradiol per liter — 45 to 396 times
those in bottled water"** `[01:00]`–`[02:01]`.

## Named-brand phthalate figures — pulled from two independent-seeming analyses that overlap

`mV58zXMBco4` and `JpW1utNfQM0` each report parts-per-billion phthalate figures for specific olive
and avocado oil brands. The two videos share an identical figure for one brand — the same "Mava"
avocado oil at roughly 50,000 ppb — which both notes flag as **"a genuine, verified overlap"**
indicating both draw on the same underlying analysis, not two independent findings:

| Brand | Type | Phthalates (ppb) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Newa | avocado oil | 75 | `JpW1utNfQM0` `[01:00]`–`[04:04]` |
| Brags | olive oil | ~650 | `JpW1utNfQM0` `[01:00]`–`[04:04]` |
| Chosen Foods | avocado oil | ~1,000+ | `JpW1utNfQM0` `[01:00]`–`[04:04]` |
| Primal Kitchen | avocado oil | ~2,000 | `JpW1utNfQM0` `[01:00]`–`[04:04]` |
| Unnamed olive oil #3 | olive oil | ~4,500 | `JpW1utNfQM0` `[01:00]`–`[04:04]` |
| Cobram-type | olive oil | over 6,000 | `JpW1utNfQM0` `[01:00]`–`[04:04]` |
| **Mava** | avocado oil | **~50,000** | `JpW1utNfQM0` `[01:00]`–`[04:04]`; also cited by `mV58zXMBco4` `[02:01]` |

For scale, `JpW1utNfQM0` notes that bottled water in plastic runs "1.5 to 4.5 parts per billion" of
phthalates by comparison `[00:00]`–`[02:03]` — meaning even the cleanest oil brand in the table above
(Newa, at 75 ppb) still runs well above bottled water's baseline, and the worst (Mava) runs roughly
ten thousand times higher.

Neither video names the lab, journal, or organization behind these figures in speech — both refer
only to "a recent independent analysis" (`JpW1utNfQM0` `[00:00]`) or "another analysis... a
Consumer Reports issue that I can link to" (`mV58zXMBco4` `[00:00]`–`[01:01]`), so the figures are
stated as fact but not citable beyond the video description.

`JpW1utNfQM0` and `mV58zXMBco4` both explain the mechanism the same way: an olive itself
**"shouldn't really have any phthalates in it"** — contamination comes from the oil's own
fat-attracting ("lipophilic") pull on plasticizers during pressing and storage in plastic
(`JpW1utNfQM0` `[00:00]`–`[02:03]`); `mV58zXMBco4` illustrates the same chemical class via an everyday
comparison — Glade plugins and rearview-mirror air fresheners `[00:00]`. `Tg6qO3aEw9E` adds a
related, brand-agnostic concern: plastic can and bottle linings for olive and avocado oil carrying
"endocrine disrupting chemicals" that are lipophilic `[04:01]`–`[07:03]`.

**Comparison to other fats:** `JpW1utNfQM0` cites unnamed studies finding "significantly lower
levels of phthalates" in butter, ghee, coconut oil, raw milk, and milk relative to the olive- and
avocado-oil figures above `[03:03]`.

# 5. Deep frying and acrolein — does switching to tallow actually fix it?

`DqHqBWCW3Hs` makes the most specific heat-breakdown claim in the cluster, then explicitly narrows
its own overstatement in real time:

> "The amount of acrolein in a large French fry at McDonald's is equivalent to the amount of
> acrolein in a pack of cigarettes." — `[03:02]`–`[04:04]`

> "People like to say Paul Saladino is saying that seed oils are worse than cigarettes, which is
> not what I just said... this is just one compound." — `[04:04]`

The video then examines Steak 'n Shake's switch to tallow fryers as a real-world test case and finds
it an incomplete fix: **"the fries were pre-soaked in seed oils"** before ever reaching the tallow
fryer, and the tallow itself had been refined to be more monounsaturated (less saturated) to stay
liquid, undercutting its own heat stability `[05:04]`–`[06:04]`. The closing point widens past any
single fat:

> "If you have any significant amount of your diet that's from a deep fried food, no matter what
> it's cooked in, you're probably not optimizing." — `DqHqBWCW3Hs` `[06:04]`

# 6. The deeper mechanism case: HNE, endocannabinoids, and oxidized LDL

`wrL-i4zy9xU` (a 111-minute long-form conversation) is the only video in this cluster to go past
"seed oil is bad" into a specific proposed biochemistry, and its own note is explicit that the
hosts consider human trial evidence incomplete: **"we don't have the studies that we would prefer
to have in humans that really put the nail in the coffin, so we have to come at it from different
angles"** `[67:22]`.

**HNE (4-hydroxynonenal)** — a breakdown product formed specifically from linoleic acid oxidation.
In early cell-culture research, cells "either died or got fat" `[51:01]`–`[54:07]`. One host
connects this to french fries being "the most fattening food that Americans eat... by six or seven
fold," attributed to HNE formed when linoleic acid is fried `[54:07]`.

**The endocannabinoid mechanism** — arachidonic acid (made from linoleic acid) converts in the gut
into endocannabinoids (AEA, 2-AG) that activate CB1 receptors, "the same receptor pathway THC
activates to cause the munchies" `[67:22]`–`[78:33]`. The CB1-blocking drug rimonabant **"nearly
eliminate[d] obesity in animals"** and improved human cardiometabolic markers but was pulled from
market because it "made people... want to kill themselves" `[70:27]`.

**Wild versus conventional feed as a linoleic-acid multiplier** — wild chickens and pigs are cited
at roughly **4–5% linoleic acid in their fat versus 16–25% in conventionally corn/soy-fed animals**
`[79:33]`–`[82:37]`.

**The clearance bottleneck** — humans lack a direct excretion route for excess polyunsaturated fat;
one cited kinetic study puts the half-life of stored omega-6 fat at **"something like 680 days"**
`[55:09]`–`[59:12]` — a figure the video's own note flags as resting on "one kinetic study," not a
consensus number.

The video also challenges the Swedish muffin overfeeding study often cited to defend
polyunsaturated fat, on the grounds that its comparison fat was **palm oil, not butter**, and palm
oil's fatty-acid position on the glycerol backbone makes it poorly tolerated even in infant formula
— reframing the study as comparing two poorly tolerated fats rather than testing a normal diet
`[60:13]`–`[67:22]`.

# 7. Olive oil: polyphenols, PREDIMED reread, and buying criteria

`d4mYLeFxONQ` reinterprets the landmark PREDIMED trial (over 7,000 participants, four-plus years,
published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*) on the grounds that its control group was not
olive-oil-free — **"the people in the control group were still using olive oil, but it wasn't the
good stuff... it was mostly refined olive oil"** `[02:03]`–`[03:03]`. Since oleic acid "is also
found in tallow and butter," the source infers the differentiator was more likely the extra-virgin
group's **polyphenol** content:

> "This doesn't seem like the benefits are coming from just olive oil or oleic acid... the benefits
> are probably from the polyphenols in olive oil." — `d4mYLeFxONQ` `[03:03]`

Three named polyphenols — oleocanthol, hydroxytyrosol, oleuropein — with hydroxytyrosol's claimed
effects repeatedly self-hedged: **"decreasing inflammation, potentially decreasing markers of
immune reactivity in the gut, *maybe* decreasing harmful species in the gut, *maybe* even
supporting the growth of beneficial species"** `[04:05]`.

## Certificate-of-analysis buying criteria

`d4mYLeFxONQ`, `mV58zXMBco4`, and `JpW1utNfQM0` converge, without citing each other, on the same
short list: **organic, extra virgin, opaque glass, cold-pressed, single-source.**

| Metric (certificate of analysis) | What it flags | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Acidity | Must be under 0.8 for extra-virgin status | `d4mYLeFxONQ` `[05:06]`–`[09:11]` |
| Peroxides | Must be under 20 meq/kg for extra-virgin status | `d4mYLeFxONQ` `[05:06]`–`[09:11]` |
| K index / delta K | Elevated delta K suggests contamination (e.g. seed-oil cutting) | `d4mYLeFxONQ` `[08:08]` |
| K232 / K270 | Light-absorption indices for degree of rancidity | `d4mYLeFxONQ` `[08:08]` |

`d4mYLeFxONQ` quantifies the adulteration risk directly: **"when I looked at data for Italian olive
oils, sometimes 10 or 15% are adulterated with seed oils"** `[08:08]`; `mV58zXMBco4` and
`JpW1utNfQM0` make the same claim without a percentage ("many olive oils are cut with seed oils,"
`mV58zXMBco4` `[01:01]`). `d4mYLeFxONQ`'s own freshness ceiling: **"a year is about the max I'm
going to go,"** despite bottles labeled "best before 2028" `[09:11]`. `mV58zXMBco4` adds that price
does not predict quality: **"it's probably essentially the same cost for a good versus a poor olive
oil"** `[02:01]`.

`d4mYLeFxONQ`'s note flags a real gap rather than a disagreement: it "never raises phthalate
contamination — the exact hazard that `JpW1utNfQM0` and `mV58zXMBco4` center their concern on,"
which reads as an emphasis gap, not a contradiction, since nothing in that video disputes the
finding.

# 8. Tallow: composition, nutrients, and sourcing

`-Wn32snt0YY` is the cluster's dedicated tallow video. Compositionally: **roughly half
monounsaturated fat (oleic acid) and just under half saturated fat (stearic, palmitic acid)**
`[02:03]`. Claimed nutrient content, each hedge preserved as stated:

- **Vitamin K2 (MK-7 form)** — "consistently associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes in
  trials like the DAM trial." On the animal-versus-plant form distinction: **"I strongly believe
  the form of vitamin K2 found in things like tallow... is a superior form,"** immediately followed
  by "there's still more research to be done there" `[03:04]`.
- **Stearic acid** — cited to "an in vivo study published in Nature Communications" showing
  improved mitochondrial function, plus epidemiologic association with "lower body mass...
  improved cardiovascular outcomes, lower rates of diabetes" `[04:07]`.
- **Trans-vaccenic acid** — raised via a Nature paper the host paraphrases as concluding it "might
  help us fight cancer" through T-cell reprogramming — explicitly flagged by the host as the
  paper's own hedge, not his own claim `[04:07]`.
- **Conjugated linoleic acid (CLA)**, distinguished from ordinary linoleic acid — correlated with
  leanness, mechanism stated as unresolved: **"we don't exactly understand the mechanism here"**
  `[05:09]`.
- **Odd-chain fatty acids C15/C17** — found in dairy fat and tallow, "consistently associated...
  with improved outcomes in humans" `[05:09]`.

Sourcing preference: **100% grass-fed, grass-finished, regeneratively raised**, specifically to
avoid glyphosate carryover from sprayed grain feed and to avoid solvent-based rendering
`[05:09]`–`[06:10]`. The video directly disputes mainstream guidance: **"canola oil is not
healthier than tallow, Harvard, you got it all wrong, and Mayo Clinic too"** `[06:10]`.

# 9. Fish oil: the same skepticism, turned on omega-3

`AN0M9rUugpI` applies the cluster's own logic — susceptibility to oxidation, industrial refining —
back onto fish oil, arguing it deserves the same scrutiny as seed oil rather than exemption from it:

> "Fish oil is the new seed oil... there's no reason for any of you guys listening to this podcast
> to be taking fish oil." — `[00:00]`–`[03:03]`

**The atrial fibrillation claim:** citing unnamed studies "in JAMA, maybe in one of the Journal of
American Cardiology journals," the host states increased arrhythmia risk at fish-oil doses of
**"four grams... per day plus"** `[00:00]` — no specific study, author, or year given in speech.

**The processing parallel:** fish oil supplements are "concentrated... refined... bleached...
deodorized," with fish burps offered as **"a clear sign of rancidity"** — even pharmaceutical-grade
capsules, the host says, are engineered to bite without tasting rancid, which he attributes to
processing rather than genuine freshness `[00:00]`–`[02:02]`.

**The oxidation argument:** the guest states omega-3s have more double bonds than omega-6s, making
them "more susceptible to oxidation, not only when they're on the shelf, but also when they're in
our tissues," and connects this to the **membrane pacemaker theory of aging** `[02:02]`–`[04:05]`.
He extends the claim across preparations — pharmaceutical-grade triglyceride fish oil, cod liver
oil, even antioxidant-fortified versions — asserting elevated lipid peroxidation in each case, and
cites animal studies where higher omega-3 intake "shortens their lifespan... increases lipid
peroxidation and... interferes with mitochondrial respiration" `[03:03]`–`[04:05]`.

The host is explicit that his own LDL-oxidation extrapolation is a guess, not a citation:

> "I don't know if there's actually a study showing that... but I wouldn't be surprised if there's
> actually data on that. Maybe not in humans, but certainly in vitro, which would be suspect."
> — `[01:01]`

`wrL-i4zy9xU` also raises fish-oil skepticism, but by a different mechanism entirely — its
objection centers on fish oil's **triglyceride** omega-3 form versus whole fish's mixed
triglyceride/phospholipid form, not on oxidation or membrane effects. The two videos reach
fish-oil skepticism from separate arguments and do not cite each other.

# 10. Practical summary

1. **Ignore smoke point as a buying or cooking criterion.** Six independent videos in this cluster
   (`Tg6qO3aEw9E`, `-Wn32snt0YY`, `JpW1utNfQM0`, `d4mYLeFxONQ`, `mV58zXMBco4`, `DqHqBWCW3Hs`) instead
   point to peroxidation index / peroxide value, and all six land on the same order: **tallow,
   butter, ghee** first; **olive and avocado oil** second (topping/dressing use, not cooking); **seed
   oils** last.
2. **Buy olive and avocado oil the same way regardless of channel:** organic, extra virgin,
   cold-pressed, opaque glass, single-source, checked against a certificate of analysis
   (acidity, peroxides, delta K, K232/K270) if one is available.
3. **Treat brand-name phthalate figures as real but not universally verified in speech** — the Mava
   avocado-oil ~50,000 ppb figure is corroborated across two videos, but neither names the testing
   lab on camera.
4. **A tallow-cooked fryer is not automatically a clean fryer** — pre-soaking in seed oil and
   refining the tallow itself toward less-saturated can undercut the switch (`DqHqBWCW3Hs`).
5. **Seed oil's industrial-lubricant history is corroborated by four independent videos** with no
   cross-citation, but the leap from that history to specific disease-rate claims is explicitly
   flagged by more than one of those same sources as correlation, not proof.
6. **Fish oil gets the same scrutiny as seed oil in this cluster**, not a pass — via two different
   mechanisms (oxidation/membrane effects in `AN0M9rUugpI`; triglyceride-versus-phospholipid form in
   `wrL-i4zy9xU`) that agree on the conclusion without agreeing on the reason.
