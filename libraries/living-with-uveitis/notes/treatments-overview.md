# Treatments

## Central claim

**Uveitis treatment is a ladder that runs from minimally invasive eye drops to surgical implants,
and the central decision at every rung is localized (affecting only the eye) versus systemic
(affecting the whole body) — which depends on whether the uveitis is tied to another disease.**
Stated directly: **"If the patient's uveitis is associated with another disease or disorder,
treatment will likely be systemic... whereas if the cause of the uveitis is unknown, localized
treatments... may be an option"** `[¶1]`.

## The drug-class ladder

`[¶1]`

**Eye drops** — steroid drops (Prednisolone Acetate, Dexamethasone, Loteprednol, Rimexolone) for
inflammation, and dilating drops for the fixed pupils associated with anterior uveitis.
**Immunosuppressants**, taken systemically, don't discriminate between cells: **"the medication
does not differentiate between cells. The patient's entire immune system is suppressed."**
**Corticosteroids** — prednisone is named as usually the first line: **"one of the first
medications prescribed for uveitis, among a myriad of other conditions. It's so commonly prescribed
that side effects may be downplayed or not discussed."** **Antimetabolites** — methotrexate
**"partially inhibits dihydrofolate reductase... within the cells"** and can take **"about three
months before the treatment has an effect."** **T-cell/calcineurin inhibitors** — cyclosporine
("often used in conjunction with Methotrexate... Hair growth is a common side effect") and
tacrolimus. **Biologic agents** — TNF inhibitors like adalimumab (Humira, **FDA-approved for
non-infectious uveitis in 2016**) target excess Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha Protein.

## Localized delivery: injections and implants

`[¶1]`

Periocular/intravitreal injections deliver corticosteroid directly around or into the eye, a
method described as more common **"before longer-acting intravitreal implants were developed."**
Implants go further — **"injected into the vitreous humor of the eye during an in-office
procedure... release a sustained dosage of the medication over time and eventually dissolve"** —
but the tradeoff is explicit: **"Side effects should not be taken likely and can (and probably
will) include: cataracts and increased eye pressure."** Three specific implants are compared:
**Ozurdex** (0.7mg dexamethasone, up to 6 months), **Yutiq** (.18mg fluocinolone acetonide, up to
36 months), and **Retisert** (0.59mg fluocinolone acetonide, FDA-approved 2005, up to 3 years,
**"contains a higher dosage of medication than the other implants and is larger (about the size of
a grain of rice)"** and is surgically implanted rather than injected.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Antimetabolite** — "a substance that interferes with the normal metabolic processes within
  cells and prevents them from surviving and/or multiplying by targeting specific enzymes" `[¶1]`.
- **Calcineurin inhibitor** — a medicine that blocks the enzyme calcineurin, which activates
  T-cells `[¶1]`.
- **TNF (Tumor Necrosis Factor) inhibitor** — a biologic that blocks excess TNF-alpha protein
  believed to drive inflammation `[¶1]`.
