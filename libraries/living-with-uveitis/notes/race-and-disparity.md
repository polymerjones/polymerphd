# Uveitis, Race and Disparity in Healthcare

## Central claim

**Written the week of a serious personal flare during the George Floyd protests, this post is an
explicit, self-described amateur research effort into what's actually known about race,
socioeconomic disparity, and uveitis outcomes — framed by the author's own acknowledged privilege
in accessing care.** Stated directly: **"I have the privilege of having access to excellent health
care and insurance coverage... that afford me clinic visits, fundus photography, and medication.
Without these things, this flare may have led to a road that I don't let my mind go down"**
`[¶1]`. She's explicit about the post's own limits: **"this is by no means a complete analysis.
It's just me doing some research while my kids are asleep to initiate thought"** `[¶1]`.

## What the epidemiological studies actually found

`[¶1]`

Findings are reported with their limitations named alongside them. A 5,106-person self-reported
sample found **"no specific ethnic group predominance, which may be partly due to the small sample
size"** (González et al., 2018) — contrasted with the Pacific Ocular Inflammation Study's higher
incidence in white populations, and a report of **"higher incidence of uveitis in African American
patients with inflammatory bowel disease"**. A Birmingham, Alabama chart review found **"African
Americans are more likely to have panuveitis while Caucasians are more likely to have posterior
uveitis"** (Read et al., 2012). A pediatric study found **"increased severity of poor visual
outcome in non-Hispanic African American children,"** with cause left explicitly open: **"could be
secondary to biologic differences or variance in health-care access"** (Angeles-Han et al., 2015).

## Disparity in access, separated from disease biology

`[¶1]`

International disparity is framed as access-driven, not purely biological: **"blindness due to
uveitis... is reported as 25% in India and other developing countries, whereas... 10% or less in
Europe and the United States"** — attributed partly to infectious causes predominating where care
access is worse (Rao, 2013), while non-infectious/autoimmune causes dominate in developed
countries. A workforce-diversity paper is cited on physician-patient concordance: **"when
physicians and patients share the same race or ethnicity, this improves time spent together,
medication adherence, shared decision-making... Not surprisingly, implicit bias from the physician
is decreased."**

## Source-stated confidence

Unusually explicit about evidentiary caution throughout: **"Again, correlation does not equal
causation"**, and multiple cited findings are presented alongside their own stated limitations
(sample size, geographic scope) rather than as settled conclusions. The post opens by naming itself
as informal research, not a systematic review.
