# Prednisone: The Good, The Bad, & the Ugly (Part II)

## Central claim

**Tapering off prednisone is harder, and its risk-benefit calculus during pregnancy and a pandemic
is more nuanced, than "just take less" — this post continues Part I with the practical mechanics
of coming off the drug and two specific situations (pregnancy, COVID-19) where the guidance is
conditional rather than universal.** Framed directly: **"The importance of tapering cannot be
overstated... it's important to wean your body off the supplemented steroid so it does not crash
and leave you with a whole host of painful (and potentially dangerous) withdrawal symptoms"**
`[¶1]`.

## Withdrawal gets worse, not better, at lower doses

`[¶1]`

A counterintuitive pattern from her own repeated tapers: **"when I have gone to taper off of
prednisone, from only 10 mg, even when I thought I was tapering slowly, I have had excruciating
joint pain... I felt like I was 80 years old!"** A cited explanation resolved her confusion:
**"'[Withdrawal] Symptoms are milder at high cortisol amounts and intensify when milligrams are
reduced below a certain point. A longer adjustment period is recommended at lower doses'"**
(Adrenal Insufficiency United). Withdrawal symptoms listed include severe fatigue, joint pain,
fever, body aches, and — flagged as rare but serious — **"Adrenal crisis, a rare, possibly fatal
reaction to a lack of steroid hormone in your body."**

## Pregnancy and breastfeeding, from direct experience

`[¶1]`

**"I took a low dosage (~10 mg if I recall correctly) during my two pregnancies and while
breastfeeding, and my sons incurred no known side effects"** — stated explicitly as personal
experience rather than general advice: **"I'm not advocating one way or another, this is just my
experience."** She made a specific precautionary choice regardless of the low measured risk: **"I
did make the choice to not donate breast milk to anyone because of my medication."**

## COVID-19-specific corticosteroid guidance

`[¶1]`

A 139-expert consensus survey on non-infectious uveitis (NIU) treatment during COVID-19 is
summarized with its risk-stratified structure preserved rather than flattened into one rule:
**"Surveyed uveitis experts recommended to not begin systemic corticosteroid or immunosuppression
for NIU treatment in sick patients with suspected or confirmed COVID-19, irrespective of risk
group"** — but **"in healthy patients, experts agreed to start oral corticosteroids only in
increased risk patients."** The overarching direction: **"local therapy (regional corticosteroid
injections) should be preferred to systemic treatment in all patients... except in healthy patients
not already on corticosteroids,"** because systemic steroids **"might be harmful, given their
mechanism of action that inhibits the immune responses and affects the pathogen clearance."**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- Severe joint pain specifically during tapering from a *low* (10mg) dose, described as
  unexpectedly worse than tapering had been at higher doses `[¶1]`.
- The withdrawal symptom list: fatigue, fever, stiff/tender muscles, lightheadedness, labored
  breathing, headaches, depression, anxiety `[¶1]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Adrenal crisis** — a rare, potentially fatal withdrawal reaction from insufficient steroid
  hormone, distinguished here from the milder, more common withdrawal symptoms `[¶1]`.
- **Immunomodulatory Treatment (IMT)** — "treatment of disease by activating or suppressing the
  immune system," the umbrella term used in the cited COVID-19 consensus paper `[¶1]`.
