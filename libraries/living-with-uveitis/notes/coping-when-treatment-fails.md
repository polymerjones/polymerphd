# Coping when a treatment fails

## Central claim

**A deliberate push toward "stability" in early 2020 instead triggered a new flare within weeks of
tapering prednisone off a Yutiq implant — told as it happened, alongside a reframe of what
"treatment failure" actually means clinically.** The trigger moment: **"I noticed a smudge on my
glasses. When I took them off to clean them, the smudge was still there. My heart dropped to my
stomach"** `[¶1]`. She names the deeper frustration directly: **"In my quest for overall health
stability, I had inadvertently destabilized my treatment"** `[¶1]`.

## Why "failure" isn't binary

`[¶1]`

The post pulls in a cited, numeric reframe of drug efficacy rather than treating it as pass/fail:
**"When one looks carefully at individual drugs and different regimens, Dr. Jabs said, any one drug
works about 50 to 70 percent of the time, and any approach works 70 to 85 percent of the time, but
nothing works 100 percent of the time... You can't rigidly stick with a single approach and expect
it to work with everyone. This is where you move out of the science and into the art of
medicine"** (Doran, AAO EyeNet, 2012). It also lists concrete, generalizable reasons a medication
can appear ineffective: **time to "kick in,"** special instructions, drug/food **interactions**,
**dosage**, or simply **"not a match."**

## The emotional structure of the setback

`[¶1]`

She draws an explicit (and explicitly qualified) analogy to grief: **"Receiving a disease diagnosis
is not something you plan on. It can feel almost like the death of your old 'healthy' self...
(Not trying to equate failure of one treatment with the death of a spouse… but feelings are
relatable.)"** The practical resolution: starting CellCept with a prednisone pulse, then **"we
decided to do an Ozurdex injection to allow me to taper down to 10mg of prednisone during this
COVID-19 pandemic"** — buying time to see if CellCept would work on its own, with Humira named as
the next option if not.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- A new blind spot appearing suddenly, described concretely as "black spots mixed in with the
  white snowflakes falling" and a smudge that wouldn't clean off eyeglasses `[¶1]`.
- Bilateral activity confirmed on optic photography during the same flare `[¶1]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Treatment failure as a small-scale version of grief** — explicitly caveated by the author
  herself as not equivalent to a major loss, but "feelings are relatable" `[¶1]`.
- **"Plan B, or more like Plan LMNOP at this point"** — a light, reusable way to describe having
  cycled through many treatment options already `[¶1]`.

The 50–70%/70–85% efficacy figures are directly attributed to a named physician (Dr. Jabs) via a
cited article, not presented as the author's own estimate. The grief analogy is explicitly
self-qualified as a comparison of feeling, not severity.
