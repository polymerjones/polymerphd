# Uveitis Basics

## Central claim

**Uveitis is not a single disease but a group of inflammatory diseases classified by where in the
eye the inflammation sits and how long it lasts — and for at least a third of patients, no cause
is ever found.** The page opens with the clinical definition: **"Inflammation of the uvea; a term
describing a group of inflammatory diseases that produces swelling and destroys eye tissues"**
`[¶1]`, and states plainly that **"Uveitis is the third leading cause of preventable blindness in
the developed world"** `[¶1]`.

## How it's classified

`[¶1]`

Two independent axes of classification are given. By **duration**: acute (sudden, limited),
recurrent (episodes separated by ≥3 months of inactivity), or chronic (relapse within <3 months of
stopping treatment). By **location**: anterior (iris — "the most common type"), intermediate
(often the vitreous, "associated with sarcoidosis and multiple sclerosis"), posterior (choroid
and/or retina, including a rare form called "Birdshot" Retinochoroidopathy), or panuveitis
("Inflammation across all areas of the eye"). This location-based scheme traces to a specific
2004 effort: **"a group of uveitis specialists met in a working group known as Standardization of
Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN)... established terminology and methods for reporting clinical data so
that doctors are all speaking the same language regarding the disease"** `[¶1]`.

## Causes, and how often none is found

`[¶1]`

Uveitis itself is not contagious, but its *causes* split into infectious (bacteria, parasites,
fungus, viruses — linked to rubella, HIV, herpes, syphilis, tuberculosis, Lyme disease) and
non-infectious (injury, trauma, or autoimmune/systemic inflammatory disease — ankylosing
spondylitis, inflammatory bowel disease, juvenile rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis,
sarcoidosis, Behçet's disease, among others). Critically: **"The cause of non-infectious uveitis
can also be unknown (idiopathic). This is true for at least one-third of the cases of uveitis."**
Two competing mechanistic theories are named without the page picking a winner: a T-cell-mediated
autoimmune process (**"driven by a T-cell mediated autoimmune process and perpetuated by
pro-inflammatory cytokines"**, quoting Yeh & Shantha 2016), versus an auto-inflammatory pathway
where **"there is a genetic mutation in the innate (rather than adaptive) immune system"**.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- Light sensitivity, blurred vision, floaters, flashes, decreased vision, eye pain, eye redness
  `[¶1]`.
- Diagnostic indicators found on exam rather than reported by the patient: anterior chamber flare,
  keratic precipitates, synechiae, the HLA-B27 gene marker, macular edema, retinal lesions, and
  retinal vasculitis `[¶1]`.
- Chronic complications named as downstream of the disease itself: cataracts, elevated
  intraocular pressure, retinal swelling, retinal detachment, and permanent vision loss `[¶1]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Uveitis** — inflammation of the uvea, defined here as a group of diseases rather than one
  condition `[¶1]`.
- **Panuveitis** — inflammation across all areas of the eye, the most extensive of the four
  location-based categories `[¶1]`.
- **Idiopathic** — uveitis with no identifiable cause, stated to account for at least a third of
  cases `[¶1]`.
- **SUN (Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature)** — the 2004 working group whose classification
  scheme this page's location-based categories come from `[¶1]`.

## Source-stated confidence

The page is careful to separate established classification (duration and location, sourced to the
SUN working group's published paper) from causal theory, which it presents as competing and
unresolved: the autoimmune and auto-inflammatory mechanisms are both described as theories, with
an explicit note that **"the underlying cause of idiopathic uveitis... involve[s] the immune
system"** without settling on one explanation.
