# Evil Eyes

## Central claim

**A deliberately lighter post prompted by her sons noticing that villains in movies have red
eyes** — she has red eyes fairly often from posterior uveitis and goes exploring, half-seriously,
why red/glowing eyes read as evil across culture and alternative medicine, while explicitly
declining to draw any real clinical conclusion from it. Framed directly: **"My sons noticed right
away that the bad guys and gals so often have red eyes. It made me feel… well, kind of bad... Does
it make me a villain?"** `[¶1]`.

## Trying acupuncture, honestly reported as untestable

`[¶1]`

She tried acupuncture on a naturopath's suggestion and is candid about why she can't actually
evaluate whether it worked: **"I told her that I had posterior uveitis, was on medication and
rarely knew when I was having a flare, so there would be literally no way of knowing if acupuncture
helped."** She reports the experience positively regardless — **"I did enjoy the sessions"** — and
relays what her acupuncturist told her: **"the eyes were connected to the liver... According to
Traditional Chinese Medicine, all diseases involving the eye are closely related to the liver."**
Two specific points used are named: **Jingming (UB-1)** and **Zanzhu (UB-2)**, both on the Urinary
Bladder Channel, described as useful "for eye problems of all kinds from early-stage cataracts or
glaucoma to hysteria with vision loss."

## The "evil eye" and blue-eye superstition

`[¶1]`

Historical detour: **"around 100 AD, Plutarch, the Greek philosopher, explained... that the human
eye was a powerful organ that could emit invisible energy rays"** strong enough, he claimed, to
harm children and animals — and that **"people with blue eyes"** were considered especially
dangerous casters of such curses, since blue eyes were rare and seemed unnatural around the
Mediterranean at the time. This is given as the origin of the cobalt-blue **nazar** amulet still
used today.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- Red eyes as a visible sign, though not specifically tied to anterior uveitis in her own case:
  **"It's not uncommon for me to have red eyes (and I don't even have anterior uveitis, which is
  characterized by inflammation in the visible part of the eye)"** `[¶1]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

None new in a clinical sense — the "Liver Fire" and Qi concepts introduced here are explicitly
framed as Traditional Chinese Medicine terminology, not Western diagnostic terms.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The Halloween "good vs. evil" framing as a springboard for a symptom-and-symbolism post** —
  reusable as a light-tone template for a post exploring folklore around a visible disease sign
  without making clinical claims `[¶1]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Explicitly and repeatedly self-aware that this post is exploratory and not evidence-based: she
notes she "was skeptical going in" to acupuncture, cannot assess its effect on her own condition,
and closes the TCM section with a joke clarifying its limits: **"(note: evil villain comment is NOT
part of Chinese medicine)."** The historical/mythological material is presented as cultural history,
not medical claim.

## Conflicts with other sources

No conflicts — this is the library's one deliberately non-clinical, exploratory post. It doesn't
contradict `healthy-vision`'s more grounded lifestyle content; if anything it demonstrates the same
willingness to try complementary approaches (acupuncture here, diet/stress reduction there) while
being explicit about what can and can't be concluded from personal experience alone.
