# New Year, Same Blur

## Central claim

**Written during the pandemic's worst winter surge, this post captures a specific, concrete
tradeoff many chronically ill parents faced: weighing a child's in-person schooling against the
parent's own immunosuppression risk — alongside a pointed observation about how vaccine
prioritization language treated that risk.** Framed directly: **"At this point, I'm not willing to
risk my vision so that he can spend half his day in a mask. I can't help but feel guilty. Kids need
to socialize right? What is worse – having no social life temporarily or potentially having a blind
parent?"** `[¶1]`.

## Vaccine prioritization, and a pointed comparison

`[¶1]`

She quotes the CDC's own hedged language on immunocompromised risk: **"'Based on what we know at
this time, adults of any age with the following conditions might be at an increased risk for
severe illness... Immunocompromised state... from... use of corticosteroids.'"** Her response
draws a specific contrast: **"'Might be,' as in, somewhere down the list from those with conditions
that 'are' at an increased risk. Among those conditions is smoking, which is a hard pill for me to
swallow... since that's a choice, and uveitis is Not a choice."** In Washington's tiered rollout,
that placed her at **"at least Phase 2... currently listed under 'future phases.'"**

## The Avastin series concludes

`[¶1]`

The CNV treatment series from `latest-on-my-uveitis` reaches its endpoint here: **"my eyes were
quiet at my appointment this month. 'Finally!' in the words of my doctor, as we both took a breath
before she injected my final Avastin shot (hopefully) of the series."** She also notes a concrete
site change made because of that experience: **"I moved some things around on the website and
created the 'Complications' page, under Treatments, which includes side effects stemming from
uveitis and treatments of uveitis"** — i.e., this post directly documents the origin of
`treatment-complications`.

## Source-stated confidence

The CDC risk-category language is quoted directly and precisely, including its own hedge
("might be"), which she then explicitly contrasts with unhedged risk-factor language for smoking —
a deliberate rhetorical point about how the language itself signals different levels of societal
sympathy, not a claim about relative biological risk.
