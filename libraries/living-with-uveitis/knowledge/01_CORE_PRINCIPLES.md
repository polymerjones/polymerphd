# 01 — Core Principles

The ideas below recur across the reference pages and the five years of blog posts in this
collection. They are not any single post's claim — they are the patterns visible once the
educational pages and the real-time patient narrative are read side by side.

---

## The disease is classified along two independent axes: duration and location

Uveitis is not one condition but a location-based (anterior, intermediate, posterior, panuveitis)
and duration-based (acute, recurrent, chronic) classification scheme, established by a 2004
specialist working group. Nearly every reference page and several blog posts hang their content on
this scaffold — see `uveitis-overview` and `anatomy-of-the-eye`. It also explains why two patients
with the "same" diagnosis can have very different disease: `qa-george`'s diagnosis
(Multifocal Choroiditis with panuveitis) and the site author's own (Multifocal Choroiditis,
described as posterior in `latest-on-my-uveitis`) share a name but differ in anatomic extent.

## For posterior/chronic uveitis specifically, there is often no reliable subjective warning sign

A pattern stated explicitly as early as `diagnosis-story` ("I never actually know how long
something's been going on in my eye before I see or feel symptoms") recurs across the entire
collection: a felt sense of stability is not evidence of disease control. `coping-when-treatment-fails` describes a flare that arrived with no warning during what felt like a stable period;
`prednisone-for-life`, the collection's most recent post, describes an identical pattern five years
later — "my eyes felt super good this morning... if not for the fundus photography... it likely
would have gone undetected." This is the direct rationale for the recurring imaging routine
described in `into-the-eyes-of-the-beholder` and `tools-tests-terms`: monitoring exists specifically
because the patient's own senses cannot be relied on.

## Complications more often come from treatment than from the disease directly

`treatment-complications` states this as an explicit thesis — "it's not the disease itself that
creates the most damage, but the complications and ancillary issues" — and the blog posts bear it
out repeatedly. Steroid treatment for inflammation causes cataracts and elevated eye pressure
(`prednisone-part-i`); pressure-lowering surgery for that steroid-induced pressure creates a bleb,
which can itself leak years later (`diffusing-the-bomb`); intravitreal injections repeated over
years require their own risk management (`intravitreal-injection-tutorial`). The chain of
complication-causing-complication is treated as the norm, not the exception.

## Localized versus systemic treatment is the central treatment-ladder decision, not drug choice alone

`treatments-overview` frames the primary treatment decision as localized (drops, injections,
implants — affecting only the eye) versus systemic (oral or injected immunosuppression — affecting
the whole body), driven by whether the uveitis is tied to an identifiable systemic disease or is
idiopathic. The same tradeoff resurfaces in COVID-19-era clinical guidance summarized in
`prednisone-part-ii`, where expert consensus specifically favored local over systemic treatment
during active infection risk.

## Treatment "failure" is probabilistic and graded, not binary

`coping-when-treatment-fails` cites a specific reframe — any single drug works roughly 50–70% of
the time, any approach 70–85%, nothing 100% — used to explain why a treatment that stops working
isn't evidence of a wrong diagnosis or a uniquely resistant case. This same graded view underlies
the collection's long medication-escalation arc (prednisone → antimetabolites → biologics →
localized injections) traced across `immunosuppressed-pnw`, `coping-when-treatment-fails`, and
`latest-on-my-uveitis`.

## Being immunosuppressed for a rarely-recognized disease creates specific access and belief problems

Three independent threads converge on this: standard risk categories and eligibility checklists
routinely fail to name uveitis specifically, forcing self-advocacy at the point of care
(`third-times-the-charm`, `new-year-same-blur`); an invisible disease in a young patient collides
with age-based assumptions about health (`qa-jack-pritchard`, `what-you-dont-see`); and low public
awareness of uveitis itself is named, independently, by three different patients as a primary
burden alongside the physical disease (`qa-george`, `qa-jack-pritchard`, `stories-support`).

## A steroid-sparing dose is a distinct, named treatment target — not simply "less than before"

`treatments-overview` and `prednisone-part-i` both define a specific low-dose threshold (below
7.5mg) below which most prednisone side effects aren't typically experienced. `prednisone-for-life`
shows this playing out as a real clinical endpoint: after a year-long taper, the conclusion isn't
"keep reducing" but arriving at a specific, sustainable, low maintenance dose — going further
(to zero) triggered a subclinical recurrence.

## A substantial share of uveitis is idiopathic, and sex and race are documented but incompletely understood variables

At least a third of uveitis cases have no identified cause (`uveitis-overview`). Where causes or
correlates are studied, the collection is careful to preserve the field's own uncertainty rather
than overstate it: `pregnancy-journey` cites competing hormonal and genetic explanations for why
women predominate among patients, and `race-and-disparity` explicitly states "correlation does not
equal causation" while surveying racial/ethnic differences in presentation and access to care.
