# How to make friends as an adult, according to a Harvard psychiatrist | Robert Waldinger

## Central claim

**Loneliness is a purely subjective, physiologically damaging state — distinct from social
isolation — and the practical route to reducing it as an adult is not seeking deep closeness
directly, but engineering repeated casual contact with the same people around something you
already care about doing.** `[08:14]` "One way to make new relationships, if you're feeling too
isolated, is to do something you care about alongside other people... it puts you in contact with
the same people over and over again."

## Loneliness versus isolation: a subjective distinction

`[02:03]`–`[03:05]` Waldinger is explicit that only the person themselves can determine whether
they are lonely: **"loneliness is the sense that I am less connected to other people than I want
to be. It's a very subjective experience. No one can tell you whether you're lonely or not."** He
notes you can be lonely inside a marriage or a crowd, and conversely content and unlonely alone on
a mountaintop — artists and writers seeking solitude are given as an example of chosen isolation
without loneliness.

## The physiological cost of loneliness

`[04:08]`–`[05:10]` Loneliness and isolation are described as chronic low-level stressors, keeping
the body "in a kind of chronic low-level fight or flight mode," with elevated cortisol and
inflammation that "break down our coronary arteries... our joints." A specific comparison is
attributed to researcher Julianne Holt-Lunstad: loneliness's health risk is "as dangerous to our
health as smoking... half a pack of cigarettes a day. As dangerous as being obese."

## "Mattering": why small encounters count

`[05:10]`–`[07:12]` Waldinger names "mattering" — feeling that one's presence is noticed and
appreciated — as central to the sense of belonging, and argues it isn't limited to close
relationships: **"it's not just our closest relationships that matter... It's the person who
delivers the mail if we have a friendly little chat. It's the cashier who checks us out."** A
specific illustration: a minister's practice of reading people's name tags and greeting them by
name through an airport security line, which Waldinger says makes people "feel noticed" instead of
anonymous.

## Building new friendships: shared activity over direct pursuit

`[07:12]`–`[09:14]` The stated mechanism: relationships often begin through repeated casual
encounters with the same people (the "water cooler" or coffee machine at work is named as the
iconic example), so the practical strategy is to **"do something you care about alongside other
people"** — volunteering, a soup kitchen, a gardening club, a bowling league — which supplies both
repeated contact and a built-in conversation starter. A second route named is being of service:
tutoring, reading to a child, or teaching a skill others would be grateful to learn.

## When loneliness itself blocks connection

`[09:14]`–`[10:16]` Waldinger notes lonely people can inadvertently signal that they don't want to
be approached, out of fear, which cognitive behavioral therapy techniques specifically target by
teaching social skills and revising the assumption that others don't want contact. His closing
recommendation: find a setting or activity where you're comfortable, and do it around other people
to "see what develops."

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **A pervasive sense of being less connected to others than desired, even while surrounded by
  people** — named as the definition of loneliness itself, distinct from actual isolation.
  `[02:03]`
- **Chronic stress-like physical symptoms with no obvious cause** — connected to sustained
  loneliness/isolation via elevated cortisol and inflammation. `[04:08]`
- **Feeling invisible, unheard, or that one's contributions don't register (e.g. in meetings, at
  work)** — named directly as the experience of "not mattering," especially common for marginalized
  people in a workplace. `[05:10]`
- **Feeling that others don't want to be approached, or fear of rejection when trying to connect**
  — named as a pattern lonely people can develop and unintentionally signal, addressable through
  learned social skills. `[09:14]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Mattering** — the sense that one's presence and contributions are noticed and appreciated by
  others, presented as a distinct component of belonging alongside closeness. `[05:10]`

## Analogies worth reusing

The office "water cooler" (or coffee machine) as the iconic example of a setting that produces
relationships through repeated, low-stakes casual contact — used as the model for how to
deliberately manufacture similar contact elsewhere in life. `[07:12]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as direct expertise from a named Harvard psychiatrist, citing one named researcher
(Julianne Holt-Lunstad) for the smoking/obesity health-risk comparison and referencing "many
research groups" studying the topic without naming further specific studies — the general loneliness
statistics (30-60% of people reporting frequent loneliness, depending on the study) are given as a
range rather than a single confident figure, preserved here as stated.
