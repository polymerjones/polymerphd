# The story you told about your trauma is making it worse | Rachel Yehuda

## Central claim

**PTSD is sustained less by the traumatic event itself than by the self-blaming narrative a
survivor constructs about their own actions during it, and MDMA-assisted psychotherapy is argued to
work by letting a person access and emotionally — not just intellectually — revise that narrative,
through a structured, multi-month therapeutic process rather than a passive drug experience.**
`[01:07]` "The reason that it's so hard for people in the aftermath of a trauma is not only because
something terrible has just happened but because they construct a narrative about why it happened
and what they did wrong as a result of it happening... this is really what's sustaining the
post-traumatic stress disorder."

## Why the self-blaming narrative persists, and why CBT alone often can't reach it

`[02:10]`–`[03:12]` Yehuda gives concrete examples of self-blame: a woman assaulted wondering if
she "should have fought harder," or 9/11 survivors "wondering whether trying to run and save
themselves was the best course of action" rather than helping others. She notes cognitive
behavioral therapy's underlying idea — correcting these "altered cognitive schemas" — "has always
been really correct," but that attempting it in an ordinary state of consciousness is "too much for
people": **"people engage in cognitive behavioral therapy and they talk about a trauma but it isn't
the trauma because they just can't bear to go into something so deeply distressing."**

## How MDMA differs from other psychedelics for this purpose

`[04:14]`–`[05:17]` A specific mechanistic distinction is drawn: psilocybin, LSD, and ayahuasca can
produce "ego dissolution" and states where "it's hard to be coherent in a sentence," pushing the
therapeutic work into after-the-fact integration. MDMA is described as different because a person
"can be coherent" during the session itself, allowing active psychotherapeutic work — revising the
narrative — to happen while the emotional charge of the memory is reduced, rather than only
afterward.

## Feeling versus knowing: why intellectual correction isn't enough

`[06:19]`–`[07:20]` The stated goal is not simply telling someone intellectually that they weren't
at fault, but helping them **feel** it: "instead of just understanding this intellectually, which
is what happens in cognitive behavioral therapy, she feels it as a truth... infused with compassion
for herself." A combat veteran example is given in parallel: the "monster... inside of him" reframed
as the same aggression that "saved him" and enabled him to protect others.

## Societal messaging that worsens trauma outcomes

`[09:24]`–`[12:28]` Yehuda names two specific historical examples of survivor-blaming messaging:
Holocaust survivors asked "why they went like lambs to the slaughter," and Vietnam veterans called
"baby killers" on return — both cited as clear illustrations of "how to not help somebody get over"
trauma. Her counter-framing: **"the biological reality of the fight-or-flight response is really to
try to preserve one's life. You're not really stepping through this cognitive process at the time
of a trauma. You're just trying to survive."**

## Treatment structure and honest limits

`[14:31]`–`[18:33]` Yehuda stresses MDMA-assisted psychotherapy is not a passive cure — **"it's not
only intention, it's the desire to go deeper... the medicine really um won't have healing
properties if you're afraid to have the experience or if you're reluctant about it."** The reported
efficacy figure — roughly two-thirds of treated PTSD patients no longer meeting PTSD criteria — is
paired with an explicit acknowledgment of the remaining third for whom it doesn't work, and the
actual protocol is described as substantial: three 90-minute medicine sessions within a roughly
3-month program including 12 psychotherapy sessions, not a single-dose fix.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Persistent self-blame or second-guessing one's own actions during a past traumatic event** —
  named directly as the mechanism sustaining PTSD, distinct from the event itself. `[01:07]`
- **Distancing from loved ones after trauma, out of fear of hurting them or being seen differently**
  — named as a common experience for trauma survivors with PTSD. `[03:12]`
- **Finding conventional trauma-focused therapy too emotionally distressing to complete** —
  addressed directly as a known limitation of cognitive behavioral therapy for many patients.
  `[02:10]`
- **Believing one "should have" acted differently during a life-threatening situation** — reframed
  via the fight-or-flight response as involuntary survival behavior, not a failure of judgment.
  `[10:26]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **MDMA-assisted psychotherapy** — a structured, multi-session therapeutic protocol combining MDMA
  administration with active psychotherapy during the drug state, distinguished here from
  unstructured recreational or purely experiential psychedelic use. `[01:07]`

## Analogies worth reusing

A psychedelic likened (quoting researcher Stan Grof) to "the telescope is to astronomy or the
microscope is to biology" — a tool for seeing what's otherwise inaccessible, extended with the image
of someone looking through a telescope while covering the lens out of fear of what they might see,
describing reluctance that blocks the therapy from working. `[13:29]`–`[14:31]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as direct expertise from a named PTSD/trauma researcher, with a specific, sourced
efficacy figure (roughly two-thirds remission in phase two/three trials) attributed to clinical
trial data without naming the specific study, and an explicit, stated caveat about the remaining
third of patients for whom the treatment doesn't work — a transparent hedge rather than an
oversold claim.
