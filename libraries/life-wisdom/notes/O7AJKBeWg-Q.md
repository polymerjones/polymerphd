# This social signal kills charisma faster than anything else | Vanessa Van Edwards

## Central claim

**A first impression is decided within seconds of being seen, before any words are exchanged,
based on how clearly a person signals warmth (trustworthiness) and competence (reliability) — and
of all the specific signals covered, "blocking" (crossed arms, or holding something in front of the
torso) is named as the single cue that kills charisma, warmth, and competence signaling fastest.**
`[00:00]` "Those first few seconds, you're trying to establish two things: presence and trust...
your first impression happens the moment someone first sees you." `[13:14]` "Let me teach you the
cue that kills any momentum, any charisma, any warmth, any competence. It's blocking."

## Warmth and competence: the two axes of presence

`[01:01]` Drawing on research attributed to Dr. Susan Fiske, presence is framed as answering two
questions as fast as possible: **"can I trust you?"** (warmth) and **"can I rely on you?"**
(competence). A specific caveat is stressed: **"this is not your actual warmth and competence...
you can be the smartest, most trustworthy person in the room, but if you're not clearly signaling
that warmth and competence, people will not believe it."**

## The cue cycle: decode, internalize, encode

`[04:02]` A described lab study: actors sent participants a deliberate cue of social rejection (an
eye roll, a scoff); participants' pupils dilated and their field of vision widened — described as
the body "internalizing" the negative cue ("uh-oh, I'm not safe") before "encoding" nervousness and
anxiety back into the interaction. This three-step cycle (decode → internalize → encode) is offered
as the underlying mechanism for why a single negative or positive cue can cascade through an
entire interaction.

## Naming an emotion to stop "catching" it

`[07:06]` A second cited study, attributed to Dr. Matthew Lieberman at UCLA: participants shown
fearful or angry faces in an fMRI scanner showed activation in their own amygdala — emotional
contagion, observed directly. The described finding that follows: **"when he taught them to say or
label anger, fear, it disengaged the amygdala. It made it so that those participants no longer felt
angry and afraid."** The practical instruction drawn from this: silently naming an emotion you
perceive in someone else is presented as a way to avoid being emotionally "hijacked" by it.

## Starter cues, in sequence

`[08:09]`–`[13:14]` A specific bundle of first-impression techniques, in the order they're
introduced: the **happy hello** (greeting on the out-breath, avoiding the pitch-raising effect of
holding one's breath), the **palm flash** (an open-palm gesture on first being seen), visible
**hands** throughout (avoiding pockets or holding objects), **gaze** (60-70% eye contact in Western
settings, not 100%), and **fronting** (angling the body toward whoever is speaking). Touch (a
handshake, hug, or high five) is named as a further warmth cue, tied to oxytocin release, with more
skin contact producing more oxytocin — but explicitly framed as optional and culture-dependent.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Feeling nervous or anxious right before an interaction (a call, meeting, or in-person
  greeting)** — addressed with the "happy hello" technique specifically to avoid signaling that
  anxiety vocally. `[10:11]`
- **Crossing your arms or holding something in front of your torso during a conversation** — named
  directly as "blocking," the single most charisma-damaging cue described, and stated to also make
  "your own thinking" measurably more closed-minded, not just how you're perceived. `[13:14]`
- **Catching someone else's anger or fear and feeling destabilized by it** — reframed as emotional
  contagion via amygdala activation, with silent labeling of the emotion offered as the specific
  countermeasure. `[07:06]`
- **Feeling inauthentic or "fake" when trying to project confidence** — addressed directly: the
  goal is stated as aligning "inner intention with outer expression," not performing false
  confidence, and admitting nervousness or vulnerability aloud is explicitly framed as itself a
  warmth cue. `[14:15]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Presence** — the combination of perceived warmth and perceived competence signaled in a first
  impression, per Dr. Susan Fiske's framework as described here. `[01:01]`
- **Cue cycle** — the three-step decode/internalize/encode process by which a social signal
  produces a reciprocal signal in response. `[04:02]`
- **Punctuator** — a habitual, largely unconscious nonverbal tic (e.g. a surprise micro-expression)
  a person uses regardless of the emotion they're actually feeling. `[08:09]`
- **Palm flash / happy hello / fronting / blocking** — the specific named cues detailed above.
  `[08:09]`–`[13:14]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- Blocking (crossed arms) compared to closing off not just to others but to your own thinking — a
  dual-directional cost for a single gesture. `[13:14]`
- The palm flash traced to "our caveman days," when an open hand signaled nothing was being hidden
  — used as the evolutionary rationale for why the gesture still reads as trustworthy. `[11:13]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented by a named behavioral researcher and author, citing two specific external
studies/researchers by name (Dr. Susan Fiske on warmth/competence; Dr. Matthew Lieberman's fMRI
study on emotional labeling) — treated as more externally grounded than a pure personal-anecdote
video. One claim is explicitly self-debunked: the popular "93% of communication is nonverbal"
statistic is directly named and corrected as "overgeneralized and unable to be repeated," with the
original researcher (Mehrabian) cited as having cautioned against its overuse himself — a rare
instance of a source correcting a widely-circulated claim rather than repeating it.
