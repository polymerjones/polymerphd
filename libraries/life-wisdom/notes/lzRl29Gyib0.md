# 62% of teenagers wish adults would do this when they're upset | Lisa Damour

## Central claim

**Damour's own survey data shows teenagers overwhelmingly want to be listened to when upset
(62%) and given space (56%) — far more than they want advice (28%) — and the recurring adult
mistake is leaping in with fixes or suggestions when what's actually being asked for is empathy
and room to self-regulate.** `[16:22]` "Because we think just listening isn't enough, we often leap
in with advice... invariably the kid's like, 'You don't get it. You don't understand.'"
`[16:22]`–`[17:24]` "62% of them said, 'I just want you to listen.' After that, 56%, 'I just want
some space,' and only 28% said they wanted advice."

## Teenagers are already good at self-regulating emotion

`[02:02]`–`[04:04]` Damour describes teenagers' own coping strategies as more sophisticated than
adults typically credit: curated, mood-matching music playlists (a "sad and peaceful playlist," a
"grrr playlist"), which research and teens' own reports say work by letting them "feel the emotion
more intensely and then move through it more quickly" rather than dwelling. Boys' video-game play
with friends is reframed as genuinely social rather than purely avoidant, and even idiosyncratic
private rituals (one teen tearing up and re-taping a piece of paper) are validated as legitimate,
harmless coping — "does no harm, brings relief. It's your thing. It works for you."

## Two realities at once: real distress and real thriving

`[05:05]`–`[07:09]` Damour insists on holding two facts together rather than picking one narrative:
today's teens report elevated anxiety and depression, but are also — by her account — "the best
behaved generation of teenagers on record" (less drug use, more seatbelt/helmet use, delayed sexual
activity). Her own Gallup data: 45% reported feeling "a lot of stress" and 38% "really anxious" the
previous day, but 94% also reported feeling happy that same day — numbers that sum past 100% because
"kids are feeling lots of things... on the very same day."

## Gender differences are real but easy to misread

`[07:09]`–`[10:12]` Girls are socialized to "collapse in on themselves" under distress (more
reported anxiety/depression); boys are socialized to "act out" (irritability, getting into
trouble) — meaning a survey that only asks about sadness will systematically underreport boys'
distress, and a survey that only asks about talking/sharing will underreport boys' loneliness, since
"companionship for a lot of boys isn't about talking... it's about doing things together." Damour
explicitly cautions that gender-group differences reflect "overlapping bell curves," not
categorical splits.

## Phones are one slice of a larger pie

`[10:12]`–`[13:16]` Damour resists treating phones/social media as the sole driver of teen distress:
"it can be very easy to focus on them as the main, if not sole, cause... but there's a lot of other
things in that pie too" — naming sleep deprivation, poverty and discrimination, gun-violence worry,
achievement pressure, climate anxiety, political polarization, family mental health/trauma, and peer
concerns (bullying, friendship worry) as co-equal slices.

## The developmental task: independence while still living at home

`[00:00]`, `[22:32]` The video's framing device: **"they have to become independent while living
with us... they do this by accomplishing a psychological distance in advance of accomplishing a
physical distance."** This reframes teen withdrawal (wanting space, pulling back from parents) as a
"pretty brilliant and elegant solution" to a real developmental problem, not a rejection of the
parent. Damour explicitly asks adults not to "take any of it personally."

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **A teenager going straight to their room after a hard day rather than talking about it** —
  reframed as a request for space to self-regulate, not a sign something has gone wrong or a
  relationship rupture. `[16:22]`–`[17:24]`
- **A boy expressing distress as irritability, acting out, or getting into trouble rather than
  sadness** — reframed as culturally-permitted distress expression, not misbehavior: "he's not
  being naughty... he's a kid in distress." `[08:10]`–`[09:11]`
- **A parent feeling rejected or "broken up with" when a teenager pulls away emotionally** —
  addressed directly as a misreading of a normal, necessary developmental process. `[22:32]`
- **Feeling that being upset about something "small" means something is wrong with you** — teens
  report worrying about this; validated directly by an adult response of "oh yeah, that stinks"
  rather than minimizing or over-analyzing. `[15:19]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **"Expressing and taming"** — Damour's two-stage model for processing difficult emotion: first
  letting the feeling out (talking, sad music), then actively countering the mood afterward (happy
  music, distraction, problem-solving). `[04:04]`, `[19:26]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Jelly beans to Brussels sprouts"** — describing the shift in how children regard parents as
  they enter adolescence: still valued, but no longer wanted in unlimited supply. `[18:24]`
- **"Psychological distance in advance of physical distance"** — the core reframe for why
  adolescent withdrawal is functional preparation for leaving home, not rejection. `[00:00]`,
  `[22:32]`
- **A pie chart of teen mental health drivers** — used to visually resist collapsing youth distress
  onto a single cause (phones). `[12:13]`

## Source-stated confidence

Damour identifies as a clinical psychologist of 30+ years with a specific research base (her own
Gallup poll of teenagers, her clinical caseload, and her book *Untangled*), and distinguishes her
own survey findings ("that's what I got from my Gallup poll") from broader claims she attributes to
"the research" or "science" without naming a specific study — the general claims (e.g., that
sad-mood music produces faster emotional processing) are stated as settled findings but not sourced
to a specific citation in the transcript.

## Conflicts with other sources

No other note in this library currently addresses parent-teenager communication specifically —
this is a first entry on that topic. Thematically, this video's "listen before you try to fix"
finding converges with the Gottmans' `mlpV7mzGfIE` ("Strong partners do this when they hear 'We
need to talk'"), which makes a structurally identical claim about adult romantic partners: that
deep listening, not immediate advice or problem-solving, is what actually repairs distress in a
relationship — the same underlying communication principle applied across two very different
relationship types (parent-teen vs. romantic partners).
