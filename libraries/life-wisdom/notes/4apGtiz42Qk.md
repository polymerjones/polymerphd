# Why relationships in 2026 carry impossible expectations | Alain de Botton

## Central claim

**Romantic culture — which holds that partners should be chosen by instinct and feeling rather than
reason, and that a good partner accepts you as you are rather than trying to help you grow — has
been in effect for roughly two centuries and has not made people appreciably happier than the
pragmatic "dynastic" partner-selection it replaced, because it actively discourages the planning
and reflection that good relationships actually require.** `[00:00]` "We are not any appreciably
happier now in a romantic culture than we were in a dynastic culture... because we live in a
romantic culture that sees something adverse in planning and thinking and reflecting too hard on
the business of love."

## From dynastic to romantic partnership

`[02:02]`–`[04:03]` De Botton traces a historical arc: for most of history, "couples were formed not
by the individuals themselves, but by the wider society" for pragmatic reasons ("you were the Duke
of Brabant and they were the Princess of Naples"). **Romanticism**, emerging toward the end of the
18th century in Britain, France, Germany, and parts of Italy, introduced the now-dominant idea that
"each individual should be left to decide on their partner by their own... movements of their own
heart" — which he calls "a beautiful idea" and, in the same breath, "a disaster," because it
elevated instinct over the deliberate relational work that actually sustains a partnership.

## Challenging what counts as "unromantic"

`[05:05]`–`[06:05]` De Botton directly attacks the romantic taboo against discussing practical or
psychological topics: **"is it truly unromantic if we define romanticism as the pursuit of a
workable relationship? On that basis, is it unromantic to talk about money? Is it unromantic to
think long and hard about childhood patterns of psychological development?"** He extends this to
the taboo against a partner wanting change: **"couples in romantic culture will sometimes complain,
'you're trying to change me'... this is seen as really offensive"** — contrasted directly with an
imagined ancient Greek answer that **"love is a process of education. It's the education of
emotion... lovers should of course be able to pick each other up for things that they don't spot in
one another."**

## The education gap

`[08:05]` A specific rhetorical figure: **"a person's life satisfaction is determined by up to
around 70% by the quality of their personal relationships"** — followed by the observation that,
despite this, "none of us are made to sit through classes in attachment theory, classes in apology,
classes in listening, classes in communication," while subjects like physics and geography receive
structured instruction. The stated cause is the same cultural aversion to "planning and thinking...
too hard on the business of love."

## Art and media as the primary, misleading teacher of love

`[09:05]`–`[10:06]` De Botton names film, music, and (to the extent it's still read) poetry as **"the
single greatest most misleading disseminator of ideas on love"** — arguing most films are "blithe,"
"superficial," and "not modest enough in showing us what's really going on between couples." He
names one specific exception, Richard Linklater's *Before* trilogy (ending in *Before Midnight*),
as "one of the most accurate representations of love" he's aware of.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Labeling a practical conversation (finances, family compatibility, psychological history) as
  "unromantic" and avoiding it** — challenged directly as a cultural prejudice rather than a
  genuine signal that something is wrong with the relationship. `[05:05]`
- **Feeling offended or threatened when a partner wants you to grow or change** — reframed via the
  ancient Greek model as a normal, even loving, part of a functioning relationship rather than a
  red flag. `[07:05]`
- **Believing relationship struggles are unusual or a sign of personal failure, when little formal
  education on relationship skill exists for anyone** — offered as context for why so many people
  feel lost in this area specifically. `[08:05]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Romanticism** — the historical shift, beginning in the late 18th century, toward choosing
  partners by individual feeling/instinct rather than family or social arrangement. `[03:02]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- A hypothetical Martian visitor, hearing that 70% of life satisfaction depends on relationship
  quality, expecting to find humans devoting 70% of their money and education to studying it — used
  to highlight the mismatch between relationships' stated importance and actual societal
  investment in relational skill. `[08:05]`
