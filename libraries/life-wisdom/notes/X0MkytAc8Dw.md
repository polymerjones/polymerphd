# These children are labeled as defiant. Why that's wrong | Becky Kennedy

## Central claim

**A subset of children Kennedy calls "deeply feeling kids" react to vulnerability with
simultaneous shame, making their emotional outbursts more intense and their shutdowns more
resistant to direct comfort than other children's — and standard advice (harsher consistency, a
clinical "oppositional defiant" label) worsens this because it confirms the child's core fear of
being "too much" rather than addressing the shame underneath.** `[00:00]` "There's this group of
kids... who really are kind of operating differently in the world. When they feel vulnerable, they
simultaneously feel shame. And shame makes feelings explosive."

## How Kennedy identified the pattern

`[00:00]`–`[02:01]` Kennedy describes a recurring pattern in her clinical practice: parents
reporting tantrums "animalistic, hissing, growling, scratching," longer recovery times, and
"strategies... making things worse" despite following her usual advice. Her private, somewhat
uncomfortable first reaction was that these parents "just [weren't] doing it right" — until having
her own second child produced the identical dynamic: standard validation ("Oh, you're so sad")
provoked "STOP TALKING TO ME!" and disproportionate meltdowns.

## The "porous" model and why vulnerability triggers shame

`[02:01]`–`[04:02]` Kennedy's working model: deeply feeling kids are **"more porous to the
world"** — more comes in (faster overwhelm) and more comes out (more intense expression) than in
non-DFK children. The key distinguishing mechanism, stated directly: **"unlike non-DFKs, when
non-deeply feeling kids are vulnerable, they trip and fall... they really take in comfort. Deeply
feeling kids, when they feel vulnerable, they simultaneously feel shame."** The stated internal
logic of a DFK: "I'm a really intensely feeling person... but the things that overwhelm me, I'm
worried are going to overwhelm [the adults I need], so I can't really ask them for help" — producing
what Kennedy calls "such a conflictual existence" even in children as young as three.

## Why direct comfort backfires, and the "side door" alternative

`[05:03]`–`[08:05]` Kennedy states that labeling these children "oppositional defiant" and
responding with more consistent discipline "only makes them even more vulnerable to all the bad
behavior we're looking to shift away from," since it confirms the DFK's fear of being "too much,
too bad for others." Her alternative — the **"side door"** — approaches a shut-down child
indirectly: state something you expect the child to reject first (to let them push back
safely), then follow with something closer to the truth for a partial acceptance ("a thumb to the
side"), rather than leading with direct validation, which a DFK experiences as "too close" and
meets with rejection ("slam the door in your face").

## Reconnecting after an outburst

`[08:05]`–`[10:07]` Kennedy's stated approach for after a meltdown: don't rush back in — **"we
might need to regulate ourself first"** — then reconnect by affirming unconditional love and shared
imperfection: **"we all have moments when we do things we're not proud of. I love you. Nothing you
could ever do would change that I love you."** She states this reconnection, done well, changes the
overall dynamic and lets a DFK's positive traits — described as "intense joy," humor, leadership,
self-assurance — become more visible once the shame cycle is interrupted.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **A child's tantrum that seems disproportionate to its trigger, with an unusually slow return to
  calm** — named as a possible sign of a "deeply feeling kid" pattern rather than deliberate
  defiance. `[00:00]`
- **A child who rejects direct comfort or validation, telling a parent to stop talking about
  feelings** — reframed as shame-driven avoidance rather than a rejection of support itself.
  `[02:01]`
- **A child insisting a parent caused something the parent didn't do** (e.g. blaming a parent for
  their own trip and fall) — offered as a recognizable DFK behavior pattern. `[01:00]`
- **Feeling "un-understandable"** — named as one of the worst feelings for any child, and
  specifically harder to resolve for DFKs because they reject the usual, direct paths to feeling
  understood. `[08:05]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Deeply feeling kids (DFKs)** — Kennedy's own term for children who feel shame alongside
  vulnerability and are "more porous" to their environment, producing more intense emotional
  reactions. `[00:00]`
- **Side door (strategy)** — an indirect approach to a shut-down or shame-triggered child, starting
  with a statement expected to be rejected before offering a closer approximation of the truth.
  `[06:04]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- Skin pores, scaled up, as the model for how a DFK takes in and expresses more emotional intensity
  than a non-DFK child. `[03:01]`
- A house's front door versus a side door — direct validation ("front door") gets slammed on a DFK,
  while an indirect approach ("side door") gets around their shame defenses. `[06:04]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as first-person clinical observation and pattern-recognition from a named psychologist,
explicitly framed as her own developing model ("I call them deeply feeling kids... I didn't quite
understand exactly why, but started to catch on") rather than a peer-reviewed diagnostic category —
she notes DFKs are "too often... labeled as oppositional defiant... even in the clinical world,"
implying her own framing is offered as an alternative to, not a confirmation of, that existing
label.
