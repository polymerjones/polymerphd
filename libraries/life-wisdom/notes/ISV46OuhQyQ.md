# Why shame works short-term and backfires for life | Becky Kennedy

## Central claim

**Punishment and reward only target a child's observable behavior, not the underlying skill they
lack, so Kennedy's "Good Inside" approach instead separates a child's identity from their
behavior — treating misbehavior as "a good kid having a hard time" — while drawing on attachment
theory and internal family systems to explain why unaddressed childhood emotional patterns
resurface, largely unchanged, in adult relationships.** `[13:14]` "Punishment and rewards are
purely behavior focused. They do not teach skills. Just like we don't punish and reward kids into
learning how to swim, we teach them how to swim. We teach them skills."

## Why Kennedy abandoned punishment-based parenting

`[00:00]`–`[04:05]` Kennedy states she was trained to teach parents timeouts and sticker charts,
but the approach became untenable once she compared it to her adult therapy practice: **"When
adults would come to me for therapy and say things like, 'I know we're working on my anger, but I
yelled this week'... I would never say to those people, give me your phone, no dessert for a
week."** The dissonance culminated in her telling parents mid-session, "I'm sorry, I actually don't
believe anything I'm telling you right now" `[03:04]`, and refunding the session.

## Separating behavior from identity

`[06:07]`–`[09:09]` Kennedy's stated foundation: **"we are born good inside... [this] creates a gap
between good identity and bad behaviors, and it allows us to wonder, well, why would a good person
do a bad thing?"** She's explicit this isn't permissiveness: **"I've never seen my kid hit another
kid and say, let out that anger, you're a good kid letting out anger, I love it. That would be so
bizarre."** Instead, the phrase "you're a good kid having a hard time" is presented as a de-
escalation tool parents report working "in a way nothing else they've ever tried has" `[09:09]`.

## Worked example: a lying/stealing teenager

`[10:11]`–`[11:11]` Responding to a parent whose teenager denied stealing money caught on camera,
Kennedy's stated principle is **"never ask someone a question you know the answer to — you're
actually just trying to catch them in a lie, which makes anyone defensive."** Her alternative
script leads with the observed fact, states the relationship isn't at risk, and asks what was
actually happening — reasoning that **"understanding what was happening is so much more important
than the behavior itself, because that's the only way we can get ahead of everything that's going
to happen after."**

## The three-pilot metaphor for "sturdy leadership"

`[17:22]`–`[21:28]` Kennedy defines her preferred leadership stance, "sturdy," through three
contrasting pilot announcements during turbulence: (1) a pilot who blames passengers for panicking
("you're making a big deal out of nothing") — the punishment-style response; (2) a pilot who
panics along with them and asks for help — the permissive/over-identified response, where "the
pilot loses themself"; (3) a pilot who validates the fear while staying calm and in control — **"I
can empathize with your feeling precisely because I'm not taking on the feeling myself... that's
exactly the type of leadership our kids are looking for from us."**

## Attachment theory and "factory settings"

`[21:28]`–`[26:36]` Attachment theory is credited to John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, with its core
claim stated as: **"the nature of the relationship we form with our kid in their earliest years is
something that impacts them for the rest of their life."** Kennedy uses a friend's phrase, "factory
settings," for the resulting relational blueprint, and argues the body generalizes early safety
lessons the same way it generalizes "look both ways before crossing the street" to any street,
anywhere — illustrated with a hypothetical adult who learned as a child that being upset was met
with dismissal, and later struggles to voice distress to a spouse.

## Internal family systems and "protector parts"

`[28:40]`–`[33:44]` Kennedy introduces internal family systems as the idea that suppressed
childhood emotions (her example: anger that was met with punishment) generate an adaptive
"protector part" that shuts the emotion down to preserve safety — a part that "doesn't know it's
[the current year]... it still thinks it's, I don't know, 1980." The stated intervention is not to
eliminate the part but to **"thank that part... for all the years you kept me safe,"** then
practice expressing the suppressed emotion in a way appropriate to the present relationship.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Treating a single instance of bad behavior as proof of a fixed bad identity** ("that person is
  lazy," "they're a hitter") — named as a common but flawed inference, since "behavior is
  observable and identity isn't." `[07:07]`
- **A parent feeling like their child's distress is "contagious"** and needing it to stop for their
  own sake — named as a sign of the second, over-identified "pilot" failure mode. `[19:24]`
- **A recurring adult pattern, such as pulling away or snapping when upset,** described as a likely
  reactivation of an early relational lesson rather than a present-day overreaction. `[33:44]`
- **Persistent people-pleasing/compliance in an adult** — named directly as a red flag, not a
  success: "I've never met one parent who says... she's so compliant... and doesn't really ever
  take care of her own needs." `[14:17]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **"Good inside"** — Kennedy's foundational premise that children (and adults) are inherently
  good, used to separate identity from behavior. `[05:06]`
- **Sturdy leadership** — the capacity to hold one's own authority and values while still
  empathizing with someone else's distress, illustrated by the three-pilot metaphor. `[17:22]`
- **Factory settings** — a friend's term (credited, not coined by Kennedy) for the relational
  blueprint a child's earliest attachment relationship establishes. `[23:32]`
- **Protector part** — an internal family systems term for an emotional-suppression pattern that
  developed adaptively in childhood and can persist maladaptively into adulthood. `[29:40]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- The three airline-pilot announcements during turbulence — punishing, panicking, and sturdy — as
  a compact model for three parental (and general leadership) responses to someone else's distress.
  `[17:22]`–`[21:28]`
- Looking both ways before crossing the street as an analogy for how the body generalizes an early
  safety lesson to new, unrelated situations throughout life. `[25:35]`
- Teaching a child to swim as the analogy for why punishment/reward cannot substitute for actually
  teaching an emotional-regulation skill. `[13:14]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as first-person professional synthesis from a named clinical psychologist, drawing
explicitly on two credited frameworks (attachment theory via Bowlby/Ainsworth; internal family
systems) rather than presenting her own "Good Inside" method as independently validated research.
The claim that "a lot of studies... show short-term impact [of punishment] but don't tell the whole
story of child development" is asserted without naming a specific study — preserved here as stated,
not independently verified.
