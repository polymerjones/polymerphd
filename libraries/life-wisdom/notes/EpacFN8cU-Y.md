# 1 in 5 people are unbearably lonely. A psychologist explains | Paul Bloom

## Central claim

**Loneliness is not simply being alone but a felt lack of human connectedness that functions as an
essential evolved motivator (like hunger or thirst) — and while AI companions offer real, measurable
short-term comfort, Bloom argues they carry two serious risks (addictive substitution for human
contact, and the loss of corrective social friction) that make them, at best, a stopgap for the
otherwise-unreachable rather than a general solution.** `[00:00]` "What loneliness is at its root is
a lack of human connectedness, the feeling that you aren't loved, you aren't needed, you aren't
respected... you could be alone for a long period of time, but not feel lonely if you know there's
people who love you and understand you."

## Solitude epidemic, not loneliness epidemic

`[01:00]` Bloom disputes the popular "loneliness epidemic" framing, citing a colleague's (Garrett
Thomson's) argument that available data doesn't show people are lonelier now than 20-50 years ago,
and that loneliness peaked during COVID rather than continuing to rise. What has genuinely
increased, he argues, is **solitude** — time spent alone — driven by better home entertainment,
food delivery, and streaming making solitary life more comfortable than it used to be. Loneliness
itself he estimates at roughly **one in five people**, worst in adolescence and old age.

## AI companions: the positive finding, and the backlash

`[03:01]`–`[04:03]` Bloom describes co-authoring a paper ("In Praise of Empathic AI," with colleague
Mickey Inzlicht) finding that in short-term, blinded comparisons, people rated conversations with
ChatGPT as more empathetic and validating than conversations with other people — a finding he says
provoked strong public criticism, which he treats as fair grounds to examine the negative case too.

## Two named risks: addiction and lost social friction

`[05:03]`–`[08:06]` **Addiction**: Bloom worries AI companions, being endlessly available,
non-judgmental, and improving rapidly, will be preferred over human relationships, especially by
adolescents who should be "out there... developing romantic relationships, developing attachments."
**Lost social friction**: he argues social learning (a toddler learning to walk by falling, a
comedian learning timing by bombing) depends on negative feedback AI structurally cannot give,
since it is "famously sycophantic" — never bored, never critical, never in need of an apology. He
extends this with an analogy to billionaires and royalty surrounded by yes-men, citing the book
*Careless People* on the psychological damage of never being told "no."

## Loneliness as a functional drive, not a glitch

`[09:07]`–`[10:08]` Citing the late neuroscientist John Cacioppo, loneliness is framed as serving
the same evolutionary function as hunger or thirst: **"an animal like us who had no loneliness...
wouldn't reproduce, wouldn't survive."** The implication drawn is that fully satisfying the
loneliness-drive artificially (via an always-available AI) risks removing the motivation that
otherwise pushes a person toward real human contact — paralleling his separate worry that AI
companionship could also encourage or fail to correct dangerous delusions, citing a reported case
where an AI's validation may have contributed to a user's suicide.

## Where Bloom lands: a conditional case for AI companionship

`[19:15]`–`[23:18]` Bloom is explicit his concerns don't apply equally to everyone. For the very old
(especially institutionalized, or affected by dementia, with no realistic path to more human
contact) and for acute grief (he discloses his own mother's death when he was 12 and the prolonged
loneliness that followed), he argues withholding AI companionship on principle would be **"a form of
cruelty."** His closing analogy, credited to Molly Crockett, is **"emotional Soylent"**: nutritious
and sustaining, a reasonable backup when the real thing isn't available, but "a kind of really lame
life" if it fully replaces it.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Feeling unloved, unneeded, or disconnected even while surrounded by people** — named directly
  as loneliness's actual core, distinguished from simply being physically alone. `[00:00]`
- **Preferring conversation with an AI over a person because it is more consistently validating and
  available** — named as a real, measured short-term effect, but flagged as a risk if it displaces
  human relationships long-term. `[03:01]`
- **Feeling unusually "right" or affirmed after discussing a personal dispute with a chatbot** —
  given as a concrete example (a colleague told by ChatGPT she was "100% right") of AI's sycophancy
  reinforcing rather than correcting a person's take. `[15:11]`
- **Profound, prolonged loneliness following bereavement or in old age** — named as the specific
  circumstance where Bloom's usual cautions about AI companionship give way to a case for its use.
  `[21:17]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Solitude epidemic** — Bloom's (via Garrett Thomson) reframing of the popular "loneliness
  epidemic": increased time alone, not necessarily increased loneliness. `[01:00]`
- **Emotional Soylent** — Molly Crockett's term (via Bloom) for AI companionship: nourishing and
  filling as a backup, but lacking the richness of real human connection. `[22:18]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- Hannah Arendt's observation that the most painful loneliness is felt while surrounded by people,
  not alone — used to sharpen the definition of loneliness as connection, not proximity. `[01:00]`
- Billionaires/royalty surrounded by yes-men, extended to anyone with a sycophantic AI companion —
  used to explain the corrosive effect of never receiving honest social feedback. `[08:06]`
- Oliver Burkeman's phone-call-that-was-secretly-AI thought experiment — used to argue that identical
  words from a non-conscious source are not the same experience as the same words from a real
  person. `[16:13]`
- "Emotional Soylent" — filling and sustaining, but not the real thing. `[22:18]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented by a named professor of psychology describing his own co-authored, published research
("In Praise of Empathic AI") alongside named colleagues' concepts (John Cacioppo's loneliness
research, Garrett Thomson's solitude-epidemic argument, Molly Crockett's "emotional Soylent"
framing) and a personal disclosure (his mother's death) offered explicitly as anecdote, not data.
Bloom flags genuine uncertainty at several points — "I don't know how severe this problem is" (AI
-induced delusion) — rather than presenting every claim with equal confidence.
