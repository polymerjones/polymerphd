# The crucial emotional skill most adults were never taught | Becky Kennedy

## Central claim

**Emotion regulation is a learnable skill acquired primarily through "co-regulation" — repeatedly
borrowing a caregiver's calm during one's own emotional overwhelm — not an innate trait, and
Kennedy builds several concrete practices from this premise: holding "two things are true" at once,
a strict definition of a boundary as something you do (not something you ask of someone else), and
a structured way to express anger directly rather than hint at it.** `[00:00]` "Anger is a healthy
emotion. The issue is most of us weren't taught skills to effectively manage anger, and we weren't
given a long enough runway to learn how to do that."

## Dysregulation, co-regulation, regulation

`[01:00]`–`[06:09]` Kennedy's stated sequence: children are "born with all of the emotions and none
of the skills to manage those emotions," so when "emotions are greater than skills," the result is
**dysregulation** — a tantrum, an outburst. **Co-regulation** is a caregiver's calm presence
transferring to the child in that moment, repeated "over and over and over," eventually producing
internalized **(self-)regulation**. She's explicit adults never fully graduate from needing this:
**"we still need co-regulation even as adults... that's why when you've had a horrible day and you
go out with some friends... you're actually feeling better"** even though nothing about the day
itself changed.

## "Two things are true"

`[06:09]`–`[10:16]` Framed as essential for both **mental health** and **successful relationships**:
the ability to hold two seemingly opposed feelings or positions without needing to resolve them
into one. Personal example: "I love my kid in a way I've never loved anyone" and "I kind of miss my
pre-child life" — both true, without one canceling the other, avoiding a "negative downward spiral"
of self-judgment. Relational example: a parenting boundary — **"my job is to set boundaries, my
kid's job is to feel their feelings... my decision doesn't dictate my kid's feelings, and my kid's
feelings definitely don't dictate my decision."**

## A boundary, precisely defined

`[10:16]`–`[15:20]` Kennedy gives a specific two-part test: **"boundaries are what we tell someone
we will do, and they require the other person to do nothing."** Worked example: telling a child
"don't press the elevator buttons" is a *request* (it requires the child's compliance to succeed);
"I'm going to stand between you and the buttons, and even if you lunge for them, I will stop you"
is a *boundary* (it requires nothing from the child). She argues this reframing is empowering —
**"why am I giving my six-year-old my power?"** — and names the resulting stance **"authority
without aggression."**

## Naming anger directly: feeling, need, specific

`[17:24]`–`[22:28]` Kennedy states anger is essential to self-worth — **"if we want to preserve any
sense of self-esteem... we have to have access to anger... someone saying 'I'm never angry' is
really a way of saying I never know what I want or need."** Her stated technique for acting on it
constructively: name the **feeling**, state the **need**, and be **specific** — e.g. "I feel
overwhelmed around doing bath time by myself, and I really need more support. Can you let me know
two nights this week you can be home by 5:25?" — contrasted with **"hint and hope,"** which she
calls "so disempowering." The acknowledge-validate-permit (AVP) sequence is offered as the
underlying regulation skill: **"I'm not going to be able to regulate my feelings... if I'm not able
to say, I think I'm feeling anxious"** (acknowledge), followed by explaining to yourself why the
feeling makes sense without excusing any resulting behavior (validate), then explicitly allowing
yourself to feel it (permit).

## The collapsing "gap between wanting and having"

`[33:42]`–`[40:51]` Kennedy attributes declining frustration tolerance — in both children and
parents — to technology closing the historical delay between desire and satisfaction. Her own
Blockbuster-membership childhood is used as a contrast: wanting to watch a movie meant a multi-day
process (asking parents, driving to the store, sometimes finding it out of stock), versus a modern
child's near-instant on-demand access. She extends this to reading acquisition (**"nobody is just
delivering to you right away the ability to read"** — a rare early experience of effortful, delayed
reward) and to parents themselves, admitting **"I'm on my phone a lot... so much less frustration,
so much convenience"** — driving a mutually reinforcing cycle where less-tolerant parents give in
faster to less-tolerant children, "off in a bad direction."

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **A tantrum or emotional outburst disproportionate to its apparent trigger** (a grilled cheese cut
  the "wrong" way) — described as evidence that "emotions are greater than skills," not that the
  stated trigger is the real cause. `[03:04]`
- **Feeling flustered and "on the edge" of yelling at a child** — reframed from "how do I not fall
  off the cliff" to "how do I recognize I'm heading toward one and exit early." `[16:23]`–`[17:24]`
- **A partner or child's complaint that someone "doesn't respect my boundaries"** — reframed as
  usually signaling a request, not a true boundary, being mistaken for one. `[10:16]`
- **A child (or adult) who struggles with waiting or interrupting frequently** — connected directly
  to reduced real-world practice at tolerating a gap between wanting and having. `[40:51]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Dysregulation / co-regulation / regulation** — the three-stage model of emotional development:
  emotions exceeding skills, borrowing a caregiver's calm, and eventual internalized self-control.
  `[02:00]`–`[04:04]`
- **Boundary** (as distinct from a request) — "something we tell someone we will do, and they
  require the other person to do nothing." `[10:16]`
- **Authority without aggression** — Kennedy's term for retaining decision-making power without
  relying on force or punishment. `[14:19]`
- **Acknowledge, validate, permit (AVP)** — a three-step sequence for regulating one's own emotion.
  `[22:28]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- Turbulence and a pilot's calm as the model for how a caregiver's regulated presence transfers
  calm to someone dysregulated. `[04:04]`–`[05:07]`
- Driving toward a cliff, and the better question being "why am I on this road" rather than "how do
  I not fall off" — used to reframe emotional escalation as something to exit early, not manage at
  its peak. `[16:23]`
- The Blockbuster-membership movie-rental process as a concrete illustration of a "gap between
  wanting and having" that has since collapsed to near-zero. `[35:45]`
- An "annoying passenger in the back seat" for a difficult emotion (e.g. jealousy) once acknowledged
  rather than left "in the driver's seat." `[22:28]`
