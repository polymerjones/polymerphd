# The strange reason nobody views their own retaliation as evil | Paul Bloom

## Central claim

**A punitive, retributive impulse is not a flaw in human morality but one of its two evolutionary
foundations (alongside kin-based compassion) — cooperation cannot survive without some cost
imposed on those who exploit it — but that same impulse "unmoored from the purposes it serves"
produces cycles of retaliatory violence, because virtually nobody experiences their own retaliation
as anything but righteous.** `[09:12]` "Very few villains think of themselves as villains. And
retributive actions always feel great when you're doing them. Not just in a visceral way, but
great, like, morally righteous. And but it makes the world worse."

## Why morality needed a punitive half, not just a kind one

`[02:05]`–`[04:07]` Bloom names two evolutionary mechanisms behind morality: **kin selection**
(compassion for genetic relatives, explaining parent-child love) and **reciprocal altruism** ("you
scratch my back, I scratch yours"). He argues reciprocal altruism cannot survive without an
accompanying punitive impulse: if defection carried no cost, **"kindness could never evolve"** —
cooperation only holds together because there's a felt cost to breaking it.

## When the punitive impulse runs out of control

`[08:11]`–`[09:12]` Bloom names specific research patterns: people told how much to fine a company
often demand the maximum penalty even when told that penalty would cause broader harm (destroying a
company that "provides a valuable product no one else provides"), and punishment severity is often
demanded regardless of whether it changes future behavior. He connects this directly to real-world
cycles of retaliation (citing the Israel/Gaza/Hamas conflict without taking a side on it), where
**"at no point [does] one of the sides say, 'I'm going to do some evil'"** — each retaliatory act is
experienced by its author as a righteous response to a prior wrong.

## Moral typecasting and competitive victimhood

`[11:15]`–`[13:18]` Citing psychologist Kurt Gray's term **moral typecasting**, Bloom describes a
binary bias: people sort others into villains or victims, when real situations usually involve
neither purely or both partially. This produces **competitive victimhood** — groups (his examples:
opposing sides of the Middle East conflict, and competing claims of victimhood in U.S. culture-war
disputes over trans athletes or campus speech) racing to be recognized as the "real" victim, since
victim status excuses one's own conduct toward the other side. A specific research finding is cited:
being reminded of one's own victimhood makes people *more* callous and *less* generous toward
others, the opposite of the sympathy-generating effect often assumed.

## What Bloom is not arguing

`[16:22]`–`[18:24]` Bloom is explicit he isn't arguing against retribution itself: **"I am not
advocating for a world without retribution... I would keep our retributive desires, but I'd hold
them more in check."** He points to the legal system as the existing institutional solution —
removing punishment from individual hands and giving it, ideally, to a fair, dispassionate process
— using the example that a car break-in victim isn't legally permitted to personally kill the
thief, even though the impulse to do so is real and, per his framework, evolutionarily
understandable.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **Feeling that a harsh, disproportionate punishment is deserved "no matter what," even when told
  it won't change anything or will cause wider harm** — named as the punitive impulse running past
  its functional purpose. `[08:11]`
- **A conviction, during conflict, that "we" are purely the victims and the other side is purely at
  fault** — named as moral typecasting, a bias rather than an accurate read of most real conflicts.
  `[11:15]`
- **Feeling less generous or more suspicious of others after being reminded of one's own past
  victimization** — cited as a documented, counterintuitive research finding. `[13:18]`
- **The specific felt sense that one's own act of revenge is righteous, not merely justified** —
  named as a near-universal experience on the part of whoever is doing the retaliating, not a sign
  the act is actually righteous. `[09:12]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Moral typecasting** — credited to psychologist Kurt Gray: sorting people into a binary of
  villain or victim, obscuring that most real people and situations are neither or both. `[11:15]`
- **Competitive victimhood** — groups or individuals contesting for recognized victim status
  because it excuses their own conduct toward an opposing side. `[11:15]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented by a named professor of psychology (University of Toronto, emeritus at Yale) describing
both his own ongoing research (a study of revenge narratives on Reddit, done with named colleagues
Anna Gantman and Jordan Wylie) and established findings he attributes to specific named researchers
(Kurt Gray's moral typecasting). Real-world political examples (Israel/Gaza, the Luigi Mangione
case) are offered as illustrations of the framework, with Bloom explicitly flagging his own view as
one perspective among people who "disagree... motivated by a moral sense that's just as intense" as
his own.
