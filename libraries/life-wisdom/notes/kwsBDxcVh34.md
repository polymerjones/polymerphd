# "Reactance theory" explains your most destructive life decisions | Paul Bloom

## Central claim

**Despite the video's title, Bloom's actual explanation for deliberate rule-breaking centers on a
desire for autonomy — "reactance" (doing something specifically because told not to) is presented
as one specific expression of that broader drive, not the whole explanation.** `[12:10]` "The most
interesting motivation for perversity is a desire for autonomy. A desire to be an agent in the
world, to exert one's will. If I just do the rational thing, if I just do the moral thing, what
good am I? Why am I needed? I could be replaced by an algorithm." `[14:13]` Reactance theory is
introduced specifically as one downstream consequence: **"sometimes people will do things just
because they are told not to do those things... I think this expresses the urge to be autonomous."**

## What counts as "perverse," and what doesn't

`[00:00]`–`[03:02]` Bloom defines perversity narrowly: doing the wrong thing — wrong morally or
rationally — specifically *because* it's wrong, not by mistake and not for a practical payoff. His
central historical example is Saint Augustine's *Confessions*, in which Augustine steals pears he
didn't want or need, purely "because I wanted to be bad" — puzzling precisely because, unlike his
other transgressions, it satisfied no identifiable desire. Bloom distinguishes true perversity from
a near-neighbor: perverse-*seeming* acts caused by intrusive thoughts or ironic mental "glitches" —
citing research that people warned not to reveal a secret, or worried about appearing prejudiced,
become *more* likely to blurt out exactly what they were suppressing.

## Two named frameworks for why perversity shows up

`[04:03]`–`[05:05]` **Hopeful monster theory**, borrowed loosely (Bloom is explicit he isn't
endorsing it as evolutionary biology) from biologist Goldschmidt's account of extreme mutations:
most of the time small, incremental changes are the right move, but "if you ain't got nothing, you
ain't got nothing to lose" — when a life situation is failing entirely, one large, uncharacteristic
jump may be worth the high risk of failure. `[11:09]` **The "need for chaos" scale**, credited to
Michael Bang Petersen and colleagues, measuring agreement with statements like "I think society
should be burnt to the ground" — found higher in the young, in men, and at either political
extreme, "not more on the left than the right... it's just political extremes."

## Reactance theory, specifically

`[14:13]`–`[15:16]` Reactance is introduced as the specific pattern where **"sometimes people will
do things just because they are told not to do those things."** Cited evidence: some people became
*less* likely to get a COVID vaccine after being told they were required to; strongly authoritarian
anti-drinking messaging is associated with *more* binge drinking in some college studies. Bloom
distinguishes this from wanting to *look* rebellious: **"it's not to impress other people... it's to
show something to ourselves. A perverse agent might break the rules in ways that are private, that
only he or she knows about."**

## When a little perversity is a strategic advantage

`[17:19]`–`[19:21]` Bloom names domains where controlled unpredictability pays off: **poker**
("the best poker players always throw some randomness into their behavior" so they aren't fully
readable), **negotiation** (an extreme threat is credible only from someone who might actually be
"a little bit nuts" — Nixon's "madman theory" of politics), and **art** (Duchamp's urinal, Banksy's
self-shredding painting), where audacious rule-breaking is itself the valued product.

## Signals and internal states addressed

- **A sudden, motiveless urge to do something one knows is wrong, disproportionate to any benefit
  gained** — named as the core signature of genuine perversity, illustrated by Augustine's pear
  theft and a modern example of sticking a finger in a friend's ice cream "just to do something
  messed up." `[07:05]`
- **Blurting out exactly the thing one was trying hardest not to say** — distinguished from true
  perversity as an intrusive-thought "glitch" caused by the suppression effort itself, not a
  deliberate choice. `[09:07]`
- **Doing the opposite of an instruction specifically because it was an instruction** — named
  directly as reactance, tied to a felt need for autonomy rather than genuine disagreement with the
  instruction's content. `[14:13]`
- **An urge, at toddlerhood, adolescence, or midlife specifically, to do something uncharacteristic
  or disruptive** (a tattoo, an affair, quitting a job) — named as autonomy-assertion clustering at
  life transitions where one's place or identity feels uncertain. `[13:12]`

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Hopeful monster theory** — Bloom's personal-decision adaptation of biologist Goldschmidt's
  evolutionary concept: most change should be incremental, but a large, risky jump can be
  worthwhile when the status quo is already failing entirely. `[04:03]`
- **Need for chaos scale** — a measured trait (Michael Bang Petersen et al.) capturing desire to
  destroy or upend functioning or beautiful things. `[11:09]`
- **Reactance (theory)** — doing something specifically because one was instructed not to (or vice
  versa), read as an assertion of autonomy. `[14:13]`
- **Madman theory** — the negotiating advantage of appearing willing to make irrationally extreme
  threats, attributed to Richard Nixon. `[19:21]`

## Analogies worth reusing

- "Life is poker at large" — the value of controlled unpredictability, applied beyond the game
  itself to negotiation and everyday strategic behavior. `[18:20]`
- Saint Augustine's stolen, unwanted pears — the clearest illustration of perversity with zero
  practical motive, used repeatedly as the touchstone example. `[06:05]`

## Source-stated confidence

Presented by a named professor of psychology describing his own original research (the crowd-sourced
"perversity project," collecting real personal anecdotes) alongside cited work from named
researchers (Goldschmidt, Michael Bang Petersen). Bloom is explicit about the limits of his
analogies — "I'm not arguing that this is true for evolution... I do think it's true for everyday
life" for hopeful monster theory — modeling exactly the kind of hedge this project's provenance
rules call for preserving.
