# 05 — Recurrence and Outcome Measurement

Recurrence sounds like a simple yes/no question. Across this collection, it turns out to be one of
the field's hardest measurement problems — and a major reason treatment comparisons in
`04_TREATMENT_MODALITIES.md` are so difficult to make cleanly.

---

## The same dataset can be made to show almost any recurrence rate, depending on definition

A literature review found more than forty different published definitions of "recurrence." Applying
different definitions to a single dataset produced reported recurrence rates ranging from 2% to
86%. This finding directly motivated a formal international Delphi consensus process aimed at
producing one standardized definition — a process that deliberately excluded nodules and cords from
the definition and debated whether a six-week or perioperative baseline should be used for
comparison. The scale of this discrepancy is worth holding onto whenever a specific recurrence
percentage is quoted anywhere else in this collection: the number is only meaningful alongside the
definition that produced it.

## Recontracture and true recurrence are conflated in much of the literature

As covered in `01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md`, "recontracture" (mechanical loss of correction) and "true
recurrence" (new disease activity) are mechanistically different phenomena that many studies do not
distinguish. Multiple speakers argue this conflation is itself a major contributor to the
definitional chaos described above — a study measuring only finger angle at follow-up cannot tell
which of the two it is actually detecting.

## Straighter is not automatically better

More than one source argues directly against treating "degrees of correction" as a complete outcome
measure. A literature review of recurrence rates across surgery, needle, and enzyme treatment argues
that a straighter finger is not automatically a better outcome, framing "straight" and "functional"
as competing rather than identical definitions of surgical success. This connects directly to
`06_PATIENT_EXPERIENCE_AND_PROMS.md`: patients often prioritize a hand that works well over one that
is maximally straight, and recurrence framed purely mechanically can miss what patients actually
care about.

## Recurrence reflects persistent disease, not treatment failure

Across sources discussing repeat treatment, the consistent framing is that recurrence after any
treatment (surgical, needle, or enzymatic) reflects the underlying disease continuing to be active —
not a mistake made during the original treatment. This framing supports repeat treatment (including
repeat collagenase after a prior collagenase treatment) being offered without it being treated as a
correction of an earlier error.

## Skin grafting is the one intervention in this collection that appears to change true recurrence specifically

As detailed in `01_CORE_PRINCIPLES.md` and `04_TREATMENT_MODALITIES.md`, multiple independent
surgical series report near-zero true recurrence specifically at sites covered with a skin graft,
distinguishing it from every other intervention in this collection, none of which is reported to
meaningfully reduce true recurrence rather than merely delaying or reducing recontracture.

## Rigorous randomized comparison may not be achievable for some technique comparisons

At least one source argues directly that a genuinely valid randomized controlled trial comparing
certain techniques (in context: percutaneous needle fasciotomy) cannot actually be designed, given
practical and ethical constraints on blinding and technique standardization in hand surgery. This is
preserved here as a stated position, not a resolved methodological consensus — but it helps explain
why propensity-score matching (used in at least one comparison in `04_TREATMENT_MODALITIES.md`)
appears in this collection as a stated alternative when randomization is considered impractical.
