# Terry Spilken DPM, "Cryotherapy for Plantar Fibromas" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Cryosurgery for plantar fibromas is a minimally invasive, in-office alternative to traditional surgery,
which Spilken cites as failing at least 57% of the time and often leaving patients worse off — and cryo's
own track record, by his account, runs 80–92% success with a single treatment.** His framing of the
traditional-surgery comparison: **"not only do they fail, [the] condition worsens — and that's something we
don't want to do to our patients... my main motto [from training] is do no harm"** `[03:07]`.

## How it works, and why it spares motor function

`[05:09]`–`[08:18]` — The device forces nitrous oxide through a probe at high pressure; as the gas expands
at the tip, it drops rapidly in temperature (**the Joule-Thomson effect**), forming an **"ice ball" reaching
about -70°C** in the surrounding tissue. This creates **vascular damage and severe endoneural edema**,
disrupting the nerve and producing **Wallerian degeneration** — but critically, **the myelin sheath,
perineurium, and epineurium are left intact.** Spilken states the clinical implication directly: **"I can
pretty much tell a patient they're not going to have any long-term motor or sensory deficit"** — sensory
numbness (typically arch to the second toe area) is common and expected, usually resolving within weeks to
a month, occasionally lasting three to six months, but **motor function is essentially never
affected.** Across "hundreds or maybe thousands" of procedures, he reports needing to prescribe
neuropathy medication for lingering symptoms in only one patient.

## The procedure itself

`[10:20]`–`[13:23]` — Performed under local anesthesia (lidocaine/epinephrine combination) via a small stab
incision; a **trocar** is used first to needle and open the fibroma directly (softening it before freezing),
then the cryoprobe is inserted for the freeze cycles. Protocol: **a 3mm probe for a fibroma (creating a
~3–3.5mm ice ball), up to 10mm for larger lesions like plantar fasciitis**; **each freeze runs about 2
minutes, followed by a 30-second thaw**, repeated **2–6 times per fibroma** depending on size — one
multi-fibroma case is described requiring 12–14 total freezes across the visit, all through minimal
incision sites. Aftercare: anti-inflammatory injection, antibiotic ointment, sterile compression dressing,
kept dry for 24 hours, then normal bathing with just a bandage for about a week. Patients typically **walk
out in a regular shoe the same day**, resume normal activity within days, and return to full activity
(including running) within about a week — a recovery profile Spilken contrasts directly with the
"extended," painful post-operative course of traditional plantar fibroma surgery.

## Reported outcomes

`[13:23]`–`[15:27]` — Cites published cryosurgery data across related conditions (not all specific to
plantar fibroma): a **2,700-subject study on neuroma treatment reporting 92% success**, a **heel pain study
reporting 90% success.** His own practice track record for plantar fibromas specifically: **about 85%
success with a single procedure** — meaning 85% of patients need no further treatment because their pain has
resolved, with treated nodules typically shrinking and softening even where some residual tissue remains.
He frames the reduction of *pain*, not nodule size, as the actual clinical endpoint throughout — the talk's
outcome statistics are explicitly pain-based, not based on nodule shrinkage.

## A cross-disciplinary lesson, stated candidly

`[09:18]`–`[10:20]` — Spilken, a podiatrist attending a hand-surgery-dominated conference, admits directly
that despite likely treating patients with concurrent Dupuytren's, **he had never previously thought to
examine or even ask about his patients' hands**: **"it never dawned on me, even though [some of my patients]
should be [known] to people in this room... it just never crossed my mind... hopefully you guys will be
telling your patients to take their shoes and socks off"** to check their feet in turn. He also flags an
important safety caution: **rule out sarcoma or another lesion type via MRI or ultrasound** before treating
an arch mass as a routine plantar fibroma.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Pain from plantar fibromas significant enough to affect walking and daily activity**, framed as the
  primary treatment target `[02:06]`.
- **Temporary sensory numbness from arch to second toe after cryosurgery**, typically resolving within
  weeks to a few months `[06:14]`.
- **Nodule softening and size reduction after cryosurgery**, presented as a secondary sign alongside the
  primary pain-relief outcome `[14:26]`.
- **The need to actively examine feet in Dupuytren's patients and hands in Ledderhose patients**, given
  clinicians in either specialty commonly don't check the other `[09:18]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Joule-Thomson effect** — the physical principle (rapid gas expansion causing rapid cooling) underlying
  the cryoprobe's ice-ball formation `[08:18]`.
- **Wallerian degeneration (with preserved sheath structures)** — the specific injury pattern cryosurgery
  produces: the nerve fiber's pain-transmitting portion is disrupted while its protective myelin sheath,
  perineurium, and epineurium remain intact, explaining the absence of lasting motor deficit `[06:14]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk presents mechanism and procedure directly.

## Source-stated confidence

Spilken is candid about the limits of the evidence base: FDA approval for lower-extremity cryosurgical use
dates only to **2003**, and he notes explicitly that **"we don't have more than say a five-year track
record"** for this specific application at the time of the talk. Success-rate figures are drawn from
different studies covering related but not identical conditions (neuroma, heel pain) alongside his own
practice's plantar-fibroma-specific 85% figure, and he does not conflate these into a single inflated
number.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Offers a genuine treatment alternative alongside `oXR_NOAaSa0`** (Seegenschmiedt's radiotherapy talk),
which reports standard Ledderhose surgical excision carries an 85% relapse rate and radiotherapy achieves
roughly two-thirds symptomatic relief with about 50% surgery avoidance — this talk's cryosurgery data (85%
single-treatment success by Spilken's own account) suggests a third, non-radiation, non-excisional option
worth weighing alongside those two, though the outcome measures aren't directly comparable (pain relief here
versus progression/remission there). **Extends the Dupuytren's-Ledderhose comorbidity discussed
quantitatively in `oXR_NOAaSa0`** (50%/20% reciprocal rates) — this talk is a first-person illustration of
exactly the cross-specialty blind spot that comorbidity data implies, from the foot-treating side of the
divide.
