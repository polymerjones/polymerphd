# Dr. Joe Dias "Patient Rated Outcome Measures" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**A PROM is now a required ingredient of clinical research, not an optional add-on — "you cannot just go
with objective measures anymore"** `[06:13]` — but Dias spends most of the talk arguing that PROM data is
far less trustworthy than a clean p-value makes it look, and that the field doesn't yet have the one number
that would make a PROM score actionable: **the minimum clinically important difference (MCID).** **"That is
not clean science yet. We are on a journey, and we haven't even established the measuring tool"** `[15:27]`.

## Why measurement quality matters for funding

`[00:00]`–`[02:03]` — Dias frames PROMs as a practical necessity for getting research funded, not just a
methodological nicety: funders require **demonstrated impact, feasibility, and trustworthiness ("a safe
pair of hands")** before committing money that is, in the end, public money either way — "it is in fact all
money that has been allocated there by the public." He notes a specific structural mismatch: **"musculoskeletal
[disease] is the biggest medical problem... that afflicts any of us anywhere on this globe, yet the funding,
regardless of where you are, is less than one percent."**

## What a PROM is, and the rule that makes it one

`[04:07]`–`[06:13]` — Citing Berwick (1997) — **"how do you judge medical effort? Only if it helps patients
or their families; anything that is done that does not help a patient is a waste"** — Dias defines a PROM as
asking patients to be the judges of whether treatment helped, via a fixed rule: **"you cannot ever do a PROM
where you interfere with the patient... you can't prompt the patient... the patient must be completely
independent of you as the clinician."** He separately invokes Lord Kelvin's dictum that unmeasured knowledge
is only superficial `[03:05]`.

## What's wrong with "clean" PROM data

`[09:14]`–`[12:23]` — the talk's most detailed section, listing specific ways a complete, statistically
significant dataset can still be false:

- **Around a third of respondents in some populations don't understand the questions** or answer to please
  the clinician rather than report honestly, including under litigation/compensation incentives to skew
  scores `[09:14]`.
- **Patients sometimes complete an outcome measure for the wrong condition** — Dias's example: a patient
  treated for carpal tunnel, now symptom-free there, fills in the form describing an unrelated new problem
  in both wrists, corrupting the intended measurement `[10:17]`.
- **Partial, not-fully-missing data**: a question left unscored because it's offensive, irrelevant, or
  ambiguous to that patient produces silently incomplete datasets `[10:17]`.
- **Personality-driven response bias**: respondents who mark one extreme regardless of severity, respondents
  who mark only the middle regardless of the question, respondents who over-rate their outcome to avoid
  disappointing the clinician even when "the arm is falling off," and respondents who interrogate the
  wording of every item `[11:18]`.
- **Guessed answers** — Dias describes a patient who marked answers without reading the form: **"I didn't
  have my reading glasses on, doctor — they just guessed where they want to put the mark"** `[11:18]`.
- **Response-rate collapse in young men specifically** — described as "a particular beast" requiring
  deliberate extra effort to retain in trial data `[12:23]`.

## The minimum clinically important difference (MCID)

`[13:24]`–`[15:27]` — defined as **"the smallest difference in [a] score that the patient perceives as a
benefit"** — distinct from statistical significance: **"the improvement after treatment can be statistically
significant... but not clinically meaningful."** Concrete figures given: **the MCID for a VAS pain score is
1.4**; **for goniometric range of motion, no MCID has yet been established** — which Dias calls out as open
work needed from the field. He warns the MCID is not a fixed property of an instrument: **it varies by
condition, comorbidity, patient demographics, and even by treatment type — "you might get a different
minimum clinically important difference for collagenase and a different one for dermofasciectomy."**

## His own outcome data — including an unflattering result

`[15:27]`–`[17:30]` — drawing on **roughly 12,000 patients and ~70,000 completed forms**, collected at every
clinical interaction. For Dupuytren's specifically, **the PEM's MCID is 3, "almost the same as for most hand
disorders."** Plotting his own results against that threshold, Dias reports that his best operations clear
it comfortably, but flags his own wrist surgery — a area he says he considers himself an expert in — as
falling **below the minimum clinically important difference for a whole bunch of patients**, adding wryly:
**"if my funder knew about this... this data is not to be shared on YouTube, please."** He separately notes
a broader, sobering comparison across hand surgery generally: **patients who are not operated on improve
by roughly half as much as those who are treated** `[17:30]`.

## Monitoring surgeon performance with funnel plots

`[17:30]`–`[18:32]` — describes a UK practice (used for hip and knee surgeons, publicly available) of
plotting individual surgeons' outcomes on a funnel plot by standard deviation from the norm, so patients can
identify outlier providers: **an "alarm" band beyond three standard deviations, an "alert" band between two
and three, and the rest considered "in control and therefore delivering care."**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None directly — this is a research-methodology talk about how outcomes are measured, not a discussion of
disease symptoms.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **PROM (patient-rated outcome measure)** — an outcome instrument completed independently by the patient,
  with the clinician forbidden from prompting or interpreting for them `[06:13]`.
- **MCID (minimum clinically important difference)** — the smallest score change a patient perceives as a
  real benefit, distinct from statistical significance `[13:24]`.
- **URAM** — described as one of the few PROMs specific to Dupuytren's alone, contrasted with hand-general
  instruments `[07:13]`.
- **Funnel plot** — a statistical-control-style chart used to flag outlier surgeon performance by standard
  deviation from the norm `[17:30]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"You are the one-eyed man in the land of the blind"** — on the danger of a researcher's own expertise
  making a flawed measurement look authoritative `[02:03]`.
- **Lord Kelvin: measurement failure limits knowledge to something "meagre" and "superficial"** `[03:05]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Dias is unusually explicit about the limits of his own field's tools: MCID is **"not established"** for
goniometric range of motion at all, PROM completion rates and reliability vary by population and are
**"a false reflection"** in roughly a third of some samples, and he states outright that hand-outcome
research broadly **"is not clean science yet."** His own wrist-surgery result below MCID is presented
candidly, not minimized.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly extends `AbY1KVhTVAo`** (Eaton's IDDB talk), which credits "Jo Dias's work with the BSSH" for a
photo-matching contracture-severity tool built specifically to avoid the goniometric measurement problem
this talk says has no established MCID — this talk supplies the methodological reasoning behind that design
choice. **Relevant to any later note discussing the PEM, MHQ, DASH, or URAM as outcome instruments** — this
talk should be treated as the primary source for how to interpret scores from any of them, given its
explicit warnings about response bias and incomplete data.
