# Dr. Wolfgang Wach: "Debate: Splinting is Beneficial" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Arguing the "beneficial" side of a formal debate against `dp45M2IEW3k`, Wach presents a series of patient
anecdotes and one small study suggesting splinting can maintain surgical correction — but only for as long as
it's actually worn, with recurrence following relatively quickly once splinting stops, even after years of
use.** He states this core pattern directly: **"splinting helps as long as the patient is wearing the splint —
when the splinting has stopped, the recurrence will probably be relatively quick. There's no time period after
which the result is stable"** `[05:05]`.

## Wach's own conflicting experience

`[00:00]`–`[01:01]` — Wach opens by undercutting his own debate position: he had Dupuytren's surgery himself
15 years earlier, wore **no splint** afterward, and remains fine — a story that, on its own, would argue
*against* splinting's necessity. He then offers a second, contrasting personal case: after a **PIP joint
collagenase injection** leaving roughly 10% residual contracture, he wore a **night splint for 14 months**,
which **maintained the correction perfectly**. He eventually found it inconvenient — the hand felt stiffer not
just briefly on removal but throughout the day — and stopped. **Within three months, recurrence returned him
to roughly his pre-injection state.**

## Patient anecdotes

`[01:01]`–`[03:04]` — Several further patient reports are described:

- A patient with **eight surgeries and one PNF procedure**, whose own impression was that splinting is needed
  specifically when a joint **already has a tendency to "spring back"** — an initial small MCP correction
  likely doesn't need it, but he personally felt he always needed splinting after his own surgeries and PNF.
- **"Reiner Z"**: wore night splints for three months on two separate occasions, then switched to wearing the
  splint only when he felt the joint "springing back" (a week or two at a time as needed) — maintaining results
  over **eight and three years** respectively across two situations.
- A US patient: no splint after a first PNF procedure; a different doctor advised splinting after a second PNF,
  which he reports **"helped immeasurably."**
- A patient who wore a splint **consistently for three years**, maintaining PNF results throughout — then lost
  the splint while moving, stopped wearing it, and had a **quick recurrence.**
- An example supplied by **Albrecht Meinel** (see `882JB9cXDME`, his own dedicated splinting-case-series talk):
  a patient shown before PNF, after PNF, and five weeks into subsequent splinting — the hand opening visibly
  further than the PNF result alone achieved — with dramatic further improvement after **15 months of
  splinting.**

## A small controlled study of splinting alone

`[03:04]`–`[04:04]` — Wach closes with data rather than anecdote: a small study (six patients, attributed to
researchers he names as Katy Ball and Nanchahal) testing whether **splinting alone, without any prior
treatment**, could improve an existing MCP/PIP contracture of roughly 10–30 degrees. Splinting alone
**improved contracture and allowed greater finger opening**, with continuing improvement over follow-up in
several patients — one patient's result was described as particularly impressive, others stabilized. Follow-up
extended to **6–24 months**, with no patients lost to follow-up; the one patient followed to **24 months**
(patient 4) had maintained the result.

## Wach's proposed rule

`[05:05]`–`[06:06]` — Splinting is **not required for simple, first-time surgeries** (Wach gives his own
example: a straightforward 20-degree MCP correction he personally got along without). Splinting **is proposed
as beneficial for**: recurrence surgeries, fingers that have already "adjusted" to their contracture and tend
to spring back quickly, and **minimally invasive techniques** specifically. His central, repeated point:
splinting's benefit appears to last **only as long as it's worn** — there is **no fixed duration after which
the result becomes permanently stable**, illustrated by his own patient who maintained correction for three
years of consistent wear but recurred quickly once he stopped. He closes by proposing splinting could also
serve as a **preventive measure without any prior treatment**, pointing to the small study above as the best
available indication of that.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **A hand feeling stiffer throughout the day, not just briefly after splint removal**, cited as Wach's own
  reason for eventually discontinuing his splint despite it maintaining correction `[01:01]`.
- **Recurrence returning a joint to roughly its pre-treatment state within months of stopping a splint**,
  reported across multiple individual patient cases `[01:01]`, `[02:02]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Wach is unusually transparent about the weakness of his own evidence base: he opens by openly undermining his
assigned debate position with his own contrary personal experience, and explicitly labels the bulk of his
supporting material as **"anecdotal"** patient stories rather than controlled data, reserving that
characterization even for a case supplied by a fellow hand surgeon (Meinel). He clearly separates this
anecdotal material from the one small, structured study he presents at the end, treating it as a distinct and
stronger tier of evidence. He does not claim to have resolved the debate, closing simply with "I hope I made
my point" rather than a stronger claim of proof.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly paired with `dp45M2IEW3k`** (Chojnowski's "Splinting Confers No Benefit" debate talk) — these two
talks are the formal opposing sides of the same symposium debate; Chojnowski's three randomized trials
specifically test **post-surgical routine night splinting** and find no significant benefit, while Wach's
anecdotes and small study more often describe splinting for **recurrence-prone joints or as a standalone
preventive measure** — a distinction that may reconcile rather than contradict the two talks' conclusions,
consistent with Wach's own proposed rule that splinting's value depends on the specific clinical situation
rather than being uniformly beneficial or useless. **Directly relevant to `882JB9cXDME`** (Meinel's own
splinting case-series talk) — the patient case Wach attributes to Meinel almost certainly comes from that
talk's own series, which reports the same pattern (continued extension gains from splinting after PNF)
across several further cases. **Relevant to `kq4hr06tOtY`** (Meinel's pathogenesis talk) — that talk's
"tissue retention" theory is the proposed theoretical basis for why splinting continues to help even after
PNF has already released the cord. **Relevant to `qoktkyD8P_o`** (Degreef & Brauns's tension-vs-compression
splinting RCT) — that trial's non-surgical splinting population and significant extension-deficit improvement
is broadly consistent with Wach's framing of splinting as most clearly beneficial outside the routine
post-surgical context Chojnowski's trials specifically test.
