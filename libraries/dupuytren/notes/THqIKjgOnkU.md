# Dupuytren Decision Trees (Dr. Charles Eaton, 2017 ASSH Annual Meeting)

## Central claim

**Surgeons are currently Dupuytren's primary caregivers only because no medical treatment exists yet — not
because that's the right long-term model — and Eaton contrasts today's deformity-driven decision tree with
the biology-driven one he thinks the disease actually deserves.** His framing: **"because we're surgeons, we
don't treat chronic conditions — we treat conditions that have a definitive beginning and end, and we are
the vehicle to go from the beginning to the end. And that's unfortunately the way we are currently with
Dupuytren's disease, because there is not yet a medical treatment"** `[01:01]`.

## The decision tree Eaton wants versus the one that exists

`[00:00]`–`[02:02]` — The aspirational model: Dupuytren's recognized as a **rheumatologic condition**,
assessed by a **biomarker activity panel**. Low activity → a genetic panel to establish lifetime risk and
decide on monitoring. High activity → **biomarker-guided, disease-specific medical treatment.** Only once
actual deformity develops does a surgeon get involved, for **"the lowest-risk, simplest procedure to reduce
the deformity,"** after which the patient returns to the rheumatologist — the same model used for rheumatoid
arthritis, gout, and Raynaud's disease. The current reality: decisions are made **on degree of deformity, not
biology**, and the standard surgical flow diagram's "end point" is really just **the patient not coming back
to see the surgeon** — **"of course it doesn't stop for the patient"** even though the surgical episode has
formally concluded.

## Outcome depends on biology and contracture together, not either alone

`[03:04]`–`[04:09]` — Eaton frames four combinations: **mild contracture + mild biology → good outlook; severe
contracture + mild biology → still good, because technical tools for deformity are well established; mild
contracture + aggressive biology → poor long-term outlook despite modest deformity; severe contracture +
aggressive biology → real risk of failing every available treatment.** The practical implication: **surgery
targeting deformity works reasonably well when biology is mild, regardless of severity — but no surgical
technique reliably controls aggressive biology itself.**

## Current decision breakpoints

`[04:09]`–`[05:09]` — **PIP contracture around 60 degrees and composite contracture around 90 degrees** are
stated as the key thresholds: below them, **the least-morbid, lowest-risk procedure is preferred** since
outcomes are good regardless of technique; **above a 60-degree PIP contracture, fasciectomy does better
long-term than minimally invasive treatment**; beyond that, secondary anatomical changes may require
**staged or combined procedures.**

## Skepticism about "barrier procedures"

`[05:09]`–`[06:09]` — Biology assessment currently relies on **clinical diathesis risk factors**, with
**dermofasciectomy as the current gold-standard biology-modifying procedure.** Newer **"barrier
procedures"** — placing an absorbable sheet or a fascial flap between skin and the wound bed — are proposed
as biology-modifying, but Eaton states direct skepticism: **"I'm not convinced of that at all. I think the
thing that we know that we're changing is the mechanics of the hand — the shear strain, stress, mechanical
forces on the soft tissues of the palm — with each of these procedures, and that may have a secondary effect
on provoking the biology."** In other words, he suspects these procedures work (if they work) by altering
local mechanics, not by directly suppressing disease biology, despite how they're often framed.

## Two commonly missed co-diagnoses, and the mistakes they cause

`[07:12]`–`[08:12]` — **Trigger finger** frequently co-occurs in the Dupuytren's demographic. Eaton describes
seeing **two cases, operated on by skilled surgeons**, where combined fasciotomy and trigger-finger release
led to **over-release of the tendon sheath and dramatic flexor tendon bowstringing at the MP level** — a
complication he calls **"very difficult... sometimes impossible"** to fix. His recommendation: be
conservative when combining these procedures, particularly in rheumatoid patients who also have trigger
finger.

A second, easily missed **"stealth diagnosis"**: **sagittal band rupture**, which can present as a severe
MP joint contracture resembling Dupuytren's. The distinguishing clue: **finger rotation at the MP level not
explained by the palmar cords alone.** Eaton has performed **sagittal band reconstruction at the same time
as needle fasciotomy or open fasciectomy** — explicitly noting this combination **"you can't do... with
collagenase,"** since collagenase doesn't allow simultaneous mechanical reconstruction the way a
needle-based or open procedure does.

## Choosing a redo procedure

`[08:12]`–`[09:14]` — Framed as movement on a **reconstructive ladder**: if a prior procedure worked well and
**lasted years** before recurring, and the patient was satisfied, it's reasonable to **repeat the same
procedure** — or step down to something less invasive. If the prior procedure **failed or recurred rapidly**,
consider **moving up the ladder**; if that failure looks diathesis-driven specifically, **consider jumping up
two rungs at once**, since the underlying goal throughout is minimizing lifetime morbidity from both the
disease itself and its cumulative treatment.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Flexor tendon bowstringing at the MP level after combined fasciotomy and trigger-finger release**,
  described as a severe, sometimes unfixable complication of over-aggressive sheath release `[07:12]`.
- **Finger rotation at the MP joint not explained by visible palmar cords**, flagged as the specific sign of
  a missed sagittal band rupture rather than pure Dupuytren's contracture `[08:12]`.
- **Rapid recurrence after a prior procedure**, used as a specific signal to escalate rather than repeat
  the same treatment approach `[08:12]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Reconstructive ladder (for redo Dupuytren's)** — Eaton's framework for stepping treatment intensity up
  or down based on how the prior procedure performed `[08:12]`.
- **Barrier procedures** — newer techniques placing material between skin and wound bed, proposed (but not
  fully accepted by Eaton) as biology-modifying `[05:09]`.
- **Stealth diagnosis (sagittal band rupture)** — a hidden cause of MP contracture that can be mistaken for
  Dupuytren's alone `[08:12]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The standard chronic-disease flow diagram versus the surgical flow diagram** — contrasting a model with
  an ongoing biologic-management loop against one with a false "end point" defined only by the patient
  not returning `[02:02]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Eaton is explicit about distinguishing established fact from his own clinical opinion: the barrier-procedure
mechanism claim is stated as **"I'm not convinced of that at all"** — his own skepticism, not a settled
finding — and the redo-treatment ladder is presented as reasoned clinical judgment rather than data-backed
protocol. He's candid that current biology assessment relies only on **clinical risk factors**, not any
direct measurement, reinforcing the talk's overall argument that a real biomarker would change everything
described here.
