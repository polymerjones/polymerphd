# Dr. David O'Gorman "Basic Research Priorities for Dupuytren's" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**No laboratory in the world currently has a model that reproduces Dupuytren's disease developing over
time — only static snapshots — and O'Gorman argues building one, done properly, requires expertise the
field doesn't yet have enough of: tissue engineering, computational biology, and epigenetics.** He states the
gap candidly, citing a rejected grant review: **"a reviewer... told me, 'Dr. O'Gorman, it's a lovely grant,
you've got all kinds of great data, but all you have are windows in time — you have not shown us that you
have a model in your laboratory that actually shows the development of disease over time.' And they were
right, and I don't think anybody else does either"** `[03:03]`.

## What a proper model would require

`[03:03]`–`[04:04]` — Human cells with **pre-characterized genetics**, grown on **physiologically relevant
extracellular matrices** (his own point from a companion talk), subjected to realistic **biomechanical
stress and biochemical stimuli**, and — flagged as a likely-overlooked variable — **correct oxygen tension**:
**"I find it very, very unlikely that Dupuytren's cords grow at 21% oxygen, [because] very fibrotic tissues...
[are hypoxic] — yet we do all of our analyses at 21% oxygen."** Current tools (he cites a Flex Cell system
his own lab uses) can impose mechanical stress and support 3D culture, and are being adapted toward lower-
oxygen conditions, but still lack a **flowing vascular supply**, sufficient cell density, and adequately
dense collagen to be truly physiological.

## Three kinds of expertise the field needs

`[02:01]`–`[06:05]` — **Tissue engineers**, to build reproducible cord models; **computational biologists**,
to handle the scale of modern genomic data (both large population studies like GWAS and deep individual-
patient sequencing); and **epigenetics specialists**, a discipline O'Gorman notes **wasn't discussed at all
at the conference that year**. To illustrate the scale problem concretely: a chromatin immunoprecipitation
sequencing (ChIP-seq) project on just **6 patients, 9 samples**, generated **~2×10⁷ sequence reads per
person**, amounting to **300 gigabytes of raw data — processing to 2 terabytes.** His point: that's the data
burden for six people; scaling to a hundred or a thousand patients isn't currently feasible, but the field
needs to be building toward that capacity.

## A striking epigenetic finding: loss of IGF2 imprinting

`[08:06]`–`[10:10]` — O'Gorman gives a detailed, concrete example of what epigenetic research can reveal that
genome sequencing alone cannot. Background: most genes are expressed equally from both inherited alleles, but
**about 1% of human genes show "imprinting"** — parent-of-origin-specific expression. At the **IGF2/H19
locus**: on the paternally inherited allele, a methylated insulator region blocks a protein (CTCF) from
binding, allowing **IGF2 to be transcribed**; on the maternally inherited allele, the same region is
unmethylated, **CTCF binds and blocks IGF2 transcription**, so **H19** (a non-coding RNA) is transcribed
instead. Net normal result: **IGF2 is expressed only from the paternal allele.** Screening roughly a dozen
Dupuytren's-diseased cell samples against matched normal palmar fascia from the same patients, O'Gorman's
group found: **9 out of 10 (roughly) diseased samples showed loss of this imprinting** — **IGF2 being
transcribed from *both* alleles** instead of just one, attributed to **abnormal DNA methylation** at the
control region. This is explicitly an **epigenetic event, invisible to standard genome sequencing** — it can
only be detected through dedicated epigenetic assays, not GWAS or exome sequencing.

## Why this can't happen without collaboration

`[10:10]`–`[11:11]` — O'Gorman closes by naming the interdependence directly: his lab lacks expertise that
colleagues (named as "Rude" [likely Ruud Bank] and Dominic Furniss) have and vice versa; **none of this is
possible without clinical colleagues supplying tissue samples**, funding support, and — explicitly —
**patient community engagement** to help raise both money and awareness for the field.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Genomic imprinting** — parent-of-origin-specific gene expression, affecting roughly 1% of human genes,
  typically involving growth-regulatory genes `[08:06]`.
- **Loss of imprinting** — the specific disease-associated finding here: a normally monoallelically expressed
  gene (IGF2) becoming biallelically expressed due to abnormal DNA methylation `[09:08]`.
- **ChIP-seq (chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing)** — the genome-wide technique used to map protein-DNA
  interactions (here, beta-catenin binding sites) across patient samples `[06:05]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Windows in time" versus a true developmental model** — O'Gorman's own phrase (via the grant reviewer
  who challenged him) for the gap between current snapshot-based research and what the field actually needs
  `[03:03]`.

## Source-stated confidence

O'Gorman is unusually candid about a personal setback (a rejected grant, quoted directly rather than
paraphrased favorably) and frames his research priorities explicitly as **his own opinion**, not a
consensus: **"I don't expect this to be representative of every research lab — Dominic will probably have
different thoughts, and Rude will probably have different thoughts."** The IGF2 imprinting finding is
presented with a specific, if modest, sample size (about a dozen matched pairs), appropriately scaled
confidence for a preliminary finding rather than a large validated result.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly extends `6aG_CIYTX8A`** (O'Gorman's own ECM talk, same symposium) — both talks argue current
laboratory methods (plastic culture substrates there, standard oxygen tension here) may be producing
non-physiological, potentially misleading data, and both call for more rigorous, physiologically matched
experimental conditions going forward. **Directly relevant to `-3a_yiOu_3I` and `-0JtjUrrU4o`** (the GWAS
talks) — this talk's emphasis on epigenetics as a layer invisible to standard genome sequencing supplies a
concrete reason why GWAS findings alone (explaining only ~14–16% of heritability per Ophoff's own figures)
would be expected to leave much of Dupuytren's biology unaccounted for. **Directly complements `8Kp5Fz7ZKl0`**
(Raykha's IGF talk, from O'Gorman's own lab, five years earlier) — that talk shows IGFBP6 downregulation
increasing IGF2 bioavailability post-translationally; this talk's imprinting-loss finding increases IGF2
*production* itself, epigenetically — two independent mechanisms converging on the same outcome.
