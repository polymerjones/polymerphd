# Dupuytren Foundation: Infographic Video

## Central claim

**This Dupuytren Foundation overview argues that every current treatment — minimally invasive or open surgery
— ultimately fails as a cure because each one treats the visible anatomy rather than the underlying cell
biology, and frames better understanding of that biology as the field's central research goal.** It states this
directly: **"recurrence, or return of contracture, is an unsolved problem... none of these are a cure — all
procedures ultimately fail because they treat the anatomy, but not the root cause, the core biology. We have
to change our approach to understand the biology, so that we can develop better treatment options"** `[02:02]`.

## Demographics and risk factors

`[00:01]`–`[01:01]` — Dupuytren's disease runs in families with a genetic basis that **can skip several
generations**, meaning many patients have no known affected relative; it is **rare in younger people**,
predominantly a disease of seniors. **Men develop it roughly 10 years younger than women on average**, though
**after age 50, both sexes develop new cases at a similar rate.** In the US it is **primarily a Caucasian
condition**, less common progressively in Hispanic, African-American, and Asian-American populations. Risk
**increases with cardiac risk factors — particularly smoking, diabetes, and an abnormal lipid profile.** The
disease is named after a 19th-century French surgeon.

## Scale and progression

`[01:01]`–`[02:02]` — Disease progresses through a sequence: **nodule, then cord, then contracture.** At any
given time, **roughly four out of five affected people are still "early"** — meaning they don't yet have a
contracture. Each year, **about 2% of people with the disease develop a contracture requiring treatment.**
Scale: **10 million Americans currently have Dupuytren's contracture — more people than live in the entire
state of New York.** The disease typically **starts in one hand in about four out of five people**, but
**ultimately involves both hands in about four out of five people** as it progresses — a notable reversal
between onset and eventual course — and is **slightly more common in the right hand.**

## What predicts a more aggressive course

`[01:01]`–`[02:02]` — Disease tends to be **more aggressive** in people who: develop it **earlier in life**,
have **a relative with the disease**, or have **both hands already involved.** It also tends to be more
aggressive in people with **related conditions**: frozen shoulder, Peyronie's disease, knuckle pads, and
Ledderhose disease. **No preventative treatment or medication currently exists.**

## Treatment options and recurrence

`[02:02]` — Contracture is treated with procedures, either **minimally invasive** (percutaneous fasciotomy —
cutting the cord with a needle — or collagenase enzyme injection to dissolve cords under the skin) or **open
surgery** (fasciectomy, removing diseased tissue; or dermofasciectomy, which additionally replaces the
overlying skin with a graft). **Recurrence is fastest after minimally invasive procedures; fasciectomy results
last, on average, over twice as long; dermofasciectomy lasts even longer** — but critically, **none of these
approaches is a cure.**

## The underlying biology, explained plainly

`[02:02]`–`[03:05]` — The body is made largely of **collagen**, described here as "little threads," and
**myofibroblasts** are the cells that modify it. Myofibroblasts **shrink collagen by pulling on some of the
threads to create slack**, which tissue enzymes then remove, **shortening the collagen strands overall** — a
process that is entirely **normal during wound healing**, such as when a cut heals. In Dupuytren's disease,
**this same normal process is activated inappropriately.** The stated call to action: more research is needed
to understand **why** this inappropriate activation happens, in order to develop genuinely better treatments —
framed as the Dupuytren Foundation's core mission.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Nodule, cord, and contracture** presented explicitly as sequential disease stages, not separate conditions
  `[01:01]`.
- **Frozen shoulder, Peyronie's disease, knuckle pads, and Ledderhose disease**, named together as associated
  conditions correlating with a more aggressive disease course `[01:01]`.

## Source-stated confidence

This is a public-education infographic video, not a research presentation — its statistics and claims are
presented as settled public-facing facts without individual citations, consistent with its role summarizing
the broader research and clinical literature rather than presenting new findings. The core argument (that
current treatments address anatomy, not biology) is presented as the Dupuytren Foundation's own stated
position and rationale for funding further research, rather than as a novel or contested claim.
