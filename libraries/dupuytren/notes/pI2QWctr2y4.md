# Guido Dolmans MD, "Linkage analysis in six Dutch families" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Using linkage analysis across six Dutch multi-generation families, Dolmans's group found only suggestive
(not genome-wide-significant) chromosomal regions — and specifically failed to replicate a previously
published Swedish-family finding on chromosome 16 — a result attributed to genetic heterogeneity, meaning
different families' disease may not share the same causal gene.** Dolmans states this directly: **"we don't
replicate that region [chromosome 16]... too bad they are not genome-wide significant — that could be caused
because of a lack of power, but I think it's also a combination with heterogeneity, and it means that probably
not the same gene causes the disease in these different families"** `[06:13]`.

## Linkage analysis versus genome-wide association

`[00:01]`–`[02:03]` — Dolmans distinguishes this method from the genome-wide association study (GWAS)
approach presented separately (see `-3a_yiOu_3I`): **linkage analysis targets rare, high-effect mutations
that cause Mendelian (single-gene) disease patterns**, studied via family pedigrees, whereas GWAS looks at
common variants across unrelated individuals. The group first confirmed a **dominant mode of inheritance** in
their studied families, then used linkage analysis to localize the genome region likely to contain a
disease-causing gene — leveraging the fact that close relatives share large stretches of identical DNA, and
tracking which DNA segments co-segregate with disease status across a pedigree.

## The LOD score

`[02:03]`–`[03:04]` — The **logarithm of odds (LOD) score** is the significance measure used: a LOD score of
**-2 disproves linkage**, **+2 is suggestive**, and **3 or higher is genome-wide significant** — a LOD score
of 3 corresponds to roughly **1,000-to-1 odds against the finding occurring by chance.**

## Study design

`[03:04]`–`[04:05]` — **Six families, 64 family members, 32 affected individuals**, recruited by asking
patients with two or more affected relatives to participate along with their families; blood was collected,
DNA isolated, and pedigrees constructed. Genotyping used a **specialized linkage panel chip with ~10,000
markers** (versus the ~300,000-marker chip used for the separate GWAS), deliberately designed with
non-correlated SNPs suited to linkage analysis. Quality control included genotyping-error checks and
pedigree-relationship verification via genetic fingerprinting, followed by statistical analysis under an
**autosomal dominant inheritance model**.

## Results

`[04:05]`–`[06:13]` — Dolmans is explicit that finding different chromosomal regions than the GWAS is
*expected*, not a contradiction — rare high-effect mutations (linkage analysis's target) and common
low-effect variants (GWAS's target) can occur at different genomic locations even for the same disease.
Results: **chromosome 7 again showed a suggestive peak (LOD 2.45)**, but at a different region than the
GWAS's chromosome 7 finding; **chromosome 18 reached a maximum LOD of 2.06** (suggestive, not significant);
**chromosome 19 reached LOD 2.40** (also not significant). **Chromosome 16** — the region previously reported
significant (LOD 3) in a published Swedish family study — **was not replicated** in this Dutch cohort.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Linkage analysis** — a family-pedigree-based genetic method for localizing rare, high-effect disease-
  causing mutations, distinct from genome-wide association studies of common variants `[00:01]`.
- **Logarithm of odds (LOD) score** — the significance measure used in linkage analysis; -2 disproves linkage,
  +2 is suggestive, 3+ is genome-wide significant `[02:03]`.
- **Genetic heterogeneity** — the phenomenon where different affected families may carry different
  disease-causing genes, offered as an explanation for failed replication across cohorts `[06:13]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Dolmans discloses no financial interest and is candid that none of the found regions reached genome-wide
significance, explicitly weighing two possible explanations (insufficient statistical power versus genuine
genetic heterogeneity across families) rather than favoring one. He states a clear priority for future work —
replicating the GWAS findings first, before pursuing sequencing of these linkage-suggestive but non-significant
regions — and explicitly invites other research groups with family cohorts to combine data to increase
statistical power.
