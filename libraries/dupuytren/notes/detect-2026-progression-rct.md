# Progression of Dupuytren Contracture: A Randomized Controlled Trial Comparing Surgery, Needle Fasciotomy, and Collagenase Injection

## Central claim

**In a randomized trial of 302 patients, surgery produced significantly less progression in the treated
finger than either needle fasciotomy or collagenase injection over 2 years — but none of the treatments
changed how likely progression was in fingers that weren't treated** `[¶6]`.

## The trial design

`[¶5]`

This is a **finger-level analysis from the DETECT (Dupuytren Treatment Effectiveness Trial) dataset**,
covering **302 participants and 423 initially treated fingers**. Progression was defined precisely, not left
to judgment: **"an increase in contracture angle between 3 months and 2 years after treatment."** The
analysis deliberately separates three finger categories — the treated finger itself, an adjacent untreated
finger, and all untreated fingers — to see whether treating one finger has any spillover effect on others.

## What it found

`[¶6]`

Follow-up was strong: **279 of 302 participants (92%) completed 3-month measurements, and 274 (91%) completed
2-year measurements.** In treated fingers, **surgery resulted in significantly less progression than needle
fasciotomy** (mean difference 9.7°, 95% CI 3.7 to 16°) **and less than collagenase injection** (mean
difference 6.0°, 95% CI 0.1 to 12°), with fewer retreatments needed after surgery. Critically, **"no
between-group differences were observed in adjacent untreated or all untreated fingers"** — treating one
finger surgically doesn't appear to protect, or otherwise affect, the fingers left alone. Risk factors
identified for progression or retreatment, regardless of which treatment was used: **younger age at
treatment, smoking, a dominantly-affected proximal interphalangeal joint, and involvement of the little
finger.**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Increase in contracture angle over time**, defined as the trial's specific progression measure, tracked
  separately in treated versus untreated fingers `[¶5]`.
- **Retreatment need**, tracked as a downstream consequence of progression `[¶6]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **DETECT (Dupuytren Treatment Effectiveness Trial)** — the source trial this finger-level analysis draws
  its data from `[¶5]`.
- **Finger-level analysis** — analysing outcomes per finger rather than per patient, which is what lets this
  study separate treated-finger effects from any effect on untreated fingers in the same hand `[¶5]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None — outcomes are reported as measured angles and confidence intervals.

## Source-stated confidence

The authors are explicit about which claim their data does and doesn't support: **"Surgery offers more
durable results in treated fingers compared with percutaneous methods, although progression and retreatment
rates in untreated fingers are similar across all treatment groups"** — i.e., don't extend the treated-finger
finding to imply any protective effect elsewhere in the hand `[¶7]`. They translate the risk-factor findings
into a specific counseling recommendation: **"Patients at higher risk for progression (ie, young smokers with
affected little fingers) may benefit most from surgery"** `[¶7]`. A funding and conflict-of-interest
disclosure is stated directly: **"The authors have no financial relationships or conflicts of interest to
declare. This study has been funded by the Research Council of Finland"** `[¶7]`.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Converges with both `shaheen-2025-treatment-comparison-review` and `nocek-2026-recurrence-systematic-review`**
on the same directional finding — surgery/fasciectomy holds its result better than needle- or
injection-based treatment — reached here from a single randomized trial rather than pooled study data, which
is a stronger form of the same evidence. **Extends `nocek-2026-recurrence-systematic-review`'s finding that
PIP-joint contractures recur more than MCP-joint ones**: this trial identifies "dominantly affected proximal
interphalangeal joint" as an independent risk factor for progression specifically, naming the same joint as a
risk marker through a different outcome measure (progression angle, not binary recurrence).
