# Ardeshir Bayat MD PhD "Genetics of Dupuytren's Disease" 2010 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Multiple independent lines of evidence support a genetic component to Dupuytren's, but individual gene-
and variant-level findings have largely failed to replicate strongly across studies — the field has
evidence of heritability without yet having a clear molecular target.** Bayat states this directly about
his own field's candidate-gene work: after pooling several studies' findings, **"really nothing... sort of
[explains] disease pathology"** on its own `[09:21]`–`[10:22]`.

## Why Dupuytren's is considered genetic at all

`[00:09]`–`[02:13]` — Bayat lists the standard criteria applied: **presence in twins, a familial tendency,
ethnic-specific prevalence, and disease severity tracking with familial predisposition.** He cites a
**sibling recurrence risk ratio**, compared between UK and Icelandic cohorts, as showing a **"parallel
phenomena"** across populations — offered as evidence the genetic contribution is not a single population's
artifact. This sits alongside the geographic pattern noted at the start of the talk: **Dupuytren's clusters
in regions of Northern European descent worldwide**, consistent with other sources in this library.

## The practical problem of studying it

`[02:13]`–`[03:14]` — Bayat flags a real methodological difficulty: genetic material can come from blood or
from tissue (fascia, skin, fat), but **disease latency means an apparently unaffected relative or control
patient may simply not yet have developed visible disease**, weakening family-history-based controls.
Historically, **carpal tunnel syndrome patients have been used as a tissue control group** for comparison.
He also flags that studies comparing **palm-only versus digit-involving disease** need to account for the
fact that a tissue sample is only a snapshot of whatever stage the disease had reached when it was removed.

## What's been tried, and how far it got

`[05:16]`–`[13:27]`:

- **Linkage study** (English/Swedish families): reported a LOD score around 3 — Bayat notes genome-wide
  significance in family linkage studies conventionally requires a LOD score of at least 3, calling even a
  LOD of 3 "not very [high]" in practice, and flags that findings from a single family study carry limited
  weight `[07:17]`–`[08:18]`.
- **Candidate-gene association studies**, run by multiple groups comparing cases to controls: identified
  several genes of interest but, pooled together, **did not converge on a consistent account of disease
  pathology** `[09:21]`–`[10:22]`.
- **Copy number variation (CNV) studies**: an earlier 2008 study using a lower-density gene chip found no
  statistically significant result; Bayat's group repeated the analysis with a newer chip (**"about a
  million" markers**) and identified areas of interest on multiple chromosomes, flagged as needing further
  investigation rather than as a confirmed finding `[10:22]`–`[11:23]`.
- **Gene expression studies** comparing nodule, cord, and control tissue (published ~2008): found clear
  differential expression between nodule and control tissue, with affected gene-function categories
  including **cell proliferation and matrix deposition/organization**. One named candidate, **ADAM-12**,
  showed differing expression levels across fascia, cord, nodule, fat, and skin samples
  `[11:23]`–`[15:32]`.

## Where this could lead clinically

`[16:34]`–`[18:37]` — presented explicitly as future direction, not current practice: Bayat frames genetic/
susceptibility testing as potentially useful for **prevention**, for **characterizing a patient's molecular
disease profile before choosing a therapy** ("a patient carrying a particular genotype... may not be
suitable" for a given treatment), and for **dosing decisions** — drawing an explicit analogy to
personalized-medicine pharmacogenomics in infection treatment, where patients on the same antibiotic
respond differently. He is clear this capability does not yet exist for Dupuytren's.

## Proposed diagnostic workflow

`[18:37]`–`[20:41]` — Bayat outlines an assessment sequence largely already standard: **detailed history**
(including diathesis and predisposing factors relevant to recurrence risk and treatment choice), **clinical
examination** to rule out other conditions and confirm the diagnosis, **Hueston's tabletop test**, and
**goniometry** for severity. He proposes genetic testing (**a buccal swab**) as a future addition to this
workflow, not a current one.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Sibling recurrence risk ratio** — a measure comparing disease risk in relatives of affected individuals
  against the general population, used as genetic evidence `[01:11]`.
- **LOD score** — the statistical measure of linkage significance in family genetic studies; ~3 is
  described as a conventional (if modest) significance threshold `[07:17]`.
- **Copy number variation (CNV)** — structural genetic variation examined as a possible susceptibility
  factor `[05:16]`.
- **ADAM-12** — a candidate gene shown here with differential expression across Dupuytren's-affected and
  unaffected tissue types `[14:31]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Antibiotic response variability as an analogy for why genetic susceptibility testing might guide
  Dupuytren's treatment dosing** `[17:36]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Bayat is explicit that this field has not converged: candidate-gene findings are described as failing to
explain pathology when pooled, the CNV finding from his own repeated analysis is presented as requiring
further investigation rather than as confirmed, and the entire clinical-application section is framed
conditionally ("may become quite relevant") rather than as available now. Where this note's own coverage is
thin, that reflects transcript quality, not a hedge stated by the speaker — see the note above on source
quality.
