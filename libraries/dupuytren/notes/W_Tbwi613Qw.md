# Vilhjalmur Finsen PhD, "The Epidemiology of Dupuytren's Disease in Bosnia" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Dupuytren's disease is genuinely common in Bosnia — not the near-exclusively-Nordic disease it's often
assumed to be — but most people who have it never need or want treatment, and the gap between "has the
disease" and "needs surgery" is much larger than symposium discussion usually acknowledges.** Finsen states
this directly: **"there's one thing... that doesn't come out in conferences like this, which is the
enormous difference between prevalence and need of treatment"** `[08:03]`.

## An imperfect but rare dataset

`[00:00]`–`[01:00]` — Motivated by the near-total absence of Mediterranean/Balkan prevalence data (the only
comparable prior study found was from Spain, 1988), a Bosnian student conducted the fieldwork for his thesis
in the early 1970s, shortly after wartime disruption — using a simple, admittedly imperfect method: **stopping
people over age 50 in the street**, recording age, ethnicity (Serbian, Croat, or Muslim), prior Dupuytren's
surgery, diabetes ("do you have sugar"), tobacco and alcohol use, then examining their hands. Finsen is
candid about the method's limits: **"the sampling method is not... the way it's supposed to be done, but...
how [else] would there be to do a thing like this in Bosnia? ...it's probably better than just anecdotal
comments from colleagues."**

## The numbers

`[02:01]`–`[04:01]` — Using a simple three-stage classification (stage 1: palmar changes only, no
contracture; stage 2: under 90 degrees total finger contracture; stage 3: over 90 degrees), **1,287 people
were stopped**, with **610 men and 597 women** aged over 50 remaining after exclusions. **210 of the men
(34%) and 94 of the women (16%) had some sign of Dupuytren's disease; actual contractures were present in
79 men (13%) and 17 women (2.8%).** Prevalence rose with age as expected, though the oldest age brackets
showed a **drop from very old age**, with Finsen cautioning the sample sizes there were small. **Right-hand
involvement was four times more common than left-hand in men**, with **no such asymmetry in women.**

## An ethnic difference, held loosely

`[05:01]`–`[06:02]` — Comparing across Bosnia's Serbian, Croat, and Muslim populations: **Muslim men showed
significantly lower prevalence than Croat or Serbian men**; **no such difference existed among women.**
Finsen draws a deliberate parallel to a separate study his group ran comparing Norwegian and Sami
populations in northern Norway, which found **prevalence twice as high among Norwegians as among Sami** —
suggesting a possible genetic-population pattern, though he flags the Bosnian ethnic finding could equally
be **"a spurious finding,"** especially given it didn't replicate in women.

## What did and didn't correlate

`[06:02]`–`[07:03]` — **Tobacco smoking showed no increased prevalence; alcohol consumption showed no
increased prevalence; urban versus rural residence made no difference.** **Diabetes mellitus, by contrast,
showed a strong association**: an odds ratio of **2.75 in men and 2.79 in women** for Dupuytren's given
diabetes. In a separate diabetic clinic sample (proven diabetics, not just self-reported), **prevalence of
Dupuytren's reached 60–70%** — a striking figure Finsen flags as coming from "small numbers" but a
"enormous" percentage nonetheless.

## Prevalence is not the same as need for treatment

`[08:03]`–`[09:04]` — Finsen makes this the talk's central point using two independent pieces of evidence.
First, despite prevalence being only somewhat lower in women than men (roughly 50–60% of the male rate in
older age groups), **the ratio of *operations* between women and men in a large Swedish study was 1:7** —
women with the disease are treated far less often than the prevalence gap alone would predict. Second,
**DASH disability scores** collected from his own institution's surgical waiting list showed **Dupuytren's
patients scoring only about 10–12** (0 = perfect function, 100 = terrible) — compared to **35 for CMC joint
arthritis, 40 for carpal tunnel syndrome**, and by contrast just **5 for a simple ganglion.** His point:
**"it's not something that makes it terrible... not a life-threatening disease"** for most who have it, and
the threshold for surgical indication will naturally vary by country and healthcare access — poorer
countries with less healthcare infrastructure would be expected to have a higher practical treatment
threshold.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **A four-fold right-versus-left hand asymmetry in men, with no such asymmetry in women** `[04:01]`.
- **Diabetes strongly co-occurring with Dupuytren's**, with prevalence reaching 60–70% in a proven-diabetic
  clinic population `[07:03]`.
- **Low measured disability (DASH ~10–12) among patients actually on a surgical waiting list**, well below
  several other common hand conditions, contextualizing how mild most treated disease actually is `[09:04]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Finsen repeatedly and explicitly flags the study's weaknesses: the street-sampling method is called
imperfect but the best available option; small subgroup sample sizes are called out specifically where they
appear (elderly age brackets, some ethnic comparisons); and the ethnic prevalence difference is explicitly
labeled as possibly spurious given its failure to replicate in women. He treats the diabetes association and
the prevalence-versus-treatment-need gap as the study's most solid, reproducible findings, while presenting
the rest with appropriate caution.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly corroborates and extends `4qSMSqYhxOc`** (Wach's Dupuytren Society talk), which separately
challenges the "Viking disease" framing using case data from Japan, Spain, and Norway — this talk supplies
hard Bosnian prevalence numbers (34% of men, 16% of women affected in some form) as concrete evidence against
the disease being exclusively or even predominantly a Northern European phenomenon. **Consistent with
`-jLfWXX3YUM`** (Seven Questions), which separately reports favorable natural-history odds (most nodules
never progress to needing treatment) — this talk's DASH-score and prevalence-versus-operation-rate data
supply the disability-severity evidence behind that same favorable overall picture. **Relevant to any note
discussing diabetes as a Dupuytren's risk factor** — this talk's quantified odds ratios (~2.75–2.79) and
60–70% clinic prevalence are among the most concrete diabetes-association figures in this library.
