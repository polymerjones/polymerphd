# A fifth-year medical student (name garbled in captions), "Percutaneous Needle Fasciotomy for Repeat Recurrences" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Retrospectively tracking patients through repeated recurrences at UMCG, this study found percutaneous
needle fasciotomy (PNF) remained effective even on a third or fourth repeat treatment — with results comparable
to a primary PNF in some cases — while a fifth treatment was notably less successful, and each successful PNF
postponed more aggressive surgery by an average of 4.5 years.** The presenter states this directly: **"the
effectiveness of PNF for secondary and tertiary recurrences was high — so high that, when you compare it to
the literature, even a fourth PNF could have similar results as a primary PNF"** `[04:05]`.

## The motivating question

`[00:00]`–`[01:00]` — PNF is already established as effective for primary contractures and first recurrences,
but some argue against it as a viable long-term strategy given recurrence rates reported as high as **85% at
five years.** What remained unknown: does **repeating** PNF on successive recurrences remain worthwhile — can
it meaningfully **postpone or avoid** limited fasciectomy or another more aggressive treatment? The stated
research question: is PNF an effective treatment option for **secondary, tertiary, and quaternary**
recurrences?

## Study design

`[01:00]`–`[02:03]` — A **retrospective medical-record study** at UMCG (University Medical Center Groningen),
covering patients treated **2007–2014**, identified via PNF procedure codes. Inclusion required a **third,
fourth, or fifth PNF treatment on the same ray.** Primary outcome: reduction in **total passive extension
deficit (TPED)** following each successive treatment.

## The recurrence cascade

`[01:00]`–`[02:03]` — Of **172 patients** treated with PNF for a **primary** contracture, a substantial group
returned with a **primary recurrence**; among those choosing repeat treatment, **39 chose another PNF while 18
chose limited fasciectomy.** Of the patients who chose repeat PNF, **roughly 41% went on to develop a
secondary recurrence** — of those, most chose PNF again rather than surgery. Of that group, **roughly 45%
developed a tertiary recurrence**, again mostly opting for repeat PNF over surgery. By the time patients
reached a **fourth PNF treatment, all of those who had chosen repeat PNF returned with a further recurrence**
(100% in this small remaining subgroup) — most of these patients opted for a **fifth PNF treatment**, with a
smaller number choosing limited fasciectomy at that point instead.

## Effectiveness by treatment number

`[02:03]`–`[03:04]` — Across the second through fifth treatments, TPED reduction was tracked in degrees
(median and range) and as a percentage improvement. **Success rates for the third and fourth treatments were
strong — roughly 70%, 73%, and 63%** across these successive rounds — while the **fifth treatment was
notably less successful.** A **trend toward lower effectiveness at the PIP joint compared to the MCP joint**
was observed, consistent with joint-specific patterns reported elsewhere in this library, but the presenter is
explicit the **subgroup sizes were too small to establish statistical significance** for this difference.

## Delaying more aggressive surgery

`[03:04]` — Across the cohort, successful repeat PNF treatment **postponed more aggressive treatment (limited
fasciectomy) by an average of 4.5 years** — offered as a concrete, practically meaningful benefit of the
repeat-PNF strategy even where recurrence itself was not prevented.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Quaternary recurrence** — a fourth successive recurrence of Dupuytren's contracture on the same ray,
  described in this study as very rare, which limited the size of the study's most advanced subgroups
  `[04:05]`.

## Source-stated confidence

The presenter, a fifth-year medical student, is candid about the study's core limitation — its **retrospective
nature**, which produced data of admittedly limited quality — and separately notes that **quaternary
recurrence proved rare enough** in this population that the most advanced treatment subgroups were very small,
undermining confidence in results at that end of the cascade. The joint-specific (MCP vs. PIP) trend is
explicitly flagged as underpowered rather than presented as a confirmed finding. The talk is framed as, to the
presenter's knowledge, **the first study of its kind**, and as covering the largest available time span at this
single institution rather than claiming broader generalizability.
