# Anatomy Cords (Dr. Charles Eaton, 2017 ASSH Annual Meeting)

## Central claim

**The palmar fascia is not a single structure, and treating it as one obscures why Dupuytren's produces such
varied contracture patterns — the hand's fibrous scaffolding is actually several distinct, layered sheets and
bands, and different cords form from different named structures with different clinical behavior.** Eaton's
framing: **"the palmar fascia is often thought of as the single structure involved with Dupuytren's
contracture, but that's an oversimplification"** — it is one part of **"soft tissue scaffolding which
extends from the skin down to the hand skeleton," quilted together "like the batting of a quilt"** `[00:00]`.

## The layered scaffold, from superficial to deep

`[01:02]`–`[04:09]` — Working from skin toward bone:

- **Superficial layer (transverse fibers)**: the distal first webspace ligament, continuing as the
  **natatory ligament** around the palm's perimeter.
- **Next layer (longitudinal fibers)**: the **palmar fascia proper**, splitting in the mid-palm into
  **pretendinous bands**, which split again at the metacarpal neck level into **spiral bands** — these dive
  deep to the neurovascular bundle on each side of the finger and end near the origin of **Cleland's
  ligaments**.
- **Next layer**: the proximal first webspace ligament, continuing as the **superficial transverse palmar
  ligament**, and, out in the finger (deep to the neurovascular bundle), **Cleland's ligament** — a
  well-defined, stout sheet. Palmar to that runs a looser, less discrete band of oblique fibers collectively
  called **Grayson's ligament**.

Further out in the finger, a **"box" architecture** appears: the transverse metacarpal ligament forms the
floor, the **septa of Legueu and Juvara** (functioning much like lumbrical fascia) form the walls, and the
superficial transverse palmar ligament forms the roof.

## Fat pads, and what patients mistake them for

`[04:09]`–`[05:09]` — Between the rays sit the **palmar comissural pads** — normal fat pads in front of the
neurovascular bundle, most prominent when fingers are abducted. Eaton notes a specific, clinically useful
point: as Dupuytren's causes adjacent pretendinous/spiral tissue to retract, **these normal fat pads become
more prominent by contrast — and "patients think that these are fluid collections or nodules," when they are
actually normal anatomy.**

## Four cord types, and why they matter clinically

`[09:15]`–`[10:15]` — Eaton simplifies finger-level cords into four types, distinguished by clinical
behavior, not just anatomic origin:

| Cord type | Frequency | Bowstrings? | Relation to neurovascular bundle | Clinical note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Central** | Most common | Yes | Doesn't displace it | Easiest to feel and diagnose |
| **Retrovascular** | Fairly common | No | Lies deep to it | **May explain residual contracting force despite no palpable cord** |
| **Lateral** | Less common | No | Runs near it | Found at the base of the middle phalanx |
| **Spiral** | Riskiest | Yes | Displaces it into the surgical field | Can appear central or lateral, in palm or digit |

The retrovascular cord's clinical significance is worth isolating: because it produces contracting force
**without bowstringing and without a palpable cord**, it can explain cases where a finger remains contracted
despite no obvious cord being felt on exam.

## Joint-specific patterns, and why PIP is harder than MCP

`[11:16]`–`[12:17]` — Data from **2,300 previously untreated hands**, plotted as a heat map, shows nodules
clustering **midline/central** (matching high-grip-contact areas) while **cords trend more lateral**,
following underlying fascial anatomy. Critically, the two main joints behave differently:

- **MCP joints**: **primarily affected by central cords** — a relatively simple, dominant pattern.
- **PIP joints**: **no single dominant cord type** — central cords are most common but not a majority;
  retrovascular cords are fairly common; lateral and spiral cords occur too, plus **accessory collateral
  ligament contracture as a separate, non-cord contracting force.** Eaton states directly: **"the greater
  the degree of contracture, the more likely there are multiple structures involved"** — specifically,
  accessory collateral ligament and retrovascular cord involvement become more common once PIP contracture
  exceeds **45 degrees.**

## Why correction fails — two different failure modes

`[12:17]`–`[13:19]` — Eaton separates two distinct problems, useful for interpreting a poor surgical outcome:

1. **Incomplete correction (can't get the finger straight during the procedure itself)**: caused by the
   **palmar plate shortening** with prolonged PIP flexion, the **accessory collateral ligament shrinking**
   longitudinally, and the **flexor tendon sheath** persisting as a restrictive force — "particularly after
   fasciotomy."
2. **Early loss of correction (finger was straightened, then drifts back during recovery)**: caused by
   changes on the *extensor* side that don't block the initial correction but undermine holding it — the
   **central extensor slip stretching out**, losing the ability to fully extend the joint, and the
   **lateral band shrinking or subluxating palmarward**, which may not block passive extension but limits
   *active* extension during the first months of recovery.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Prominent fat pads between fingers mistaken by patients for new nodules or fluid collections**, actually
  normal anatomy made more visible by adjacent tissue retraction `[04:09]`.
- **Residual contracting force in a finger with no palpable cord**, potentially explained by a retrovascular
  cord lying too deep to feel `[09:15]`.
- **A finger that straightens during surgery but drifts back over the following months**, distinguished
  explicitly from a finger that never fully straightens in the first place `[12:17]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Retrovascular cord** — a cord lying deep to the neurovascular bundle, producing contracture without
  bowstringing or an easily palpable presence `[09:15]`.
- **Palmar comissural pads** — normal interdigital fat pads, often mistaken for pathology once adjacent
  tissue retracts `[05:09]`.
- **Septa of Legueu and Juvara** — fascial walls forming part of the "box" architecture between the
  transverse metacarpal ligament and superficial transverse palmar ligament `[04:09]`.
- **Incomplete correction vs. early loss of correction** — Eaton's explicit distinction between two separate
  surgical failure mechanisms with different anatomical causes `[12:17]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The palmar fascia scaffold as a quilt** — sheets of tissue "quilted together by thousands of individual
  threads passing through a cushion of fat" `[00:00]`.
- **Drawing the anatomy yourself, "like the brachial plexus"** — Eaton's own closing recommendation for
  retaining this anatomy if you don't operate on it daily, calling it "a humbling experience" `[13:19]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Presented as a structured anatomy review for a surgical specialty society, not a research talk — claims are
descriptive of known anatomy rather than hedged findings. The heat-map data (2,300 hands; a separate 70-patient
Doppler-location dataset) is cited by sample size, giving the anatomical generalizations a stated empirical
basis rather than presenting them as purely theoretical.
