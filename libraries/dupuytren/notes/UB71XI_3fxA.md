# Janine Morris PhD, "Collagen Degradation and MMP/TIMP Expression in Dupuytren's-Mediated Contraction" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Dupuytren's tissue doesn't just make more collagen — it also under-degrades it, because the balance
between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their tissue inhibitors (TIMPs) is tipped toward inhibition —
and Morris's own data suggests one specific enzyme, MMP-14, may evade that inhibition and drive contraction
directly.** Her framing of the normal-versus-diseased balance: **"in normal tissue... TIMPs [and] MMPs [are]
perfectly balanced... in fibrotic disorders such as Dupuytren's disease, the balance has been kind of tipped
in favor of our TIMPs"** — meaning MMPs are suppressed and **"more collagen is laid down"** `[03:05]`–`[04:06]`.

## The enzyme families involved

`[02:03]`–`[03:05]` — Of **23 human MMPs**, the collagen-degrading subset relevant here is **MMP-1, 8, 13,
and 14**; these are regulated by **four TIMPs**, which bind them with high affinity — **with the specific
exception of MMP-14**, flagged as behaving differently (a point that becomes central later). A separate
family, **ADAMTS (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase domain with thrombospondin motifs, 19 members)**,
processes procollagen peptides; **ADAMTS-2, -3, and -14** are the ones relevant to collagen synthesis.

## A genuine paradox in the literature

`[04:06]`–`[05:07]` — In terminal gastric carcinoma patients given broad-spectrum MMP inhibitors as cancer
treatment, an unexpected clinical side effect emerged: **frozen-shoulder-like and tendinitis-like
symptoms**, which **resolved when the inhibitors were stopped** — suggestive of MMPs playing a protective
role against fibrotic-type symptoms clinically. But taken back into the lab, **the same class of inhibitor,
given directly to myofibroblasts, actually reduced their contraction** — the opposite of what the clinical
observation would predict. Morris names this directly as **"a bit of a paradox"** rather than resolving it.

## Prior expression-profiling evidence

`[05:07]`–`[07:09]` — A 2007 study (credited to a group led by "Philip Johnson," not fully legible in the
transcript) built a complete MMP expression profile comparing Dupuytren's nodular tissue to normal and
non-nodular control tissue, finding **MMP-1, -13, and -14 all upregulated in nodular tissue**, alongside
**TIMP-1 also upregulated** — and went further by correlating this expression data with **post-surgical
recurrence**, raising the possibility that **gene expression itself could predict clinical outcome.**

## Her own experiments

`[07:09]`–`[11:13]` — Using **fibroblast-populated collagen lattices (FPCLs)** from Dupuytren's primary cell
lines as a contraction surrogate, Morris measured **collagen degradation directly via a hydroxyproline
assay** (hydroxyproline being a collagen-specific amino acid marker) across three conditions: cells in
open monolayer (baseline control), cells under tension in the gel, and cells after release from tension.
Findings: **collagen degradation increased during tension, and increased further after release** —
proposing that **"collagen contraction and collagen degradation go hand in hand."** Gene expression tracked
the same pattern: **MMP-1, MMP-13, and TIMP-1 all peaked after release** from tension (MMP-13 showed more of
a general increase, which she attributes to the cells' response to being in a 3D matrix at all). The most
striking finding was the **scale of TIMP-1's response — gene expression units around 500, compared to
roughly 10–14 for the MMPs measured alongside it** — a concrete, quantified illustration of exactly the
TIMP-dominant imbalance described earlier. **Casein zymography** (a method for visualizing active, not just
expressed, enzyme) confirmed the pattern for MMP-3 specifically: **little active enzyme under tension,
markedly more after release**, consistent with the gene expression data. A separate look at **MMP-14 and
MMP-2** showed a less clean pattern by timing, but **notably high absolute expression levels** for both
compared to the other MMPs studied.

## The proposed resolution: MMP-14 as the real driver

`[11:13]`–`[12:15]` — Since collagen degradation was clearly still occurring (confirmed by the hydroxyproline
assay) despite the heavy TIMP-1 presence that should suppress most MMPs, Morris proposes **MMP-14
specifically is responsible**, because **it is comparatively resistant to TIMP-1 inhibition and can
therefore "maintain its [proteolytic] activity"** even in a TIMP-1-saturated environment. She adds a second
mechanistic layer: **MMP-14 also activates MMP-2** — proposing the two act as **"partners in crime,"**
jointly responsible for both degrading collagen and enabling contraction.

## Ongoing work at the time of the talk

`[12:15]`–`[13:17]` — Morris describes having just performed an **siRNA knockdown of MMP-14** in Dupuytren's-
derived myofibroblasts the week before the talk, with plans to test the knocked-down cells in the FPCL
contraction assay against unmodified controls, and to re-run the hydroxyproline degradation assay on the
same cells — explicitly framed as ongoing, unfinished work aimed at identifying **a potential drug target.**

## Glossary terms introduced

- **TIMP (tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase)** — a protein family regulating MMP activity; TIMP-1's
  dramatically elevated expression relative to MMPs is central to this talk's proposed imbalance mechanism
  `[02:03]`.
- **MMP-14 (MT1-MMP)** — proposed here as the key TIMP-1-resistant collagenase actually responsible for
  ongoing degradation in Dupuytren's tissue, and as an activator of MMP-2 `[12:15]`.
- **Casein zymography** — a technique used to visualize active enzyme (as opposed to gene expression alone)
  by its ability to digest a casein substrate `[10:12]`.
- **Hydroxyproline assay** — a biochemical method measuring collagen degradation via a collagen-specific
  amino acid marker `[08:10]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **MMP-14 and MMP-2 as "partners in crime"** — a memorable framing for their proposed joint role in
  degradation and contraction `[12:15]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Morris is candid about unresolved tension in the evidence: she names the clinical-versus-laboratory MMP
inhibitor finding explicitly as **"a bit of a paradox"** without resolving it, and frames her MMP-14
hypothesis as a proposed explanation ("we think") rather than a settled conclusion, backed by ongoing
knockdown experiments she had only just begun at the time of the talk. The 2007 expression-profiling study's
recurrence-prediction angle is presented as a possibility ("maybe gene expression could be used to predict
the clinical outcome"), not a validated tool.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly extends `Fj5WfRonTOg`** (Hart's collagen biology primer), which describes the same MMP/TIMP
remodeling-imbalance hypothesis in general terms (citing MMP3 downregulation and TIMP1 upregulation from
gene-expression studies) — this talk supplies Morris's own quantified experimental data for exactly that
imbalance, plus the novel MMP-14-as-key-driver hypothesis Hart's talk doesn't cover. **Extends
`QRUnLzXLyjE`** (Nanchahal's active-contraction debate talk), which also describes high matrix turnover
(simultaneous synthesis and MMP-driven degradation) during active contraction — this talk's tension/release
degradation kinetics data is a direct experimental demonstration of that same claim. **Relevant to
`dMW6VVm4FhU`** (Robson's lab models talk) — both use FPCL contraction assays as a core methodology,
though Robson's focus is TGF-beta signaling and apoptosis while this talk focuses on the MMP/TIMP
degradation machinery specifically.
