# Dr. David Warwick "Debate: Collagenase Over PNF" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Warwick argues collagenase's biggest practical advantage isn't outcome data — it's how much easier it is
to learn and use safely, while candidly admitting he doesn't actually know whether it produces less
recurrence than needle fasciotomy.** His framing of the learning-curve gap: **"giving Xiaflex is actually
very easy... there's no learning curve to it... whereas needle fasciotomy... is actually quite a difficult
technique to learn — it takes years of confident use before you get good at it."** On recurrence, by
contrast, he's direct: **"is there less recurrence? I don't know, actually... we're comparing apples with
oranges here"** `[06:09]`.

## The learning curve argument

`[00:00]`–`[03:03]` — Warwick states collagenase's only real technical danger is going too deep and
puncturing the flexor tendon — otherwise, **"you find the needle, you find the cord, you put it in."** Needle
fasciotomy, by contrast, he frames as genuinely difficult, requiring years of practice — evidenced, he
argues, by how few surgeons actually perform it compared to how many have adopted collagenase, a pattern he
attributes partly to marketing but also to the real difference in how quickly each technique can be taught
and safely deployed. He describes the two approaches with a memorable, self-deprecating theatrical
comparison: collagenase as **"elegant... smooth,"** versus needle fasciotomy as his debate opponent
**"sweating"** over what he calls **"his hydraulic jackhammer."**

## An unexpected pharmacodynamic finding

`[01:01]` — Citing the pan-European **POINT-X study** (published early 2014), Warwick notes something
genuinely puzzling: **collagenase's own drug half-life is only a couple of hours, yet clinical improvement
continues well beyond that window** — suggesting, in his words, **"some different [biological] change going
on"** beyond simple direct enzymatic action, not yet explained.

## His own outcome and satisfaction data

`[01:01]`–`[04:04]` — From his own recent patient series: **94% full correction at the MCP joint, 72% at the
PIP joint**, using an extended indication covering complex, multi-joint, and natatory cords. Patient
satisfaction tracked over a **four-year period**: roughly **70% satisfied overall**, though **satisfaction
fell over time as recurrence set in**, along with declining willingness to repeat the treatment as the
disease returned. Among his patients who had previously had surgery, **70% said they would choose the drug
again** if given the choice. He also notes a practical economic detail: **nearly half his patients were
self-funded, paying roughly €2,000 and traveling specifically to seek the treatment out.**

## A striking, unexplained observation about how far the effect spreads

`[04:04]`–`[05:05]` — Warwick describes being **"staggered"** by how much of the cord clears after injecting
only a small, localized area — and even more surprised by **bruising extending well beyond the injection
site, up the arm and even into the armpit or chest** in some patients. His interpretation: **"the drug isn't
just dissolving collagen — I think it must be provoking some other chemical reaction at the site of
injection"** with effects reaching further than the literal injection point, particularly noticeable at the
PIP joint, where he's seen collagenase achieve full straightening in cases where an hour of surgery **"never
goes straight."**

## Why both techniques may share the same recurrence problem

`[05:05]`–`[06:09]` — Warwick draws a specific histologic distinction: **needle fasciotomy removes neither
collagen nor cells; collagenase removes collagen but not the cells.** He proposes this is exactly why **both
he and his debate opponent see recurrence** — **the cells responsible for producing new disease tissue are
left behind either way**, regardless of which mechanical or enzymatic technique is used to clear the
existing cord.

## The cord-size ("twigs versus logs") argument, reprised

`[06:09]`–`[07:09]` — Restating the size-based case for technique selection: for **large, thick, broad
cords**, Warwick states plainly, **"I'm not sure I would [be able to] manage those with a needle... those
big fat thick ones"** — favoring collagenase there specifically. He also flags the **PIP joint as
particularly risky for needle work given nerve proximity**, unless the operator is expert-level, whereas he
considers collagenase **"dead safe"** if injected properly, away from the tendon, stating **"there's never
been a [significant] nerve injury"** with the drug. One explicit caution: **collagenase used under a
previous skin graft is "a bit dangerous."** He also reports having performed **six operations after prior
failed collagenase treatment**, finding the tissue **"a little bit gummy and gooey"** but otherwise
straightforward to operate on.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Bruising extending well beyond the injection site — up the arm, into the armpit, or the chest** — noted
  as an unexpectedly wide-reaching side effect `[05:05]`.
- **Declining patient satisfaction and declining willingness to repeat treatment over a four-year period**,
  tracked as recurrence set in `[04:04]`.
- **Tissue described as "gummy and gooey" during surgery performed after a prior failed collagenase
  treatment** `[07:09]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **POINT-X study** — the pan-European collagenase study cited for the persistent-effect-despite-short-half-
  life finding `[01:01]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Collagenase as "elegant," needle fasciotomy as "a hydraulic jackhammer"** — Warwick's memorable, if
  one-sided, framing of the two techniques' relative technical demands `[02:02]`.
- **"Twigs versus logs"** — cord size as the deciding factor in technique suitability, echoed from his other
  talk in this library `[06:09]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Unusually candid for a debate-format talk: Warwick opens by acknowledging the format itself is "one-sided"
and that he's "been put up for this," explicitly states uncertainty about the central recurrence-rate
question ("I don't know, actually"), and flags his own bruising/spread observation as unexplained rather than
offering a confident mechanism. He also names a specific real risk (collagenase under a prior skin graft)
rather than presenting the drug as risk-free.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly paired with a needle-fasciotomy-favoring debate opponent** (referred to only as "Paul" in this
transcript, not confidently identifiable) — the two talks are staged as opposing positions and should be
read together where available. **Extends `93IWNYYqCjs`** (Warwick's own "what we may never know" talk, same
symposium) — this talk's own admitted uncertainty about relative recurrence rates is a live illustration of
exactly the methodological problem that other talk argues makes a valid comparative trial impossible.
**Directly relevant to `oRdE0UKeJF0`** (Van Rijssen's randomized PNF-vs-fasciectomy trial) — that trial's
85%-at-5-years PNF recurrence figure is far higher than Warwick's own MCP/PIP correction figures here would
suggest, though the two aren't measuring quite the same outcome (initial correction rate here versus
long-term recurrence there). **Corroborates `UB71XI_3fxA`** (Morris's MMP/TIMP talk) and **`dMW6VVm4FhU`**
(Robson's lab models talk) — Warwick's "cells are left behind" explanation for recurrence is consistent with
those talks' molecular findings that neither mechanical disruption nor collagenase removal eliminates the
cellular population actually driving the disease.
