# Dr. Joe Dias: "URAM as a PROM" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Comparing URAM directly against the PEM (see `-PUJvVwlJis`) in the same patient cohort, Dias found URAM
shows a clear ceiling effect and explains meaningfully less of patients' own sense of improvement — a
counterintuitive result, since disease-specific measures usually outperform generic ones — leading him to
recommend URAM never be used alone in a comparative study.** He states this directly: **"this is surprising —
[URAM is] worse than a generic hand questionnaire; usually it's the other way around, where if you've got a
disease-specific questionnaire, then it tends to be better than a generic [one], and here that is not
happening — therefore you'd be very cautious about studies that use URAM"** `[03:03]`.

## What URAM measures

`[00:00]`–`[01:01]` — Dias frames outcome measures in three tiers: **generic** (e.g. broad health measures),
**region-specific** (e.g. the DASH for the whole upper limb, or the PEM/Michigan Hand Questionnaire for the
hand specifically), and **disease-specific** — URAM being the disease-specific measure for Dupuytren's,
originating from a French research group and published in English. Dias notes a specific caveat about its
validation: he had assumed it was psychometrically validated for English use, but was told by David Ring that
this validation **had not actually been done in English.** URAM consists of **nine questions, all addressing
disability from flexion contracture** — eight about the digits generally, and one specifically about
**radial-sided (thumb) Dupuytren's disease.** Each item uses a **six-point interval scale with no true middle
option**, forcing patients to lean toward "good" or "bad" rather than a neutral response.

## Study design

`[01:01]`–`[02:02]` — Using the same cohort described in the companion PEM talk (`-PUJvVwlJis`): of **1,200
Dupuytren's patients**, 146 refused participation; of the remainder, **531 patients completed both the PEM
and URAM**, including both operated and non-operated patients. The same **transition question** ("cured" to
"terrible," split into better versus worse) was used to anchor comparisons.

## Deriving URAM's MCID, and a direct comparison to PEM

`[02:02]`–`[03:03]` — The published literature reports URAM's minimum clinically important difference (MCID)
as **2.9**; Dias's group, using a slightly different calculation method, arrived at a closely consistent
**2.6.** Comparing URAM and PEM scores directly against the transition-question grades of change: **PEM
continues rising as patient-reported improvement increases, while URAM flattens out** — a clear **ceiling
effect**, which Dias attributes to URAM having **no domain addressing treatment complications**, meaning it
cannot register the negative effect a complication has on a patient's overall outcome. A linear regression
against the same anchor (transition) question found **PEM explained 71% of the variance**, while **URAM
explained only 53%** — roughly 20 percentage points less explanatory than PEM.

## Do patients feel URAM reflects their experience?

`[03:03]`–`[04:03]` — Asking patients directly (455 responses) whether URAM reflected the problems they
actually experienced: **24% said no** — meaning URAM captures the lived experience of roughly three out of
four patients, but not all.

## Dias's recommendation

`[04:03]`–`[05:05]` — URAM does reflect finger contracture and related disability reasonably well, and **2.6
is the recommended minimum difference threshold** for studies using it comparatively. But Dias is explicit
that URAM **does not fully capture the state of the hand, particularly after intervention** — lacking domains
for pain, overall function, and general dysfunction. His practical recommendation: URAM should **never be used
alone** in a comparative clinical study, since studies relying on it risk failing to detect a real treatment
difference; it should instead be **combined with a generic or region-specific hand questionnaire** — the PEM,
the Michigan Hand Questionnaire, or the DASH (for whole-upper-limb studies).

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Disability specifically from finger flexion contracture**, the narrow domain URAM's nine questions are
  built around `[00:00]`.
- **Pain, general function, and overall hand dysfunction** — explicitly named as dimensions URAM does *not*
  capture, a gap Dias treats as its central limitation `[04:03]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **URAM** — a French-developed, Dupuytren's-disease-specific patient-reported outcome measure, nine questions
  addressing flexion-contracture-related disability `[00:00]`.
- **Ceiling effect** — a measurement pattern where a score stops increasing even as the underlying condition
  continues to improve, limiting the measure's ability to detect further change `[02:02]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Dias is direct about a specific credibility gap in URAM's evidence base — its published English-language
validation had not actually been done, a caveat he only learned secondhand from a colleague (David Ring) rather
than through his own literature review, and he reports this candidly rather than omitting it. He treats his own
finding (a disease-specific measure performing worse than a generic one) as genuinely surprising and contrary
to the usual pattern, rather than downplaying or explaining it away, and offers a specific, testable mechanistic
reason (the missing complications domain) for the ceiling effect he observed.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly extends `-PUJvVwlJis`** (Dias's own PEM MCID talk, same patient cohort and methodology) — that talk
establishes the PEM's 3-point MCID using the identical transition-question technique this talk uses to derive
URAM's 2.6-point MCID, and this talk's head-to-head comparison directly shows PEM outperforming URAM as an
outcome measure. **Relevant to `93IWNYYqCjs`** (Warwick's "what we may never know" talk) — Warwick specifically
names the lack of a validated, sensitive outcome measure as an obstacle to comparative Dupuytren's trials; this
talk's finding that even a purpose-built disease-specific measure (URAM) underperforms a more general
region-specific one (PEM) is a concrete, current illustration of exactly that problem. **Relevant to
`R4WPhcOpAMo`** (Selles's consensus recurrence definition talk) — that talk's consensus definition is built
purely around angular contracture measurement, the same narrow domain URAM covers well; this talk's finding
that contracture-focused measures miss pain, complications, and overall function is a relevant caution when
interpreting any recurrence figure based solely on an angular threshold.
