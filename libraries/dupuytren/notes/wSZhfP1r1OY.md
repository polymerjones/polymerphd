# Dr. Charles Eaton, "Needle Fasciotomy for Dupuytren's" 2010 IFSSH Presentation

## Central claim

**Drawing on his personal experience across over 8,000 hands, Eaton argues needle fasciotomy avoids the skin
dissection that provokes prolonged post-operative inflammation in open surgery, and that its central safety
principle is simple: if the fingertip stays sensate, the needle doesn't provoke paresthesia, and active
flexion doesn't catch the needle tip, no harm has been done.** He states the rationale for avoiding
dissection directly: **"the biology of Dupuytren's is provoked by flap elevation and by tension on the
wounds. The point of needle release is to avoid the skin dissection which results in prolonged post-operative
inflammation"** `[00:02]`.

## Why patients choose needle release over surgery

`[00:02]`–`[01:04]` — Eaton frames the two goals of Dupuytren's treatment as **straighter fingers** (achievable)
and **disease control** (not achievable, since "Dupuytren's isn't a surgical disease, it's a medical
condition which doesn't yet have a medicine"). Bigger operations, he argues, buy only modest gains in
remission length at the cost of longer recovery and a disproportionate rise in complications. He uses a
**prospect-theory analogy**: **"you have a room in your house with a light switch, but you know that about
one out of a hundred times if you touch that light switch you'll get a terrible shock — well, you're not
going to turn the light on"** — for most patients, a modest but salient risk outweighs a modest gain, and
because they're likely to know someone who had a difficult surgical recovery, they'll decline surgery but
still want treatment, making a lower-risk needle procedure the practical alternative `[01:04]`.

## Technical keys

`[01:04]`–`[02:06]` — **A 25-gauge needle** with a cutting edge half a millimeter wide and under a millimeter
long — smaller than a digital nerve — used **as a miniature scalpel, cutting with the edges, not the point**,
costing 11 cents, contrasted against the much larger 18-gauge needle, Luer fasciotome, or #11 blade used in
other variants. **No skin flaps** avoids the inflammatory trigger; **no deep anesthesia** means the patient
can report proximity to important structures directly, since **tension on the fascia bowstrings the cord up
and away from deeper structures**. Subcutaneous fasciotomies open lenticular defects that allow the finger to
straighten.

## The safety check, and when needle release is preferred

`[02:06]`–`[03:06]` — Eaton's stated safety rule: **"if the fingertip isn't numb, and if the needle doesn't
provoke paresthesias, and if active finger flexion doesn't catch the tip of the needle, you have done no
harm."** He identifies needle release as the most logical option specifically when a patient carries **medical
risk factors that make a long recovery unsafe**: anticoagulation (Coumadin, Plavix, Eliquis, or combinations —
none of which were deal-breakers in his practice) and **lymphedema**, which is problematic for open procedures
but not for needle release; he also notes **collagenase is contraindicated** in some of these same situations
where needle release remains viable. Range-of-motion improvement is described as similar to fasciectomy;
recurrence occurs earlier, but **repeat needle releases remain possible** as long as skin reserve and a
palpable cord remain.

## Technique and portal planning

`[03:06]`–`[05:07]` — Skin-wheal anesthesia, working **distal to proximal**, repeatedly checking nerve and
tendon status as portals are planned toward areas of greatest expected improvement — while **avoiding portals
where skin has no more give**, or risky spots like the base of a dimple. **Pre-operative Doppler** is used to
screen for spiral cords — aiming down to listen for pretendinous spiral cords, and tangentially for
prevascular spiral cords. Three needle maneuvers, summarized as **"clear, perforate, sweep"**: clear a path,
feel the cord's surface contour by gently probing, then divide it by gradually sweeping through the surface.
Skin signs guide portal placement: bunching, curved skin creases, and absence of blanching all indicate where
the fascia is tightest and release will have the most effect. Post-release, the fascial wound sites are not
tender; patients are cautioned against strenuous gripping for about a week, and Eaton notes **one hand can be
done one day, the other the next, with the patient able to golf within a week.**

## From the original technique to Eaton's current version

`[05:07]`–`[08:10]` — Eaton credits the **original technique to Dr. Lermusiaux in Paris**: a 25-gauge needle
on a syringe of anesthetic-plus-steroid mixture, injecting the cord and cutting through one or two levels
before a firm pull. His own current modification uses **intradermal anesthetic and more portals**, illustrated
in a full case: a patient with four affected fingers, cords across the palm and proximal fingers (lateral,
central, oblique, natatory), MCP and PIP contracture at multiple joints — walked through from intradermal
anesthetic (aided by a vibration device to reduce injection pain), sensation checks, locating the cord by
feel, gradual weakening and pulling through, continuous sensation and tendon-excursion checks, and separately
addressing **natatory cords** (described as relatively easy and safe to access between neurovascular bundles,
aided by a scissoring motion) and an **oblique cord**. Eaton notes deliberately limiting palm work when
multiple fingers are involved, since numbing one area can prevent safe work on an adjacent, not-yet-treated
finger. Final result: fingertip sensation intact, MCP joints hyperextending, and good active PIP extension
across all treated fingers — achieved with the patient awake throughout and only surface-level anesthesia.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Fingertip numbness, needle-provoked paresthesia, or active flexion catching the needle** — the three
  specific real-time signals Eaton names as his intraoperative "do no harm" check `[02:06]`.
- **Skin bunching, curved skin creases, and absence of blanching** — visual/tactile signs used to identify
  where fascial tension is greatest and portal placement will be most effective `[04:06]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **"Clear, perforate, sweep"** — Eaton's three-step needle maneuver sequence for dividing a cord `[04:06]`.
- **Spiral cord Doppler screening** — pre-operative ultrasound Doppler used to locate pretendinous and
  prevascular spiral cords before needle placement `[04:06]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The light-switch/prospect-theory analogy** — for why patients weigh a modest, salient risk (a "shock")
  more heavily than a modest, uncertain gain, explaining why patients who know someone with a difficult
  surgical outcome will avoid surgery but still seek lower-risk treatment `[01:04]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Eaton grounds the talk explicitly in personal technique experience across "over 8,000 hands" rather than
formal trial data, and is direct about what needle release cannot do — disease control, as opposed to
achieving straighter fingers. He credits Lermusiaux's original technique by name and explicitly frames his own
version as a modification of it, rather than presenting it as a novel invention.
