# Dr. Charles Eaton "Debate: Passive More than Active Contraction" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Dupuytren's contracture is not the tissue actively pulling itself shut the way a wound does — it is the
resting posture of the hand, combined with the myofibroblast's weaker "periodic" contraction, passively
locking in whatever slack already exists in the tissue.** Eaton states the distinction precisely at the
outset: **"active contraction takes a finger which is straight and pulls it into a bent position... exactly
the same way that wound contracture takes an open wound and pulls it shut, whereas passive contraction is
the process by which... tissues are remodeled so that the fingers stay that way"** `[00:00]`.

## Three clinical observations against the active model

`[00:00]`–`[02:02]`:

1. **Location specificity.** Dupuytren's nodules form in three places — the palm, the dorsum of the
   fingers, and the plantar fascia (Ledderhose) — with **"the same histology, the same myofibroblasts, the
   same biology,"** yet **only the palm regularly develops contracture.** Eaton attributes this to resting
   mechanics, not biology: **"the palm is the only area where the tissues rest in relative laxity"** — the
   finger dorsum and plantar fascia instead rest under relative tension (toes resting in MTP
   hyperextension) `[00:00]`–`[01:02]`.
2. **Timing.** Active wound-contracture myofibroblasts close tissue at roughly **a centimeter a month** —
   at that rate, a nodule should become a fully bent finger in under a year. **"That is very rare,"** which
   Eaton takes as evidence Dupuytren's cellular mechanics must differ fundamentally from ordinary active
   wound contraction `[01:02]`.
3. **Endpoint.** Unchecked active contraction should progress until fingertips dig fully into the palm.
   Eaton concedes this does happen, but **only when something additional is present** — undiagnosed
   spasticity, scar tissue from prior injury or surgery, or (in boutonnière deformity) cortical
   reorganization letting patients actively flex a finger out of the way as a "functional amputation."
   Passively relaxing such a hand lifts the fingertips off the palm. **"The contracture follows the
   posture, not vice versa"** `[02:02]`.

## The mechanistic argument: periodic versus isometric contraction

`[02:02]`–`[04:05]` — Eaton distinguishes two myofibroblast contraction modes, both operating through
cell-matrix focal adhesions linking extracellular collagen to intracellular contractile stress fibers:

| Type | Strength | Regulation | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Periodic** | Weak — cannot actively deform the matrix | Calcium biology; cycles roughly every 100 seconds | Only takes up existing slack in already-lax collagen |
| **Isometric** | ~1,000× stronger than periodic | Rho-kinase pathway | Actively deforms and holds the matrix — the mechanism of true wound contraction |

In the **active model** (ordinary wound healing), isometric contraction creates slack around the cell, which
extracellular cross-linking enzymes then trim, ratcheting the wound progressively shut. In Eaton's
**passive model**, the first step is matrix laxity from resting posture itself — not cell-driven
deformation — and the periodic contraction merely takes up that pre-existing slack as cross-linking enzymes
trim it, with no isometric deformation required. **"Dupuytren's myofibroblasts are unique in that they
primarily reduce collagen slack. If the resting posture produces collagen slack, as it does in the palm,
then the deformity follows the resting posture"** `[04:05]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Contracture occurring only in the palm, not the finger dorsum or sole, despite nodules appearing in
  all three sites** `[00:00]`–`[01:02]`.
- **Slow, gradual progression from nodule to bent finger, typically over years rather than the roughly
  one-year timeline active contraction would predict** `[01:02]`.
- **Fingertips digging fully into the palm — flagged as a sign of an additional process** (undiagnosed
  spasticity, prior scarring, or boutonnière-related cortical reorganization) **rather than Dupuytren's
  contracture alone** `[02:02]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Periodic contraction** — weak, calcium-regulated myofibroblast contraction that takes up existing
  matrix slack but cannot actively deform matrix `[03:03]`.
- **Isometric contraction** — strong, Rho-kinase-pathway myofibroblast contraction capable of actively
  deforming and holding matrix, characteristic of ordinary wound contracture `[03:03]`.
- **Passive contraction model** — contracture driven by resting tissue laxity plus slack-trimming, rather
  than active cell-driven shortening `[00:00]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"The contracture follows the posture, not vice versa"** `[02:02]`.

## Source-stated confidence

This is explicitly a debate-format talk arguing one side of a stated disagreement, opening with direct
acknowledgment of the opposing position ("we're just really saying... the same thing, except that you're
wrong"). Eaton frames his argument as resting on three specific clinical observations he considers decisive,
not on new experimental data of his own.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly opposes `QRUnLzXLyjE`** (Dr. Jagdeep Nanchahal, "Debate: Contracture in Dupuytren is Active,"
same symposium) — staged as the paired opposing argument and best read together. The disagreement is
substantive, not just rhetorical: Nanchahal's talk argues myofibroblast cell-to-cell coupling and a
TGF-beta activation feedback loop actively drive ongoing contraction; this talk argues the same cell type
mostly performs weak, slack-trimming periodic contraction, with the palm's resting laxity — not active
cellular force — determining where and how much contracture develops. **Both talks agree on the underlying
cell biology** (alpha-smooth-muscle-actin-expressing myofibroblasts, focal/cell-matrix adhesions) and
disagree specifically on which contraction mode (isometric versus periodic) dominates and how much
explanatory weight resting tissue mechanics carries.
