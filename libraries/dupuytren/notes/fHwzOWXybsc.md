# Dr. Paul Binhammer: "Steroid Injection and Needle Aponeurotomy" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a long-term follow-up (7–53 months) of a randomized trial, adding a steroid injection series to needle
aponeurotomy produced significantly better total extension deficit than needle aponeurotomy alone through 24
months — but that advantage disappeared beyond 24 months, and while overall retreatment rates did not
significantly differ between groups, the non-steroid group sought retreatment measurably sooner.** Binhammer
states the conclusion directly: **"our null hypothesis was rejected, and for the short term — at least up to
24 months — there was a statistical difference with steroid-treated NA patients versus non-steroid-treated"**
`[05:14]`.

## Background: the original trial

`[00:00]`–`[01:01]` — This talk reports long-term follow-up of a previously published randomized controlled
trial comparing **needle aponeurotomy (NA) combined with a triamcinolone (steroid) injection series** against
**NA alone.** Binhammer is candid that the original trial, while randomized, was **not blinded** — he
performed the measurements himself and knew each patient's group assignment — and used **no stratification**,
enrolling all comers. The original 6-month result: **steroid + NA produced significantly greater improvement
in motion than NA alone.** This follow-up study's hypothesis: steroid + NA patients would show **less joint
contracture and a longer delay to retreatment** than NA alone; the stated null hypothesis was that retreatment
rate and contracture severity **would not differ** between groups.

## Follow-up design

`[01:01]`–`[02:03]` — Unlike the original trial's fixed follow-up schedule, this study allowed patients to
return **up to 18 months** on their own initiative; those who hadn't returned by then were contacted directly
and had their extension deficit measured. **Retreatment itself was patient-driven** — patients could choose
fasciotomy or repeat needle aponeurotomy (collagenase was not yet available at the time of this study), and
study participation ended once a patient underwent a second treatment. Primary outcomes: retreatment and
**total active extension deficit (TED).** **44 of the original 47 patients** were followed, over a range of
**7 to 53 months** (the short end reflecting one patient who proceeded to secondary treatment at 7 months).

## TED results over time

`[03:07]` — Consistent with the original 6-month report, **mean TED remained significantly higher in the
NA-alone group than the steroid group through 24 months.** **Beyond 24 months, this difference was no longer
statistically significant** — the steroid-added benefit, real in the short-to-medium term, did not persist
indefinitely.

## Retreatment results

`[03:07]`–`[05:14]` — Comparing overall retreatment rates: **no statistically significant difference** was
found between groups, though the p-value was **0.07** — close to, but not crossing, the conventional
significance threshold. A **Kaplan-Meier analysis** of retreatment as a percentage of subjects over time
showed the two curves converging by around **48 months.** However, a **log-rank test** found the **non-steroid
(NA-alone) group sought retreatment sooner** over the observed time period — a difference in *timing* not
fully captured by the simple overall-rate comparison. Additional predictors of retreatment, independent of
steroid use: patients whose **initial treatment involved two or more joints** were more likely to need
retreatment, and **younger patients** were more likely to need retreatment. A **Cox proportional hazards
regression** confirmed **baseline age and number of joints treated** as significant predictors.

## Source-stated confidence

Binhammer is unusually direct about the original trial's methodological weaknesses — explicitly acknowledging
it was **unblinded, with the same investigator performing measurements while aware of group assignment**, and
**not stratified.** He is candid that this follow-up study's design (patient-driven return and retreatment
timing) is itself a limitation, since sample size naturally erodes over a long observation period as in any
longitudinal study, and patients self-select when to report back. He reports the borderline p=0.07 retreatment
result plainly rather than rounding it into either a positive or negative finding, and lets the more nuanced
Kaplan-Meier/log-rank timing result stand alongside it rather than picking one number to lead with.
