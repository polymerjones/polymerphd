# Dr. Marie A. Badalamente "Clinical Research" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Speaking from direct experience developing collagenase from bench to bedside, Badalamente argues that
under-powered small studies don't actually change clinical practice, and that successful clinical research
requires a deliberately built team, a sequential funding strategy, and non-negotiable independent safety
monitoring.** She frames the underpowered-study problem bluntly: **"when you see something written about
maybe 10 or 15 patients and the conclusion of the paper says, well, there was a trend but no statistical
significance — how does that help you as clinicians? It doesn't. It may generate an idea for you here and
there, but is it going to change the way you practice? I don't think so"** `[03:05]`.

## Starting from skepticism

`[01:03]`–`[02:04]` — Badalamente recalls that when she and Dr. Hurst began the collagenase research program,
colleagues openly doubted them: **"you guys are absolutely crazy... you're just thinking too far outside of
the box, and you're going to fall off a ledge — and at worst, you're going to do this to some patient's
finger."** She frames the eventual success as the product of **hard work and persistence**, not a shortcut:
**"there's no elevator to success, you have to take the stairs."**

## Building the team

`[02:04]`–`[03:05]` — For national/international collaborative research, Badalamente identifies concrete
structural needs: **one or two principal investigators acting as leads, associate investigators, study
coordinators, and staff**, plus practical consideration of patient access to the study site, and clear
ownership of **who coalesces, analyzes, and verifies the data** — she calls this last point **"incredibly,
incredibly, incredibly important"** especially with large datasets.

## Safety monitoring

`[04:05]`, `[08:11]`–`[09:13]` — **"Safety is king,"** stated as the defining principle of running a trial.
In the collagenase development program, an independent safety monitor visited **every six weeks**, reviewing
paperwork and computer-generated data continuously — visits she describes as unpleasant but essential.
Concrete safety infrastructure she recommends: **pre-planned stopping rules** for adverse events (halting the
trial to review, potentially self-imposing a clinical hold before the FDA does), and **independent safety
review** — critically, **anyone with a financial conflict of interest cannot be the person performing the
safety review**, a structural separation meant to keep the data credible to regulators.

## Funding strategy

`[05:08]`–`[07:10]` — Badalamente describes a **sequential, small-to-large funding approach**: starting with
small NIH grant programs (she cites an example with ~$50,000 direct costs) and FDA pilot funding, then
building persistence with study section contacts (**"get on the phone, learn who the study section heads
are"**), incorporating modest industry funding, and eventually pursuing larger government and industry grant
programs once a dataset exists to point to. She notes candidly that **total NIH budget is only about a third
of total US R&D spending**, with pharmaceutical industry funding making up the larger remainder — meaning
credible, safety-conscious data is often what makes an idea fundable by those larger private sources.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Stopping rules** — pre-planned criteria for halting a clinical trial to review an adverse event before
  continuing `[08:11]`.
- **Clinical hold** — a formal FDA-imposed (or self-imposed, in anticipation) suspension of a trial `[09:13]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"There's no elevator to success, you have to take the stairs"** — for the sustained, incremental nature
  of translational research `[01:03]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Badalamente frames the entire talk explicitly as personal experience and practical advice ("a few ideas...
things to think about") rather than a formal research finding, and is candid that the early collagenase
research program faced real skepticism from peers before succeeding — she does not present the eventual
success as inevitable or obvious in hindsight.
