# Dr. Charles Eaton: "2023 Dupuytren Disease and Research for a Cure" — Full Lecture

## Central claim

**This is Eaton's most detailed public lecture on practical patient options: a comprehensive, risk-stratified
review of every current nodule treatment (from cortisone to radiotherapy to nutritional supplements), specific
splinting guidance including a named failure mode to avoid, and the fullest public account of the pilot
biomarker study's real scope, cost, and realistic funding timeline.** He frames the field's core error with a
vivid analogy: **"arguing about which procedure is the best procedure is kind of like arguing about which is
the best broom to keep water off the beach... it misses the fundamental question of what is the basic nature
of water"** `[68:19]`.

*Note: this talk shares extensive material with `LkOronhIgQU` (2022) — the Viking-myth debunking, the
evolutionary sickle-cell/Black Death argument, the two-hit model, the obesity paradox, natural history
statistics, PIP joint geometry, diathesis reconsidered, the CMC joint measurement problem, the treatment
ladder, and the "water on the floor"/surgical-versus-medical research distinction are covered there in
comparable or greater detail — see that note. This note focuses on what is new or substantially expanded here.*

## A hair-follicle finding relevant to skin grafting

`[25:31]`–`[26:32]` — Eaton notes palm skin, sole skin, and knuckle-pad skin all **lack hair follicles** — a
2015 study confirmed this specifically for knuckle-pad skin. When a **full-thickness skin graft** (which
typically does contain hair follicles) is used in dermofasciectomy, hair may grow from the grafted area — an
odd but potentially meaningful side effect, because hair follicles produce **bone morphogenic proteins
(BMPs)**, which are known to be capable of transforming **myofibroblasts** (Dupuytren's key abnormal cell) into
**fat cells** — a plausible, though unproven, biological explanation for why grafting affected skin may reduce
recurrence beyond simply providing a mechanical barrier.

## Which fingers and cord positions progress fastest

`[31:37]`–`[32:38]` — Extending the geometric explanation of why cords near a joint's pivot point cause more
angular change per unit of shortening (also covered in `LkOronhIgQU`), Eaton adds a specific mapping: cords
running up the **side** of a finger sit closer to the joint's pivot point than cords running up the
**middle**, so side-running cords are expected to progress faster. Combined with finger size, this predicts —
and matches observation — that the **pinky finger is the worst-behaving digit**, the **ring finger** next, and
the index/middle fingers roughly similar to each other.

## A comprehensive review of nodule treatment options

`[53:07]`–`[59:12]` — Eaton frames nodule-treatment decisions around a key distinction: patients with actual
**symptoms** (tenderness, burning, pain, interference with specific activities like steering or golf) can
evaluate treatment against a **measurable benefit**; patients motivated purely by **anxiety about future
progression** cannot, since **no treatment has proven preventive benefit** — making risk tolerance a much
harder calculation for that group. He reviews specific options, each with an explicit evidence/risk
assessment:

- **Cortisone injection** — an established option for symptomatic nodules, knuckle pads, or Ledderhose lesions.
- **Colchicine** (an old gout medication) — no formal trial data, but Eaton reports **anecdotal reports from
  multiple patients** describing symptom reduction and nodule shrinkage; he describes patients using it
  **as-needed** (stopping when symptoms resolve, restarting if they return) rather than continuously.
- **Humira injected directly into a nodule** — ongoing work in England; Humira (and related drugs ending in
  "-mab"/"-cept," used for rheumatoid arthritis) targets a factor with **some, though not major, involvement**
  in Dupuytren's biology; direct nodule injection can shrink it, but **no evidence yet on long-term outcome**,
  and the requirement to inject the **full dose into a single nodule** (not split across several) is a
  practical logistical limitation.
- **Radiotherapy** — gaining popularity in the US, long-established in Germany; uses roughly **one-third the
  dose given for cancer treatment**, killing rapidly-dividing nodule cells to shrink them, but **no evidence it
  prevents future contracture.** Real risks: roughly **a third of treated patients** get lasting **dry, flaky
  skin** from sweat-gland damage, and there is a **small but non-zero increased skin cancer risk** from the
  radiation exposure itself.
- **Shockwave therapy** — very early-stage evidence (a couple of patients) for palm nodules and Ledderhose
  disease; may reduce inflammation and size, but long-term effects are unknown.
- **Topical Voltaren** (an over-the-counter anti-inflammatory gel, same drug class as aspirin/Advil/Aleve) —
  practical because it's alcohol-based and dries rather than staying greasy on the hands; Eaton considers
  **absorption risk low** given the small treated area, and reports some patients getting good results.
- **Topical verapamil** (a cardiac medication, theoretically relevant via calcium metabolism) — no long-term
  evidence of benefit, and Eaton has not personally heard reports of good outcomes from patients using it.
- **Oral/nutritional supplements** — acetylcysteine (studied in other fibrotic diseases; can cause a sulfurous
  body odor at effective doses); sulforaphane (found in broccoli/cruciferous vegetables, also sold as a
  supplement; degraded by cooking heat; effective clinical dose unknown, making dietary extrapolation
  unreliable); caffeine (theoretically beneficial); magnesium (topical magnesium can irritate skin, but
  Dupuytren's patients are commonly magnesium-deficient anyway, so oral supplementation is reasonable
  regardless). Eaton is explicit **none of these has solid long-term clinical documentation** — only
  laboratory-level plausibility.

## Mechanical-stress-reduction advice, restated with more detail

`[62:15]`–`[64:16]` — Beyond the general advice given in `LkOronhIgQU` (padded gloves, pull-up handles, wider
sports-equipment grips, avoiding flat-palm stretch positions), Eaton adds: fingerless gloves with **silicone
rubber padding** to reduce shearing/impact force; **soft wrist weights** (noting most commercial ones are
uncomfortably hard and require deliberately shopping for softer padding) as an alternative to gripped dumbbells
during exercise.

## Detailed, practical splinting guidance

`[64:16]`–`[67:19]` — Eaton reviews the evidence directly: **well-designed studies have found no added benefit
to splinting after surgery** (consistent with `dp45M2IEW3k`), specifically because post-surgical tissue has
already been biologically activated by the trauma of the procedure itself. But for **mild, untreated
Dupuytren's**, the rationale differs: since resting fingers sit naturally flexed for hours overnight, splinting
may help by simply **reducing the time slack is allowed to be taken up** in the cord. His key practical
warning: a splint that's **too tight fails predictably** — patients confident in their own motivation
routinely over-tighten, wake in the night, remove it, repeat this pattern for a while, and then one night sleep
through and find the splint on the floor — **at that point, the patient has simply trained themselves to
remove the splint during sleep**, ending compliance entirely. His guidance: the splint should be **loose
enough to still be on the hand in the morning**, resting the hand rather than forcing it. He names one specific
product (Fixed Glove, disclosing no financial interest) using bendable struts that can be adjusted looser than
a rigid splint.

## The "broom on the beach" analogy

`[68:19]`–`[69:21]` — Eaton argues debating which surgical technique or which precise injection placement is
"best" misses the deeper problem: **Dupuytren's is fundamentally a biological disease, and no purely mechanical
technique, however refined, addresses that** — comparable to debating which broom sweeps water off a beach
most effectively, when the real issue is that the tide keeps coming back in regardless of broom choice.

## The pilot study's real scope, cost, and timeline

`[79:33]`–`[85:41]` — The **International Dupuytren Data Bank** registry had, at the time of this talk, **more
than 9,000 enrollees** (with ongoing attrition in follow-up survey completion). The **biomarker pilot study**,
launched roughly two years before this talk, runs **four types of gene-related tests** (DNA sequencing —
considered least useful since DNA doesn't change with treatment or age; whole-genome bisulfite sequencing, for
epigenetic changes; RNA; and small/micro/messenger RNA, which reflect real-time gene-to-gene signaling) plus
**three types of protein tests.** The gene analysis alone generated **14 terabytes of raw data from 45
patients**, requiring cloud computing infrastructure. Stated goals at the time: finish and publish the protein
analysis, then finish and publish the gene analysis, **hopefully by early the following year.** The **lab
budget alone was $400,000** — described by Eaton as small by pharmaceutical industry standards but a major sum
for the group to raise. He recounts the **NIH's own stated position**: they will not fund discovery research
without existing preliminary/pilot data — meaning the pilot study itself was a **necessary prerequisite**
before any NIH grant application could even be considered. The stated realistic NIH timeline: submit pilot
data, wait roughly **a year or two** for a funding decision, and even a successful grant (with only about a
**3% acceptance rate**) typically takes another **18 months** before funds actually arrive — putting substantial
additional research funding **roughly five years away in the best case.** Given this, Eaton states the group
cannot simply wait for NIH funding and must keep working with what it can independently raise.

## Source-stated confidence

Eaton is careful throughout the nodule-treatment review to grade each option's evidence level explicitly —
distinguishing established practice (cortisone) from anecdotal-only reports (colchicine) from early/limited
trial work (Humira, shockwave) from laboratory-plausible-but-clinically-undocumented supplements — rather than
presenting the list as uniformly validated. He is explicit the hair-follicle/BMP mechanism is a plausible
hypothesis, not a proven explanation. He reports the pilot study's real budget, data scale, and realistic
funding timeline candidly, including the roughly five-year best-case estimate before major NIH funding could
arrive, rather than presenting an optimistic timeline.
