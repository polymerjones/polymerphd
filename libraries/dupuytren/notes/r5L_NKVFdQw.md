# Bernhard Lukas Dr med, "Treatment of severe Dupuytren contracture" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a five-year, 50-patient series, primary use of homodigital and heterodigital transposition flaps for PIP
joint contracture over 30 degrees considerably improved medium-term results compared to older techniques, but
severity still mattered: contractures under 60 degrees typically achieved full intraoperative correction with
some loss (15–30°) by two years, while contractures at or above 90 degrees could not be fully corrected even
intraoperatively despite arthrolysis.** Lukas states the conclusion directly: **"primary implementation of
homo/heterodigital transposition flaps at [a PIP joint] contracture over 30 degrees improves the postoperative
result — at least in the medium term — considerably"** `[10:20]`.

## Treatment algorithm by joint and severity

`[00:03]`–`[02:06]` — Using **Tubiana staging (0–IV)** as the severity framework, Lukas describes the MCP
joint as comparatively straightforward ("not a big problem" in his assessment). For the **PIP joint**: 
**contracture under 30 degrees** is managed with **Z-plasty**; **contracture over 30 degrees** uses a
**dorsolateral transposition flap**. When both MCP and PIP joints are contracted, an **open palm technique** in
the palm is combined with a Z-plasty over the PIP joint.

## The flap technique, original and modified

`[02:06]`–`[03:07]` — The group's original approach used a Bruner incision to the PIP joint followed by an
ulnodorsal or lateral-dorsal transposition flap over the middle phalanx, closed with full-thickness skin
grafting. This was **modified to a rectangular, predominantly ulnar-based flap over the proximal phalanx**,
combined with a **homodigital flap over the middle phalanx** (mostly ulnar-sided), with the resulting defect
closed by full-thickness skin graft. A case example with 60 degrees of PIP contracture, transposed flap from
the lateral side and skin graft harvested from the groin ("tina") region, showed a good one-year result with
slight loosening of active extension but well-preserved passive extension.

## Alternative flap options

`[04:10]` — A **heterodigital transposition flap** from an adjacent finger (e.g., ring to small finger) is
described as another option, also closed with full-thickness skin graft. **Cross-finger flaps** were
considered but judged less favorable by Lukas's group: early finger mobilization is not possible, and a
second procedure is required roughly two weeks later to divide the flap bridge.

## Postoperative protocol

`[04:10]`–`[05:12]` — Intensive postoperative management: **lymph drainage starting day one**, **individualized
splinting from day two**, hand therapy, and — once sutures are removed — **scar massage, silicone application,
and vibration** to promote scar softening.

## Outcomes over time

`[05:12]`–`[06:13]` — In a homodigital flap case example: full extension and good flexion at 4 weeks; scar
still red at 4 months, improving with physical treatment over subsequent months; by **6 months, full extension
could not be maintained** (some loss observed); a flap-related **sensibility deficit and cold sensitivity in
the finger** were present at this stage, described as expected/normal for the procedure. At **1 year**, full
extension was not sustained, though sensitivity had recovered well aside from mild residual cold sensitivity.
Lukas is explicit about how he characterizes this loss: **"I don't know — is it a recurrence? It's a
recurrence of joint contracture — there are no nodules — but we couldn't keep, on this fifth finger, the
extension of zero degrees which we had after surgery"** `[06:13]` — flagging the ambiguity between true
disease recurrence and a mechanical loss of surgical correction.

## Complications

`[07:15]` — Across the series: **four wound-healing problems**, **one lost transposition flap**, **partial
full-thickness skin graft loss**, and **vascularity problems specifically in Tubiana stage IV patients** when
attempting full intraoperative extension — vascular compromise in these more severe cases required
deliberately loosening the achieved correction rather than risking the flap.

## Five-year series results

`[07:15]`–`[09:20]` — **50 patients over five years**, mostly ring and small fingers; roughly half presented
with PIP contracture under 60 degrees; staged distribution weighted toward Tubiana stage II, with fewer stage
III and stage IV cases. **30 patients underwent primary surgery, 10 were recurrence operations**; **35
homodigital and 5 heterodigital flaps** were performed, with **15 cases involving open arthrolysis**.
Severity-stratified outcome at two years:

- **Preoperative contracture under 60°**: full intraoperative extension typically achieved, but with a loss of
  **15–30°** by two years.
- **Preoperative contracture ≥90°**: full extension **could not be achieved even intraoperatively** despite
  arthrolysis; a further ~10° was lost, with recurrent contracture of **30–60°** by two years.

Compared against a zigzag-incision technique, Lukas reports **less residual flexion contracture** with this
flap approach, particularly at the fifth finger, and notes recurrence contracture was **considerably higher**
in revision (previously operated) cases than in primary cases. Sensibility was initially poor after surgery,
as expected, with cold sensitivity gradually improving over roughly two years. **Two revision procedures**
were required: one PIP arthrolysis and one fifth-finger amputation.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Flap-related sensibility deficit and cold sensitivity**, tracked from early postoperative period through
  gradual improvement at two years, and explicitly described as an expected part of recovery rather than a
  complication `[06:13]`.
- **Vascular compromise during intraoperative extension in Tubiana stage IV disease**, requiring surgeons to
  deliberately accept less correction to preserve blood supply `[07:15]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Lukas discloses nothing to disclose (no financial conflicts). He is explicit and unresolved about whether
post-surgical loss of correction in the absence of new nodules should be called "recurrence" — presenting this
as a genuine, unresolved question rather than asserting a definitive answer. He reports complications
(wound-healing problems, lost flap, vascularity compromise, two revision procedures including an amputation)
candidly alongside the favorable comparative results, and frames the conclusion's benefit as holding "at least
in the medium term," not as a permanent solution.
