# Dr. Wolfgang Wach: "The Patient's View — 1" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**A ~2,500-response global patient survey found that smoking's effect on age of disease onset is
threshold-like — negligible early on, then substantial after about ten years of smoking — and that this
effect is considerably stronger in patients with no family history of the disease, alongside marked
country-level differences in how patients rate their consultation quality.** Wach summarizes the smoking
finding directly: **"once you had about ten years of smoking, probably then — bang — there's a big effect of
smoking on the onset of the disease"** `[06:10]`, and separately notes the family-history interaction: **"we
see a strong effect [with] seven years of smoking [versus not] — the effect is much stronger for the group of
patients with no family history"** `[07:15]`.

## Survey design

`[00:00]`–`[02:02]` — Jointly initiated by the Ledderhose disease online community (credited to Gary Manley)
and joined by the **International Dupuytren Society**, with support from the British Dupuytren Society, the
German Dupuytren Society, and the (US) Dupuytren Foundation. Goals: assess perceived quality of treatment and
consultation, lifestyle effects (smoking, drinking), related-disease associations, country-level differences,
and patient needs. Delivered as a **global online survey, under five minutes to complete**, in parallel
English and German versions with identical questions for later merging. Distributed via the Ledderhose
community, society forums, and the Dupuytren Foundation's mailing list, plus some German clinics inviting
patients directly. **Roughly 10,000 patients were approached; about 2,500 responded**, with the largest
contributions from the US, UK, and Germany, plus notable participation from Australia and Canada.

## Related diseases and demographics

`[03:04]`–`[05:09]` — **35% of respondents had Ledderhose disease** — Wach notes this is somewhat
over-represented given the survey's Ledderhose-community origin. Dupuytren's patients frequently also reported
**frozen shoulder and knuckle pads** — an expected association — plus **12% thyroid disease and 5% diabetes**;
Wach notes he could not find a reliable general-population baseline to compare these figures against. He also
notes relatively few respondents with a separately named condition (referred to in the transcript only as
"IPP," left unexpanded), attributing this to the survey not specifically reaching that patient community
through its own forums or mailing lists.

## Age of onset

`[04:05]`–`[06:10]` — The survey captured **age of onset**, distinct from the age-of-*treatment* figures more
commonly published elsewhere. Males dominated even in the **10–19 age group** — since occupational or
lifestyle exposure would have had little time to act by that age, Wach suggests this points toward a
**genetic component** to why men tend to develop the disease earlier. A roughly 10-year male/female peak-age
gap first appeared in the data but was identified as an artifact of 10-year age-bin grouping; correcting for
this, the true difference was **about four years**.

## Smoking and family history

`[06:10]`–`[07:15]` — No meaningful difference in age of onset was found by amount of alcohol consumption
(tested at several thresholds). Smoking showed **little effect initially**, but a substantial effect appeared
**after roughly ten years of smoking history**, and this effect was **considerably stronger in patients with
no family history of the disease** than in those with one — Wach notes this pattern held for both male and
female respondents, while cautioning that drilling down that far (e.g., non-smoking females with definitively
no family history) reduced the group to as few as 23 patients from a starting pool of over 2,000.

## Consultation and treatment ratings

`[07:15]`–`[09:18]` — Perceived **quality of consultation varied substantially by country** — relatively good
in Germany, notably poor in Australia (which Wach flags as needing attention), and a high number of
dissatisfied patients among the roughly 900 US respondents, though he notes the US figures were not
statistically as bad as they might first appear given the sample size. **Treatment satisfaction**, rated 1–10:
**fasciectomy rated favorably**, with PNF, collagenase, and radiotherapy also doing well; **steroid injection
rated less favorably**; **verapamil cream/ointment was rated as largely ineffective**, consistent with the
group's own prior assessment of it. PNF's relatively high representation in the survey is attributed partly
to the Dupuytren Foundation's involvement — many of Charles Eaton's patients participated.

## Closing patient comment

`[09:18]` — Wach closes by quoting one patient's comment directly, without further comment of his own:
**"this is the disease you want to get — it's a bit inconvenient, but it doesn't kill you, and it won't limit
what you can do, unless you let it."**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Frozen shoulder and knuckle pads**, named as commonly co-occurring conditions among Dupuytren's survey
  respondents `[04:05]`.
- **Onset shifting several years earlier among smokers**, specifically after a roughly ten-year smoking
  history threshold `[06:10]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Wach is candid about the survey's structural biases throughout: the Ledderhose disease over-representation
(35%) is explicitly attributed to the survey's originating community, the omission of another patient group is
explained by the survey's distribution channels rather than treated as a true prevalence finding, and he
flags that subgroup analyses (e.g., non-smoking females with no family history) shrink to very small sample
sizes. He notes he could not find a reliable comparison baseline for the thyroid/diabetes comorbidity figures.
The talk closes by deferring detailed patient-needs findings to a forthcoming symposium book chapter rather
than summarizing them on slide.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly complements `splC4aATa3g`** (Manley's companion talk, "The Patient's View: Ledderhose Disease") —
that talk isolates the Ledderhose disease subset of this same survey, including a striking gender skew (women
outnumbering men with Ledderhose) not covered in this Dupuytren's-focused talk. **Directly relevant to
`S2k9bDzxSOU`** (van Driel's Peyronie's disease talk) — that talk's 22% Dupuytren's
comorbidity rate among 400+ Peyronie's patients, and its likely identification of "IPP" as induratio penis
plastica (Peyronie's disease), together explain why this survey found "relatively little" of that condition:
Peyronie's patients weren't specifically reached through this survey's Dupuytren's-focused distribution
channels, so the low count reflects sampling, not true low comorbidity. **Directly relevant to `_hoXDJ5qBFU`** (Wach's own welcome talk) — both talks come from the same presenter and
share his patient-perspective framing; this talk supplies the quantitative survey data behind the more
speculative ideas raised there. **Relevant to `HE6nBpteAAo`** (Rayan's risk factor talk) and any note
discussing family history or genetic predisposition — this survey's finding that smoking's effect is much
stronger without a family history is a specific, quantified gene-environment interaction not covered in more
general risk-factor discussions elsewhere in this library. **Relevant to any note reporting patient
satisfaction with radiotherapy, steroid injection, or verapamil** — this survey's patient-rated satisfaction
figures are a self-reported perception measure, distinct from and worth comparing against the clinically
measured outcome data reported in other notes for the same treatments.
