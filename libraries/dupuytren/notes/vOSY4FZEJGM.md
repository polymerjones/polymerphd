# Holger Erne Dr med, "Percutaneous Needle Fasciotomy — a Serious Alternative" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a retrospective series of 43 patients (58 rays), needle fasciotomy achieved a mean 39.4-degree
improvement with a 10% recurrence rate at 11 months and zero nerve or vessel injuries — recurrence and nerve
safety being the two specific concerns Erne's group set out to test.** He states the conclusion directly:
**"yes, of course, the percutaneous needle fasciotomy is a serious alternative, with a good outcome and
satisfied patients"** `[07:13]`.

## Background

`[00:03]`–`[02:07]` — Erne credits **Felix Baur** as the first to describe the disease pattern, originally
attributing it to shrinkage of the flexor tendons themselves (etiology he notes is still not fully clear,
though genetic factors are substantial, with Northern European populations more affected than African
populations). He cites a **German prevalence estimate of roughly 1.4–1.9%,** corresponding to an incidence
around 2.4%. **Tubiana staging (first described 1961)** is defined here with specific degree ranges of total
passive extension deficit across the MCP, PIP, and DIP joints combined: **stage 1: 0–45°, stage 2: 45–90°,
stage 3: 90–135°, stage 4: >135°.** Surgical options are reviewed: **needle fasciotomy** (first described by
Lermusiaux, 1980), mini-open fasciotomy, partial/selective fasciectomy, complete fasciectomy, and more
aggressive options (dermofasciectomy, total anterior tenoarthrolysis, credited to Zafar Alwana). Erne notes
complete/partial fasciectomy is agreed to be time-consuming both operatively and in recovery, with real risk
of **infection, nerve injury, diminished blood flow, cold intolerance, and reduced range of motion.**

## Technique

`[02:07]`–`[04:10]` — A **20-gauge needle on a syringe of local anesthetic** is used with the affected finger
held extended to make the cord prominent. Injection typically starts **distal to the palmar crease, then
proximal**, cutting the cord from different angles with the needle bevel — most cases needed only one or two
levels in the palm to regain full extension. Once the cord feels cut or nearly cut, the finger ray is
**hyperextended to crack remaining fibers**. Critically, **the actual cutting maneuver is performed without
local anesthesia** at that specific moment — a deliberate choice **"under the idea to prevent nerve damage,"**
with patients instructed to alert the surgeon to any pricking sensation, functioning as a live safety check.
Post-procedure: dressing applied, a **full-extension splint worn at night for three months**, with unrestricted
daytime hand and finger movement recommended.

## Study design and results

`[04:10]`–`[05:11]` — Retrospective review, patients treated 2008–2009: **43 patients, 58 rays**, mean
follow-up **11.1 months**, mean age **59.7**, 33 men and 14 women. Stage distribution: **stage 1: 34 rays,
stage 2: 20 rays, stage 3: 4 rays.** Results: **52 of 58 rays achieved full extension**; **6 rays showed
recurrence of 5–15 degrees**, for an overall **recurrence rate of 10%**; **mean improvement was 39.4
degrees.** Safety: **no hematoma, no nerve or vessel lesion**, though **three minor skin lesions** occurred
from the hyperextension maneuver, in each case where the cord remained adherent to the skin. Erne notes almost
every patient would undergo the procedure again if needed, and average **inability to work was 11 days** —
described as a notably short recovery.

## Comparison against other techniques

`[05:11]`–`[06:12]` — On nerve safety, the primary initial concern: **zero nerve or artery lesions across all
58 rays**, even across multiple treating surgeons — compared to **0–5% nerve injury rates reported by other
needle-technique authors**, and **0–20% reported in open-technique studies.** On recurrence, the second
concern: this series' **10% at 11 months** compares favorably to **24–65% reported by other needle-technique
authors**, though Erne notes longer-observation open-technique series report **9–11%** recurrence — a range
close to, or possibly lower than, this study's own short-term figure, a comparison he does not resolve or
downplay. Predictors of better outcome (not statistically verified in this small series): a **well-defined,
visible cord without arthrogenic contracture**, and **lower Tubiana stage (1–2) outperforming higher stages.**

## Advantages and disadvantages, stated directly

`[06:12]`–`[07:13]` — Advantages: high patient satisfaction, short operative time without need for general
anesthesia, short recovery, low complication rate, and — a phrase Erne uses directly — **"we don't burn any
bridges,"** meaning minimal scar tissue leaves future surgery unhindered if needed later. Disadvantages:
**painful skin penetration, the cord itself remains in place (not removed), and a higher recurrence rate**
than more invasive techniques. His final recommendation: needle fasciotomy for **flexion contracture stage 1
or 2, with a definable cord and no arthrogenic contracture**, particularly for **patients with limited time**
who understand and accept the increased recurrence risk.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Minor skin lesions from the hyperextension "cracking" maneuver**, specifically occurring where the cord
  remained adherent to the skin `[05:11]`.
- **A pricking sensation reported by the patient during the anesthesia-free cutting step**, used directly as
  an intraoperative nerve-safety signal `[04:10]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"We don't burn any bridges"** — for needle fasciotomy's minimal-scarring advantage, leaving future surgical
  options open `[06:12]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Erne frames this explicitly as a **retrospective** series, and is candid that the outcome-predictor findings
(cord definability, stage) are **not statistically verified**, presenting them as an observed pattern rather
than a proven association. He directly juxtaposes his own recurrence figure against both more and less
favorable comparisons in the literature (worse-performing needle series, better-performing open series with
longer follow-up) rather than selectively citing only the comparisons that favor his own results.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly comparable to `-32nd2vQexQ`** (Gan's needle aponeurotomy talk) and `wSZhfP1r1OY` (Eaton's IFSSH
needle fasciotomy talk) — all three report needle-technique series with broadly consistent zero-to-low nerve
injury rates, adding a third independent dataset supporting needle fasciotomy's nerve safety profile. **Its
zero-nerve-injury, 10%-recurrence result is notably more favorable than several other needle series it cites
by comparison (24–65% recurrence)** — worth flagging directly to readers comparing needle fasciotomy outcomes
across this library's notes, since technique details (anesthesia-free cutting as a live safety check, in
particular) may meaningfully affect results between series. **Relevant to `R4WPhcOpAMo`** (Selles's consensus
recurrence definition talk) — this study's 11-month follow-up and 5–15-degree recurrence threshold are both
shorter/looser than that consensus definition's one-year, 20-degree standard, a limitation worth weighing when
comparing this series' 10% figure against other notes using the later, stricter consensus definition.
