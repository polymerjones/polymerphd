# Michael Papaloïzos MD, "Minimizing Cutaneous Necrosis and Delayed Healing" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**A short-zigzag incision design with thick, tangentially elevated flaps produced very low rates of skin
necrosis across 55 consecutive cases, offering an alternative to both open-palm technique and the larger
flap elevations of standard Z-plasty and Bruner incisions.** Papaloïzos's summary of the tradeoff he was
trying to solve: standard closed techniques **"usually [require] large skin flaps... elevated, [which] leads
to skin loss and delayed healing"** `[03:12]`, while open-palm technique is **"very demanding
post-operatively for the patient"** and its effect on recurrence **"has been questioned recently in
different papers"** `[02:10]`.

## The problem with existing approaches

`[01:05]`–`[04:13]` — Both major closure strategies carry real costs: **open-palm (McCash) technique**
applies only in the palm (fingers still need skin flaps), and requires **daily or every-other-day dressing
changes over a long period.** **Closed-skin techniques** (his general preference for primary cases) — Z-
plasty, V-Y plasty, zigzag, or Bruner incisions — typically require **large skin flap elevation**, causing
skin loss, delayed healing, and a bothersome, repeatedly-redressed wound that can **impair early
rehabilitation and lead to edema, infection, or algodystrophy (reflex sympathetic dystrophy).**

## A sailing-inspired solution

`[04:13]`–`[05:15]` — Papaloïzos describes the idea's origin directly: **"I was sailing on the Geneva
lake... when you are against the wind, you have to tack from one side to the other... maybe I could do
small tacks"** — instead of the large flap-elevation "tacks" standard techniques require, use **many short
zigzag increments.** The resulting technique: short-armed zigzag incisions with **flaps elevated tangentially
and kept as thick as possible** (minimizing devascularization), stitched with single sutures at each flap
tip.

## Technique specifics

`[07:16]`–`[08:16]` — Short incision arms of **4–5mm**, with the elevation kept tangential and thick at the
base rather than broad and thin. One "way" (ray) typically corresponds to one incision, and up to **five**
have been used in a single case; the technique combines readily with **transverse or T-incisions in the
palm** for broad, thick aponeurosis, and with **V-Y plasty** when more skin coverage is needed. He notes he
suspects the arms could be made **even shorter**, but hasn't yet confirmed this is technically manageable.

## The series and its necrosis-grading system

`[05:15]`–`[08:16]` — A **prospective, consecutive series, November 2007 to November 2009: 55 unoperated
cases**, mean age 63 (range 29–94), only **3 recurrent** (the rest primary). Deliberately short **mean
follow-up of seven weeks**, since the study's specific objective was skin healing, not recurrence. Staging:
roughly a third early-stage (Tubiana stage 1), about half stage 2, and about a quarter stage 3–4; joint
involvement spanned **20 MCP, 23 PIP, and 12 DIP** joints. Skin outcome was graded on a **four-point scale**
(0 = no skin loss; 1 = one limited point of superficial necrosis; 2 = two to three points of superficial
necrosis or one point of deeper, full-thickness necrosis; 3 = multiple, extended, or deep subdermal
necrosis), assessed at **day 5 (first dressing), day 12 (stitch removal), and four weeks.**

## Results

`[10:17]`–`[11:17]` — **Very few necrosis cases at 5 days**; slightly more at one week but still limited in
extent; by one month, the picture had **settled back to no necrosis or only limited superficial necrosis.**
Total complications: **three small hematomas**, all resolving without further surgery or intervention.

## Stated advantages and honest limits

`[11:17]`–`[12:18]` — Advantages: a **simple design** not requiring large flaps, easily combined with other
local flaps (V-Y specifically), **versatile and usable anywhere on the hand**, and — notably — **"very easy
to learn and very easy to teach,"** promoting primary skin healing. Disadvantages he names directly: flap
elevation still requires care, closure is **relatively time-consuming**, and — importantly — **"I do not
pretend that this technique can be used in every case"**: dermofasciectomy with grafting remains necessary
for genuinely difficult cases. On recurrence specifically, he's blunt: **"with my seven-week follow-up, I
have no idea"** — explicitly promising to investigate that separately in the future rather than claiming any
recurrence benefit from a study never designed to measure it. He closes by quoting **Houston**: **"it is the
patient, not the surgeon, who determines the recurrence, by his inherited tendency to the disease"** — a
direct restatement of the diathesis concept discussed throughout this library.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Skin necrosis at incision flap tips**, graded systematically (0–3) rather than described impressionistically, at three specific follow-up points `[08:16]`.
- **Delayed wound healing requiring prolonged, repeated dressing changes**, named as a specific source of
  patient burden with standard flap techniques `[02:10]`.
- **Small hematomas as the only complications observed**, all self-resolving `[11:17]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Necrosis grading scale (0–3)** — the four-point skin-outcome scale defined and applied throughout this
  study `[08:16]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Sailing "tacks" against the wind, applied to incision design** — many small directional changes instead
  of a few large ones, the direct inspiration for this technique's short zigzag arms `[04:13]`.
- **Houston's dictum**: **"it is the patient, not the surgeon, who determines the recurrence, by his
  inherited tendency to the disease"** `[12:18]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Notably candid about the study's scope: Papaloïzos states outright that the seven-week follow-up means **he
has no data on recurrence at all**, rather than implying a benefit the study wasn't designed to detect. He
also explicitly limits the technique's claimed applicability — **"difficult cases remain difficult
cases"** — rather than presenting it as a universal replacement for dermofasciectomy and grafting.
