# Annet Van Rijssen MD, "5-Year Results of the First-Ever Randomised Trial of PNF versus Limited Fasciectomy" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In the first randomized trial to directly compare percutaneous needle fasciotomy (PNF) against limited
fasciectomy (LF), recurrence was both far more frequent and far sooner after PNF — but the difference
narrowed sharply with patient age, disappearing almost entirely in the oldest patients.** The headline
numbers: **LF recurrence was 26% (mean time to recurrence 3.8 years); PNF recurrence was 85% (mean time to
recurrence 2.1 years)** `[05:09]`–`[06:10]`. Van Rijssen's practical conclusion: **"PNF is suitable for
patients who require fast recovery of hand function, who are willing to accept early recurrence"** — and
specifically **"less suitable for younger patients,"** better reserved for **older patients with a clearly
defined cord** `[10:15]`–`[11:16]`.

## Trial design

`[00:03]`–`[03:07]` — Every patient presenting to the department between **August 2002 and January 2005**
was considered. Inclusion: **at least a 30-degree flexion contracture at PIP, DIP, or MCP**, with a
**clearly defined cord**, willing to participate. Exclusions: prior special treatment, post-surgical
recurrence/extension already present, or being unable to stop anticoagulants or otherwise unfit for surgery.
Pre-operative measures recorded: demographics, **total passive extension deficit (TPD)** across DIP, PIP,
and MCP, **DASH score**, and a 10-question satisfaction survey. **Randomization was by drawing an envelope**
(PNF or LF), with treatment within one month by a senior author. LF used regional/general anesthesia, a
Skoog incision in the palm plus Bruner incision in the finger, loupe magnification, transposed and sutured
skin, and one week of bandaging with instructed flexion/extension exercises. PNF used local anesthesia and
the standard needle-fasciotomy technique. Follow-up: **1 week, 6 weeks, 6 months, then annually to 5
years.** **Recurrence was defined as a 30-degree worsening of TPD compared to the 6-week post-operative
measurement** — chosen specifically because a prior pilot study had shown the 6-week mark represents peak
results.

## The results

`[05:09]`–`[06:10]` — **115 hands in 111 patients** (95 male, 17 female, evenly split between arms), mean
age 63 (also balanced between arms). **54 treated with LF (11 lost to follow-up)**: of the 43 remaining, **32
had no recurrence (74%), 11 recurred (26%)**, at a mean of **3.8 years (SD 1.1).** **61 treated with PNF (7
lost to follow-up)**: of the 54 remaining, **only 8 had no recurrence, 46 recurred — an 85% recurrence
rate** — occurring much sooner, at a mean of **2.1 years.** Both the recurrence *rate* and *timing*
differences were statistically significant with small p-values.

## Sensitivity analysis for missing patients

`[06:10]`–`[07:10]` — Because it's unknown whether lost-to-follow-up patients recurred, Van Rijssen ran both
a **worst-case analysis** (assuming every lost patient in each group had recurred) and a **best-case
analysis** (assuming none had). The PNF-versus-LF gap **persisted under the worst-case assumption, and grew
even larger under the best-case assumption** — indicating the core finding is robust to how the missing data
is handled.

## The age effect

`[07:10]`–`[08:12]` — Plotting recurrence against age revealed a clear pattern: **the gap between recurrence
and non-recurrence narrows steadily with age.** Patients **over 75 showed more non-recurrent than recurrent
disease**, while **younger patients recurred almost universally, regardless of which treatment they
received.** This is presented as an independent risk factor cutting across the treatment comparison, not
just a confound within it.

## What patients did after recurrence, and how satisfied they were

`[08:12]`–`[10:15]` — Given a free choice after recurrence: of PNF patients, **25 chose repeat PNF, 7
switched to LF, 12 chose no further treatment**; of LF patients, **4 switched to PNF, none chose repeat LF,
7 chose no further treatment.** Despite PNF's much higher recurrence rate, **overall satisfaction was high in
both groups, though statistically significantly higher after LF.** Van Rijssen flags the seemingly
paradoxical fact that many patients remained satisfied with PNF and would choose it again despite recurring:
this reflects satisfaction measured **across the whole treated group, not only those who recurred** — most
PNF patients' overall experience (speed, low initial morbidity) stayed positive even when the correction
itself didn't last.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Recurrence quantified as a specific 30-degree TPD threshold from the 6-week post-operative baseline**,
  not a vaguer clinical impression `[04:08]`.
- **Faster recurrence after PNF (mean 2.1 years) versus LF (mean 3.8 years)**, a concrete timing difference
  patients can be counseled on directly `[06:10]`.
- **Near-universal recurrence in younger patients regardless of technique chosen**, versus much lower
  recurrence risk in patients over 75 `[07:10]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Total passive extension deficit (TPD)** — the summed extension deficit across DIP, PIP, and MCP joints,
  used as this trial's primary contracture measure `[02:06]`.
- **PNF (percutaneous needle fasciotomy) and LF (limited fasciectomy)** — the two randomized arms compared
  throughout `[00:03]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk presents trial design and statistical results directly.

## Source-stated confidence

This is presented as rigorous randomized-trial evidence with pre-specified statistical methods (chi-square
for categorical data, Student's t-test, Kaplan-Meier survival curves, linear-by-linear association for age),
not observational data — a notably higher evidence tier than many other sources in this library. Van Rijssen
proactively addresses the loss-to-follow-up limitation with sensitivity analysis rather than ignoring it, and
is explicit that the definition of recurrence (30-degree TPD worsening from the 6-week mark) was chosen
deliberately based on prior pilot data, not arbitrarily.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Is very likely one of the primary trials behind the general "minimally invasive procedures last about
half as long as open surgery before recurrence" claims made in `-jLfWXX3YUM`** (Seven Questions) and
consistent with the **~50%-at-3–5-years** needle fasciotomy recurrence figure in `nice-htg21-needle-
fasciotomy` — though this trial's own PNF recurrence figure (85% by 5 years, mean time 2.1 years) reads
considerably higher than that NICE guidance figure, worth flagging as a real discrepancy rather than
smoothing over: possible explanations include this trial's specific recurrence definition (30-degree TPD
threshold, arguably stricter than some other studies') and its patient population (minimum 30-degree
contracture required at baseline, likely a more severe population than "any needle fasciotomy patient").
**Directly relevant to `9LY5fyLw618`** (Hurst's collagenase talk) and `1BURdqs3a4U`** (Pess's technique
talk) — both favor minimally invasive treatment for faster recovery despite acknowledged shorter durability;
this trial's age-stratified finding (younger patients recur regardless of technique; older patients favor
PNF's lower morbidity since their recurrence risk is low either way) supplies a concrete, data-driven
rationale for age-based technique selection that neither of those talks quantifies as precisely.
**`93IWNYYqCjs`** (Warwick's "what we may never know" talk) argues trials exactly like this one can't
produce genuinely generalizable results, given cord heterogeneity and patient self-selection bias — a
methodological challenge worth weighing against this trial's own conclusions rather than treating them as
unqualified fact.
