# Dr. Caroline Leclercq "Severe PIP Joint Contractures" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Severe PIP contracture (defined here as greater than 90 degrees, or lesser contracture combined with
recurrence or strong diathesis) forces surgeons to solve four separable problems at once — skin, fascia,
joint, and extensor mechanism — and skin grafting specifically, not the release technique, is what
protects against recurrence.** Leclercq states this directly from her own data: **dermofasciectomy patients
recurred in only 21% of cases at an average 5.6-year follow-up, "whereas the rest of the series had a much
higher rate of recurrence"** — concluding **"it seems that the skin graft protects the digit from
recurrence... it has a protective effect"** `[15:24]`.

## Managing skin shortage

`[00:00]`–`[04:09]` — For isolated PIP contracture up to 90 degrees, Leclercq uses **local V-Y flaps**
("quite safe"). Beyond that, or with recurrence and heavy scarring, **skin grafts** become necessary despite
being technically demanding — she shows a case with graft necrosis salvaged by a cross-finger flap, noting
wryly the cosmetic result was imperfect but **"the patient is one of my happiest patients."** The core
tradeoff: grafts carry surgical risk and a less-than-ideal appearance, but **"apart from a few examples,
there is no recurrence on the graft"** — provided the graft covers the important area (essentially the
whole proximal phalanx).

## Two-stage surgery and external fixation for extreme cases

`[04:09]`–`[06:13]` — In very severe cases, Leclercq sometimes performs a **primary palmar fasciotomy first**
to release the MP joint, making subsequent access to the cords easier without fighting skin shortage at the
same time. For the most extreme contractures, **external fixator techniques** (originating with Messina) can
achieve meaningful correction — she cites a 2010 series of 34 patients at 30-month follow-up with **an
average residual flexion deficit of only 21 degrees.**

## Fascia and joint technical hazards

`[06:13]`–`[09:18]` — Severe cases present **central, lateral, pre-vascular, retrovascular, spiral, and
double-spiral cords together**, too complex for simple needle treatment; she notes ultrasound has been
explored for cord mapping but **cords can be iso-echoic (invisible against surrounding tissue), with poor
sensitivity and no demonstrated inter-observer reliability.** A specific safety tip: when operating on the
**second or fifth finger**, the vascular pedicle can be very thin — **protect and control the contralateral
artery first**, before dissecting near the at-risk one, to avoid compounding an injury. Releasing the flexor
sheath risks a large tear that complicates any planned skin graft, addressed with a **"check-rein" release**
done carefully and laterally, described as "quite a safe procedure" when done that way.

## The extensor mechanism is easy to forget

`[09:18]`–`[10:20]` — In long-standing severe contracture, **the extensor mechanism itself often slackens** —
assessed intraoperatively via what she calls the **Tubiana disease test.** In one case, she notes removing
the Dupuytren's tissue and finger stiffness fully corrected nothing if the extensor slackening itself wasn't
separately addressed; her preference for fixing it is **K-wiring**, expressing more caution about tendon
repair. She states directly: **"should we obtain complete PIP extension at all costs? Well, probably not"**
— citing a study (from a group she names "Lens") showing collateral ligament and capsule release procedures
**don't meaningfully change long-term outcomes**, and warns specifically about arthritic joints and
unhappy high-function patients (naming golf-playing retirees as a group who remain dissatisfied regardless).

## Comparative outcome and recurrence data

`[12:22]`–`[15:24]` — Several points assembled from the literature and her own series:

- **Fasciectomy** achieves close to full MP correction and near-full PIP correction, though **PIP results
  vary with initial contracture severity** in most reported data (an exception she notes: Joe Dias's own
  data did not show that correlation).
- **Needle aponeurotomy** results are described as **far less satisfactory at the MP level**, and a
  retrospective study (Hurst's) found **little difference between needle and collagenase** for either MP
  or PIP joint outcomes.
- A cited **2011 meta-analysis** found recurrence rates **lower after fasciectomy than after needle
  treatment.**
- **Fasciectomy recurrence was previously thought to occur early and then plateau, but longer-term data
  shows it continues growing, "although at a less steady pace."**
- Her own dermofasciectomy series (**34 patients, 41 procedures, average 5.6-year follow-up**): **only 21%
  recurrence**, markedly lower than the rest of her non-grafted series — presented as her strongest evidence
  for the graft's protective effect.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **PIP joint stiffness after surgery, explicitly distinguished from true contracture recurrence** — shown
  in a case with a good functional result that some might misread as recurrence `[02:05]`.
- **Extensor mechanism slackening in long-standing severe contracture**, assessable intraoperatively and
  easily missed if attention stays only on the flexor-side cords `[09:18]`.
- **Vascular compromise risk specific to the second and fifth fingers during dissection near the vascular
  pedicle** `[08:17]`.
- **Persistent patient dissatisfaction in high-function patients (e.g., competitive golfers) even after a
  technically successful release**, described as an unresolved problem in her own long-term follow-up
  `[10:20]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Dermofasciectomy's protective effect** — the specific, literature-supported finding that recurrence is
  rare within the boundaries of a skin graft, unlike after fasciectomy alone `[15:24]`.
- **Tubiana disease test** — the intraoperative test used to assess extensor mechanism slackening `[09:18]`.
- **Check-rein release** — the lateral flexor sheath release technique described as safe when done
  carefully `[09:18]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk is a direct case-and-data review rather than built around illustrative comparison.

## Source-stated confidence

Leclercq opens by flagging the field's lack of a shared definition for "severe" PIP contracture, and
repeatedly qualifies her own recommendations against literature that sometimes disagrees (noting Dias's data
did not replicate the initial-contracture-severity correlation others report). Her main protective-effect
claim is backed by her own quantified series data rather than asserted generally, and she explicitly flags
the extension-versus-flexion tradeoff study as showing **"not too much difference in the long term"** rather
than overstating it.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly corroborates `9LY5fyLw618`** (Hurst's collagenase talk), which separately recommends
fasciotomy-plus-split-thickness-skin-grafting for recurrent disease with damaged skin — Leclercq's
quantified recurrence data (21% under graft vs. much higher elsewhere) is the outcome evidence behind that
same recommendation. **Extends `nice-htg21-needle-fasciotomy`** and **`-jLfWXX3YUM`** (Seven Questions),
both of which describe needle/minimally invasive treatment as shorter-lasting than open surgery before
recurrence — this talk's cited meta-analysis (lower recurrence after fasciectomy than needle) and her own
graft-protection data quantify exactly that gap for the most severe cases specifically. **Relevant to
`2A9j4yqtpGI`** (Agee's Digit Widget talk) — both talks address PIP joint extensor-side changes during
severe, long-standing contracture, though from different angles (extensor tendon moment arm there, extensor
mechanism slackening here). **In tension with `1Xqmh80rYPQ`** (Degreef's surgical outcome talk), whose
patient-reported recurrence survey found no significant advantage for more extensive surgery over segmental
fasciectomy, after adjusting for indication bias — the two studies aren't directly comparable (Degreef's is
a general first-surgery population; this talk's comparison is within a severe-PIP-contracture population
specifically), but together they suggest the graft-protective effect may hold mainly for severe, high-risk
disease rather than as a general rule, exactly the kind of claim Degreef's talk warns needs bias-adjustment
before trusting. **`klWbjOcG3Kk`** (Ketchum's dermofasciectomy talk) supplies much stronger evidence for the
same graft-protective effect — a 204-hand, 25-year series with zero recurrence beneath the graft, versus this
talk's smaller 34-patient series — and should be treated as the primary source where the two overlap.
