# Dr. Charles Eaton: "2019 Dupuytren Review" — Full Lecture

## Central claim

**Eaton presents a striking, counterintuitive finding from two large patient databases: patients with a family
history of Dupuytren's and early disease onset — the classic textbook predictors of "aggressive" disease —
actually show a *slower* measured rate of contracture progression (degrees per year) than patients without
those risk factors, the opposite of standard teaching, and he uses this to argue Dupuytren's biology is best
understood as a pathologically slowed, incomplete version of normal wound healing rather than an accelerated
one.** He states the finding directly: **"the person on the left with more benign biologic disease actually
had a contracture that progressed at a faster rate... than the person on the right [with high-diathesis
factors]. This is the opposite of what one might expect... but it's not an isolated finding — I have access to
two large databases... that confirm, overall, this finding"** `[35:45]`–`[36:47]`.

*Note: this talk substantially overlaps with `kLYBA2hLRPs` (Eaton's 2017 "Dupuytren Awareness" lecture) on
anatomy, general epidemiology, and the biology-versus-anatomy framework — see that note for fuller treatment of
those shared topics. This note focuses on the material genuinely new or more developed here.*

## Ethnic pattern and the fibrotic disease family

`[07:06]`–`[09:09]` — Beyond the general Caucasian-predominance pattern, Eaton cites specific figures: compared
to Caucasian Americans, the risk in **Hispanic Americans is about one-third**, in **African Americans about
one-fifth**, and in **Asian Americans about one-tenth** — consistent with a Caucasian-ancestry admixture
effect, since the average African American carries roughly one-fifth Caucasian heritage. Studies from **Korea
and China** report native incidence roughly **1,000 times lower** than in the US. He groups Dupuytren's with
other **Caucasian-predominant fibrotic diseases** — Peyronie's disease, Ledderhose disease, frozen shoulder,
pulmonary fibrosis, primary biliary cirrhosis, and myelofibrosis — noting some fibrotic diseases show no known
ancestry effect at all, so this pattern isn't automatic for all fibrosis.

## Natural history before any visible change

`[09:09]`–`[13:15]` — Before any visible sign, patients may notice **loss of elasticity in the palm** — a
"rubber bands" sensation — occurring before any visible change or even microscopic change is detectable.
Following a nodule's first appearance, the **10-year outlook is scattered**: roughly **1 in 10 nodules
resolve**, roughly **7 in 10 (three-quarters) show little to no meaningful change**, and roughly **2 in 10** are
followed by contracture severe enough to need treatment. Critically, Eaton notes the baseline risk for a
**normal hand with no nodule at all** to progress to needing treatment is *also* roughly **1 in 10** over a
similar span — a fact he flags as making preventive-treatment research especially difficult, since nodule
presence alone only modestly changes absolute risk. Most people with Dupuytren's never see a doctor and never
need treatment. Roughly **1 in 30** patients has **severe, treatment-resistant disease** — typically with
strong family history and early onset — facing a real chance of losing hand function over their lifetime.

## Associated conditions and systemic risk

`[13:15]`–`[15:17]` — Conditions associated with Dupuytren's, for unclear reasons, include diabetes, thyroid
problems, cholesterol problems, depression, psoriasis, and being underweight. Patients with bent fingers from
Dupuytren's also show **increased risk of cancers and sarcomas**, **somewhat shorter average life expectancy**,
and **more sick time off work for unrelated conditions** — all with unknown mechanisms, which Eaton frames as
the core motivation for needing to understand the underlying biology.

## Early, pre-visible symptoms

`[15:17]`–`[16:18]` — About **a quarter of people with early disease** (nodules, or even just before nodules
appear) notice **stinging, burning, itching, or tenderness** in the developing area, plus a tightness sensation
("rubber bands"). Patients may also notice **clumsiness or reduced fine dexterity**, specifically in **learned,
repetitive activities** (writing, typing, playing an instrument) — Eaton's explanation: those skills were
learned using the hand's *original* mechanical properties, and as Dupuytren's biology subtly changes those
properties, motor signals calibrated to the old hand produce imprecise results in the new one, sometimes also
causing early muscle fatigue from compensatory positioning.

## Measurement and functional staging

`[16:18]`–`[20:24]` — Beyond the goniometer and clock-face DIY method (see `deJJtpYEy1k`, `cR-2s5DUzUc`), Eaton
details the **Tubiana score** by functional consequence: **0–45°** is disturbing and limits hand use;
**45–90°** becomes more disabling; **90°** is **"the biggest nuisance angle"** — the finger can't be gotten out
of the way in either direction; beyond **90°**, the finger acts like a **hook** and becomes genuinely
**dangerous** — he cites real cases of steering-wheel-related accidents where a hooked finger prevented a
driver reacting in time; past **135°**, the finger has essentially no functional use, and some patients (he
cites actor Bill Nighy) adapt by deliberately curling the finger fully into the palm — a **"functional
amputation."**

## Prevention: what's known and what isn't

`[20:24]`–`[22:27]` — Since Dupuytren's is a fibrotic disease and arteriosclerosis is also fibrotic, with some
biological overlap, Eaton suggests **"what's heart-healthy should also be Dupuytren-healthy"** — inflammation
is part of Dupuytren's biology, and lifestyle factors reducing inflammation (diet, exercise, sleep) are
reasonable candidates. Known risk-increasing factors: **heavy smoking, heavy drinking, and prolonged heavy hand
use.** Whether diet, supplements, or massage help or hurt is **genuinely unknown**, and — repeating the core
argument developed at length in `kLYBA2hLRPs` — this remains untestable at scale without a biomarker, since
Dupuytren's is too slow and unpredictable to evaluate a preventive intervention using only the current
"wait years, then remeasure bend" approach.

## The 20–40 degree treatment window, and joint-specific difficulty

`[22:27]`–`[25:34]` — Treatment follows a wait-then-correct model. The **20–40 degree "sweet spot"** exists
because: below 20°, surgical risk isn't justified by the modest deformity; above 40°, prolonged joint bending
increases risk of secondary tendon/ligament injury. Two procedure categories: **fasciotomy** (open, zigzag or
side-to-side incision, diseased tissue removed) and **dermofasciectomy** (fasciotomy plus removal and grafting
of affected skin, aimed at reducing recurrence); **minimally invasive** options are enzyme (collagenase)
injection or needle fasciotomy. **PIP joints are harder to fully straighten and harder to keep straight** than
MCP joints, a consistent pattern across this library.

## Two kinds of "severity"

`[25:34]`–`[26:36]` — Eaton distinguishes **mechanical severity** (how bent the joint is, predicting whether it
can be straightened at all) from **biological severity** (the likelihood disease activity continues after
treatment, predicting recurrence) — genuinely different axes. Severe mechanical bending can itself cause
secondary tendon/ligament changes that erode initial correction over the following months, independent of
biological activity. Best long-term outlook: mild bend + mild biology. Worst: both severe.

## Skeletal traction for severe PIP contracture

`[27:36]`–`[28:38]` — For PIP joints with severe secondary changes, a pre-fasciotomy technique called
**skeletal traction** is described: a device attached to the finger bones (several models exist, e.g. rubber
bands providing continuous gentle pull) gradually straightens the finger over **6–8 weeks**, followed by
fasciotomy — described as producing the best results for severe PIP contracture specifically. For patients
assessed as having aggressive biology, dermofasciectomy may be chosen as the **first** procedure rather than
fasciotomy, since retreatment after recurrence carries higher complication risk than a first procedure.

## Recontracture versus recurrence

`[28:38]`–`[30:39]` — Soft (unformalized) recurrence risk factors: strong family history, diagnosis under age
50, and lumps outside the palm (knuckle pads, Ledderhose). Eaton distinguishes **recontracture** (a mechanical
bend returning, often from secondary joint/tendon changes, with **no palpable evidence of active disease**)
from true **recurrence** (a genuinely new cord, confirmed by feel) — illustrating with paired cases: one
patient with recontracture but no palpable disease, another with clear new-cord recurrence. He notes outcome
studies generally **don't distinguish these two**, even though the underlying process (and its treatment
implications) differ.

## Detailed recurrence statistics

`[30:39]`–`[33:44]` — Concrete figures: **fewer than 1 in 4 PIP joints remain straight 6 months after
fasciotomy**; **fewer than 1 in 6 fingers remain fully straight 1 year after a minimally invasive procedure**;
**more than 6 in 7 PIP joints have a contracture greater than 20 degrees 5 years after a minimally invasive
procedure.** Annualized recurrence rates (across all comers, acknowledging inconsistent definitions across
studies): **10–20% per year after minimally invasive procedures, 5–10% per year after fasciectomy, roughly 2%
per year after dermofasciectomy.** The lifetime-complication math: **total lifetime complication risk equals
number of procedures times per-procedure complication risk**, and retreatments carry **higher** per-procedure
risk than first treatments due to scar tissue. On cost: minimally invasive procedures cost less individually,
but because retreatment is common, **overall lifetime cost ends up lowest for needle fasciotomy**, with
fasciectomy and collagenase injection landing at similar overall cost to each other.

## The diathesis paradox

`[33:44]`–`[36:47]` — Eaton presents two matched patients: both with identical **50-degree contracture of the
small-finger PIP joint.** Patient A: late age of onset, no family history, no lumps elsewhere (low diathesis).
Patient B: early onset, mother affected, Ledderhose disease in the feet (high diathesis). Standard teaching —
and some published recommendations to operate earlier on high-diathesis patients — predicts B will recur
faster. Calculating each patient's actual **rate of contracture progression (degrees per year)** from known
disease duration, Eaton found **Patient A (low diathesis) had progressed faster** than Patient B — the
opposite of expectation. This wasn't an isolated case: **two large databases** (his own practice and the
International Dupuytren Data Bank) confirm that **family history and early age of onset are each
independently associated with *slower*, not faster, contracture progression rate.**

## The wound-healing-gone-slow hypothesis

`[36:47]`–`[41:53]` — Eaton's proposed explanation: Dupuytren's biology mirrors normal wound healing —
**inflammation → proliferation (repair cells accumulate, felt as early firmness) → collagen deposition → the
cells set tension on that collagen by shrinking it (closing a healing wound) → resolution.** Dupuytren's
follows the **same stages**, but **fails to reach resolution** — the process simply doesn't shut off. His
hypothesis: **severe/"aggressive" Dupuytren's biology means the wound-healing process is even more disrupted
— and therefore even *slower*** — not faster, reconciling the diathesis paradox above. Where a patient gets
"stuck" in this staged process produces distinct clinical patterns: stuck at inflammation → persistent
tenderness/redness without progression; stuck at proliferation → nodules that never resolve for years; stuck at
collagen deposition → thickening cords and skin; stuck at the contracture-setting stage → ongoing, unresolving
bending. He contrasts two patient phenotypes directly: soft, loose skin over a thin, string-like cord with
little collagen (nodule-only-for-a-decade type) versus hard, thick skin fused to underlying tissue with dense,
poorly-defined collagen accumulation (contracture-with-no-nodule-ever type) — different points of "stuck-ness"
in the same underlying process. This also explains poor post-surgical outcomes in aggressive-biology patients:
**surgery is itself an injury**, which normally triggers the same wound-healing cascade — in tissue where that
cascade is already abnormal and stuck, the added injury response has nowhere to resolve and the process simply
continues.

## The compartmentalization problem, and the systemic mechanism

`[41:53]`–`[44:57]` — Eaton names the field's core structural obstacle (echoing `Iu7ui2TQ4CY`): **chronic-
disease/biomarker researchers don't treat Dupuytren's, and the surgeons who treat it don't do that kind of
biomarker research** — leaving patients stuck between two research silos for centuries. He argues the
"battlefield" isn't confined to the palm: when tissue is injured anywhere, damaged cells release **signaling
proteins into the bloodstream**, which circulate to the **bone marrow**, triggering release of repair cells
back into circulation; those cells then **home specifically to areas of high signal concentration** and migrate
into the tissue to begin repair. Since this is a **whole-body circulatory process**, Dupuytren's activity —
being fundamentally a wound-healing-like process — should in principle be **detectable in the bloodstream**,
not just locally in the palm.

## An AI-driven path to a drug

`[44:57]`–`[48:59]` — Illustrating biological complexity, Eaton describes mapping just the connections of a
single molecule, **TGF-beta1**, to other proteins, genes, and growth factors — a network compared to
**"degrees of separation from Kevin Bacon"** — too complex to map manually, requiring **artificial
intelligence, machine learning, and computational biology.** The proposed research pipeline: (1) identify which
blood molecules differ — higher, lower, or structurally altered — between people with active Dupuytren's
disease and those without, building a **blood profile/test**; (2) cross-reference those abnormal molecules
against a database of roughly **13,000 known biologically active molecules** (over-the-counter drugs,
prescription drugs, and molecules that failed FDA approval for other indications); (3) use AI/ML matching to
identify candidate molecules that might **normalize** the abnormal blood profile; (4) use the blood test itself
to quickly evaluate whether a candidate treatment actually shifts the profile toward normal — a computational
drug-discovery approach Eaton describes as having only become practical in the **last 5–10 years**, alongside
growing computational power and biological knowledge bases. This research runs through the **International
Dupuytren Data Bank** (sign-up referenced as "dupstudy.com"), a free, online, longitudinal study. As of this
talk, Eaton states the group was **ready to begin its Dupuytren biomarker blood test pilot study in 2019.**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Stinging, burning, itching, or tenderness in the palm before any nodule is visible**, reported by roughly a
  quarter of early-disease patients `[15:17]`.
- **Clumsiness in learned, repetitive fine-motor tasks** (writing, typing, playing an instrument), attributed to
  a mismatch between trained motor patterns and subtly altered hand mechanics `[15:17]`.
- **Fingers catching on objects (stair banisters, ladder rungs, steering wheels) past 90 degrees of
  contracture**, with real documented accidents from a hooked finger delaying reaction time `[19:23]`.
- **Soft, loose skin over a thin cord versus hard, thickened skin fused to dense collagen** — two visibly
  distinct clinical phenotypes tied to where the wound-healing-like process is proposed to be "stuck"
  `[39:51]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Recontracture** — a mechanical return of bending after treatment with no palpable evidence of active
  disease, distinguished from true biological recurrence `[28:38]`.
- **Skeletal traction** — a pre-fasciotomy device-based technique gradually straightening a severely contracted
  PIP joint over 6–8 weeks `[27:36]`.
- **Functional amputation** — a patient's deliberate, adaptive curling of an unusably bent finger fully into
  the palm to get it out of the way `[19:23]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Dupuytren's as wound healing that never reaches resolution**, with different patients "stuck" at different
  stages producing distinct clinical patterns `[37:48]`.
- **TGF-beta1's molecular network compared to "degrees of separation from Kevin Bacon"** — illustrating why
  Dupuytren's biology requires computational, not manual, analysis `[44:57]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Eaton is explicit that the diathesis/rate finding contradicts what's "taught for years" and what some published
authors recommend, presenting it candidly as a genuine reversal of expectation rather than downplaying the
conflict with prior teaching — and he supports it with two independent large datasets rather than a single
anecdotal case. He frames the wound-healing-stage hypothesis explicitly as a hypothesis offered to explain that
finding, not a proven mechanism. He is direct that recontracture-versus-recurrence conflation in outcome
literature is a real, acknowledged data-quality problem, and that recurrence-rate figures vary because
different studies use different definitions (a limitation echoed in `R4WPhcOpAMo`).
