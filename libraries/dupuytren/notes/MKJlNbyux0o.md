# Catherine Ball "Systematic Review of Non-Surgical Treatments" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**A systematic review of non-surgical treatments for early Dupuytren's disease found only 15 usable studies
out of 618 screened abstracts — all observational, all graded at the lowest levels of evidence — showing
tentative signals that intralesional steroid injection and radiotherapy improve nodule consistency and
splinting may improve digital extension, but with quality too poor to draw firm conclusions.** Ball summarizes
the state of the evidence directly: **"there were limitations to the studies — quality was poor, with no
blinding or randomization and predominantly subjective outcome measures"** `[05:06]`.

## No agreed definition of "early disease"

`[00:03]`–`[01:03]` — Ball notes there is currently **no accepted definition of early Dupuytren's disease** —
it's generally described as palmar nodules with limited or no extension deficit, with surgery usually reserved
for flexion deformity of **30 degrees or more.** For this review's own inclusion criteria, "early disease" was
operationalized as: a clearly identifiable cohort with **combined MCP and PIP flexion deformity of 60 degrees
or less** (30° or less at each joint), patients explicitly **described as having early disease** by the
original study authors, or **Tubiana grade up to 45 degrees** total digital flexion deformity.

## Search methodology

`[01:03]`–`[02:04]` — Using a **PECOS framework** (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome, Study design),
the review covered patients receiving **pharmacological treatment, physical therapy, or radiotherapy**,
measured via patient-reported outcomes, physical measures, clinical assessment, or clinical observation — and
accepted **any study design**, including randomized trials, prospective/retrospective case studies, case
series, conference abstracts, and letters. Databases were searched systematically with broad terms, covering
English-language studies **from 1957 onward**. **618 abstracts** were screened by two independent reviewers;
**66 full texts** were examined; **15 studies** were ultimately included — **8 pharmacological, 4 physical
therapy, 3 radiotherapy.**

## Evidence quality

`[02:04]`–`[03:04]` — All 15 included studies were **observational**, graded **level 4 or 5** on the Oxford
Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine scale (where 1 is high-quality evidence and 5 is the lowest). The set
comprised **10 case series and 5 case studies**; only **one** case series — by Ketchum — reported results for a
cohort composed **exclusively** of early-disease patients; every other included study mixed early-disease
patients within a broader cohort that also included more advanced disease.

## What the evidence shows, treatment by treatment

`[03:04]`–`[04:05]`:

- **Steroid injection**: three case studies on intramuscular, oral, and topical steroids were **inconclusive.**
  Ketchum's series reported observational improvement in **nodule consistency in 62 patients** following
  **intralesional triamcinolone at 6-week intervals, three injections per site**, using **ease of subsequent
  injection** as the (unblinded) outcome measure — with **50% recurrence reported at 1–3 years**, and mild
  adverse events resolving within six months.
- **Vitamin E**: results **varied**, with larger studies showing **no benefit**; all based on clinical
  observation rather than objective measurement.
- **Hyperbaric oxygen**: one case study showed **minor improvement in a single patient.**
- **Physical therapy** (splinting, ultrasound, stretching): objective measures showed a **trend toward
  improvement in splinting**, and improved grip strength following ultrasound and stretching — but **patient
  numbers across all these studies were too small to draw firm conclusions.**
- **Radiotherapy**: improved nodule consistency by clinical observation in the majority of patients in the
  largest included study (attributed to a researcher referred to in the transcript as "Keilholz," dosing at
  **6-week intervals over five sessions, total dose 30 Gray**), with improvement in nodule size, number, and
  consistency. Similar total doses were used in studies by Finney and a researcher referred to as "Lukács."
  **Toxicity was reported by two studies**, but only for their **full cohort** (including more advanced
  disease) — data specific to early-disease patients' toxicity was **not available.**

## Recommendations

`[05:06]`–`[06:07]` — Based on this review, Ball recommends: **agreeing on a clear, shared definition of early
disease**, **measuring treatment outcomes with objective, reproducible methods** rather than subjective clinical
observation, and **reporting and describing safety data in all future studies.**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Nodule consistency**, the primary outcome most consistently tracked (albeit subjectively) across the
  steroid and radiotherapy studies reviewed `[03:04]`.
- **Grip strength**, specifically noted as improved following ultrasound and stretching in the physical
  therapy literature, despite small sample sizes `[04:05]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine grading** — the evidence-quality scale used to grade all 15
  included studies, where every one fell at level 4 or 5 (the lowest tiers) `[02:04]`.
- **PECOS framework** — Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome, Study design; the structured approach used
  to define this review's search and inclusion strategy `[00:03]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Ball is unusually direct about the weakness of the entire evidence base being reviewed, not just the results:
every included study was low-evidence-grade, unblinded, and mostly used subjective outcome measures. She
explicitly separates what "appears to" show benefit (careful, hedged language for steroid injection, splinting,
and radiotherapy) from a stronger causal claim, and flags that toxicity data specific to early-disease patients
simply doesn't exist in the reviewed literature rather than assuming it would mirror the full-cohort data.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly relevant to `dp45M2IEW3k`** (Chojnowski's "splinting confers no benefit" debate talk) and
`2-Ad4vp1EOA` (Wach's "splinting is beneficial" debate talk) — this review's own splinting evidence (a trend
toward improvement, but too few patients to conclude anything) sits between those two debate talks' positions,
and its rigor-based skepticism supports Chojnowski's more evidence-cautious framing while not outright refuting
Wach's anecdotal cases. **Relevant to `rNihCBbmTx8`** (Lanting's disease clusters talk) — this review's own
methodological gap (few studies with an early-disease-only cohort, unblinded observational designs) is
precisely the kind of study design that Lanting's finding about natural disease-course fluctuation would call
into question, since none of the reviewed studies could rule out that observed "improvement" partly reflects
natural disease variation rather than treatment effect. **Relevant to `93IWNYYqCjs`** (Warwick's "what we may
never know" talk) — this review's findings extend Warwick's skepticism about comparative trial validity into
the specific, under-studied domain of early-stage non-surgical treatment, where the evidence gap is especially
stark.
