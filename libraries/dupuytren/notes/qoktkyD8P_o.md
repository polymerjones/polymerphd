# Drs. Ilse Degreef and Annelien Brauns "RCT Tension versus compression" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a pilot randomized trial, tension and compression splinting produced statistically equivalent
extension-deficit reduction over three months, but compression splinting was significantly better tolerated
and left patients meaningfully more satisfied — even though the trial wasn't designed or powered to detect a
significant difference in satisfaction.** Brauns states the tolerability gap directly: **"11 patients, or 85%
of the patients of the compression group, were very satisfied about the treatment result, and only 5 patients,
or 38%, of the tension group"** `[04:06]`.

## Why splinting at all — the mechanistic rationale

`[00:00]`–`[02:04]` — Degreef frames the study's premise: since **mechanical forces are already known to
influence Dupuytren's tissue**, and the myofibroblast is the cell driving contracture progression and
recurrence — already targeted by surgery, radiotherapy, and pharmacotherapy — **why not target it with
mechanical therapy directly, in vivo, in the patient**? The two splint designs were built on **opposite
proposed mechanisms**:

- **Tension**: mechanical tension is thought to induce myofibroblast contractile activity and collagen
  production, though continuous limited tension had separately been discussed elsewhere at the symposium as
  potentially inducing matrix-remodeling enzyme stimulation instead.
- **Compression**: inspired by burn-scar treatment, compression is proposed to trigger **myofibroblast
  apoptosis** and restore normal cell organization; spreading pressure via a silicone sheet was hypothesized
  to soften nodules and reduce contracture.

Degreef notes candidly that the study's underlying idea was "kind of malign" in intent — deliberately
comparing a treatment meant to worsen the tissue against one meant to improve it — a framing she says would
not have passed ethical review, so the trial was run instead as a comparison of two splinting *methods*.

## Trial design

`[02:04]`–`[03:05]` — **30 patients** (21 male, 9 female) with measurable flexion contractures were
randomized to tension or compression splints, worn **20 hours/day for 3 months**. Outcomes: total extension
deficit, a function/aesthetic satisfaction scale, and patient satisfaction. Mean age at presentation was 63;
mean age of disease onset was 54. Groups were comparable at baseline. **Two patients in the compression group
and two in the tension group** failed to complete the protocol (discomfort, a family member's skepticism of
non-operative treatment, and — for tension — excessive pain); analysis used an as-treated approach.

## Results

`[03:05]`–`[05:08]` — **No disease stabilization or plateau was seen — all patients improved.** Total active
extension deficit dropped significantly with both tension and compression splinting, **with no significant
difference between the two groups.** Function/aesthetic scores likewise increased significantly in both
groups from initial to final consultation; the functional score was **11% higher in the compression group**,
but the difference between groups was not statistically significant. **Satisfaction, however, clearly favored
compression** (85% vs. 38% very satisfied), though Brauns notes this difference did not reach statistical
significance given the small pilot sample. Only one patient overall quit therapy for an alternative treatment;
the rest continued splinting, most switching to nighttime-only wear to maintain the effect. The tension
splint's main downside was **pain**; the compression splint's main limitation was **difficulty achieving
adequate, consistent skin contact** across the whole surface.

## Open questions

`[05:08]`–`[06:09]` — Degreef flags that the **ideal interface pressure is not evidence-based** — most authors
suggest around 25 mmHg, but this figure isn't derived from controlled data. She also notes an important
unresolved practical question: **since it's unclear when splinting should stop**, longer-term follow-up is
needed to know whether gains are maintained or lost after discontinuing. Both approaches were reported
effective as **primary treatment and after recurrence**. On the literature: **tension splinting has some
prior published support** for gaining extension (though not for how to *maintain* the gain), while the
authors **found no prior literature at all on compression splinting** for this indication.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Splint-related pain**, specifically flagged as the tension splint's disadvantage and the reason two
  patients dropped out of that arm `[03:05]`.
- **Difficulty achieving full, consistent skin contact**, flagged as the compression splint's specific
  limitation `[05:08]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Both presenters are candid that this is explicitly a **pilot** randomized trial with a small sample (30
patients), not powered to detect a significant difference in satisfaction even though a clear numerical gap
was observed. Degreef states she was "somewhat disappointed" the two methods didn't differ in measured
outcome, but frames the result positively for patients since both approaches helped. She's explicit that
interface pressure guidance (25 mmHg) is not evidence-based, and that the trial cannot yet answer how long
splinting should continue or whether gains persist after stopping.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly relevant to this library's other conservative/non-surgical management notes** — this is a rare
head-to-head randomized comparison of two non-invasive splinting mechanisms with opposite proposed cellular
mechanisms (contraction-inducing tension versus apoptosis-inducing compression), useful context for any
splinting recommendation elsewhere in this library that doesn't specify tension versus compression design.
**Relevant to `_hoXDJ5qBFU`** (Wach & Eaton's welcome talk) — Wach's speculative observation that cords can
shrink and "vanish" when the finger is mechanically straightened is a related but distinct mechanical-force
idea; this trial supplies controlled, quantified data for a related but not identical mechanical intervention
(sustained splinting rather than acute straightening). **Relevant to `R4WPhcOpAMo`** (Selles's consensus
recurrence definition talk) — this trial's three-month follow-up and total-extension-deficit outcome measure
are both far shorter and differently defined than that consensus definition's one-year, 20-degree-threshold
standard, a limitation the presenters themselves flag when calling for longer-term follow-up.
