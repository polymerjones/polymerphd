# Dr. Marie Badalamente "Concurrent Collagenase Injections" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a 714-patient efficacy analysis across 1,448 joints, injecting two collagenase doses in the same hand
within 24–72 hours of each other reduced contracture by 74% overall, with efficacy and safety broadly similar
to single-dose historical data — except for a higher rate of skin laceration (22%) — and no evidence of
greater overall risk from treating two joints concurrently rather than one at a time.** Badalamente states the
conclusion directly: **"the concurrent administration of collagenase in the same hand decreased total
contracture and increased range of motion, and efficacy and safety were similar to prior studies, although the
skin lacerations at finger manipulation were higher... there doesn't appear to be a greater risk of using the
two doses concurrently as compared to one dose"** `[06:18]`.

## Study design

`[00:01]`–`[01:06]` — This talk reports the trial data underlying the double-dose approach discussed
clinically in a companion talk by Dr. Pess (see `1BURdqs3a4U` for his related percutaneous fasciotomy/
collagenase technique talk). **56 sites** enrolled patients with **two or more contractures of at least 20
degrees, affecting at least two joints in the same hand.** Patients received **up to 0.58mg collagenase per
injection**, with the second injection given **24–72 hours after the first**, followed by manipulation and a
**31-day follow-up.** Outcomes tracked: change in flexion contracture from baseline, range of motion, clinical
success (defined, per prior trial convention, as essentially a straight finger), clinical improvement (greater
than 50% contracture reduction), and adverse events.

## Results by joint pairing

`[02:07]`–`[03:11]` — **714 patients analyzed for efficacy, across 1,448 joints (896 MP, 552 PIP)**. Overall
contracture reduction across all treated joints: **74%.** Broken down by joint-pair combination:

| Joint pairing | Mean contracture reduction |
|---|---|
| **Two MP joints, different fingers** | 84% |
| **Two PIP joints, different fingers** | 61% |
| **One MP + one PIP, different fingers** | 68% |
| **One MP + one PIP, same finger** (largest sample) | 72% |

Significant increases in total range of motion were seen across all joint-pair combinations. Overall clinical
**success** (straight finger) rates: **64% for MP joints, 28% for PIP joints** — notably lower for PIP than
the roughly 40% single-joint PIP success rate reported in the original CORD I trial (`OwSS6grbMAc`), a
comparison Badalamente does not explicitly draw but which is directly relevant. Clinical **improvement**
(>50% contracture reduction): **89% for MP joints, 71% for PIP joints.**

## Patient satisfaction

`[04:12]`–`[05:15]` — Badalamente notes the FDA specifically requires surveying patients' own perception of
treatment, not just objective clinical measures — most patients in this study reported being **either very or
quite satisfied.** She frames this as important context for interpreting the strict >50%-reduction success
metric: a contracture reduction that falls short of that strict threshold can still leave a patient satisfied
if their hand feels functional to them in practice.

## Case examples and safety

`[05:15]`–`[06:18]` — Several case examples are shown, including a PIP-contracture case on the ring and small
fingers, a more complex case, and one patient with prior surgery. On safety: **skin laceration incidence was
somewhat higher (22%)** than typically reported for single-dose injections, echoing a point Badalamente notes
was also raised in Dr. Pess's companion talk — but this was the specific, isolated safety difference found;
no broader increase in risk was observed from treating two joints concurrently compared to treating one joint
at a time.

## Regulatory context

`[06:18]`–`[07:19]` — Badalamente notes this concurrent, double-dose approach was approved by the **US Food
and Drug Administration in 2014**, described as a very recent approval at the time of this talk, and frames
the practical benefit as allowing **more rapid treatment of two affected joints** rather than requiring
sequential, separately-scheduled single-joint treatment sessions.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Skin laceration during finger manipulation**, specifically identified as elevated (22%) relative to
  single-dose historical safety data — the one clear safety trade-off of the concurrent approach `[06:18]`.
- **Patient-perceived hand functionality**, tracked separately from the strict clinical-success contracture
  threshold, per FDA-required patient-reported outcome surveying `[04:12]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Badalamente discloses her financial relationships upfront (research support, consultancy, and partial Xiaflex
royalties for herself and Dr. Hurst). She is direct about the one clear negative finding (elevated skin
laceration rate) rather than omitting it, and explicitly contextualizes the strict clinical-success metric
against the more favorable patient satisfaction data, without claiming the two measures tell the same story.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly complements `OD7cAtaNl3c`** (Pess's own talk on the same underlying trial) — that talk isolates the
specific question of manipulation timing (24 vs. 48 vs. 72+ hours), finding no efficacy difference and actually
a lower laceration rate at 72 hours, a practical scheduling finding this talk's efficacy/safety summary does
not itself cover. **Directly complements `1BURdqs3a4U`** (Pess's percutaneous fasciotomy and collagenase technique talk) — this
talk supplies the formal trial data behind the concurrent double-dose approach Pess discusses from a technique
perspective; Badalamente explicitly frames her talk as overlapping with his. **Directly comparable to
`OwSS6grbMAc`** (Badalamente's own CORD I single-joint collagenase trial talk) — this double-dose study's
lower PIP joint clinical success rate (28% versus CORD I's roughly 40%) is a notable difference worth flagging
directly when comparing single-dose and concurrent double-dose collagenase outcomes at the PIP joint
specifically. **Relevant to `-PUJvVwlJis`** (Dias's MCID talk) — this talk's own point about patient
satisfaction diverging from strict clinical-success thresholds is a direct, practical illustration of exactly
the gap between statistically/clinically defined outcome measures and patient-perceived benefit that Dias's
talk formally quantifies.
