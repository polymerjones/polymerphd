# Likelihood of Dupuytren Contracture Recurrence After Limited Fasciectomy, Needle Aponeurotomy or Collagenase Clostridium histolyticum: Systematic Review of Prospective Data With 2- to 7-Year Follow-up

## Central claim

**Across ten prospective studies and nearly 1,700 treated joints, recurrence after Dupuytren treatment was
common regardless of method — but the three treatments recur at very different rates: limited fasciectomy
16.5%, collagenase 32.5%, needle aponeurotomy 46.4%** `[¶7]`.

## How the evidence was screened

`[¶5]`

The review is deliberately restricted to studies that could actually answer the recurrence question
cleanly: **"prospective cohort series or randomized controlled trials treating DC with LF, CCH, and/or NA,"**
requiring **"≥2-year, in-person clinical follow-up and explicit definition of contracture recurrence."**
Studies relying on telephone or questionnaire-only follow-up, non-English publications, studies with no
recurrence data at follow-up, or fewer than 15 patients were excluded — a deliberate filter against the
weaker data other reviews sometimes include.

## What it found

`[¶6]`, `[¶7]`

**Ten studies, 1,411 patients, 1,698 treated joints, mean follow-up 3.8 years.** Across studies, **"LF
demonstrated a lower risk of recurrence than both CCH and NA, and CCH demonstrated a lower risk of recurrence
than NA"** — the same ordering restated with hard numbers in the conclusion: **LF 16.5%, CCH 32.5% (an
"intermediate" position), NA 46.4% (the highest)**. **Joint location also mattered independently of
treatment**: **"Recurrence was less likely in the treatment of MCP versus PIP contractures across all
treatments,"** and this held regardless of which of the three treatments was used. Reintervention followed
the same pattern as recurrence — **"a lower likelihood of reinterventions after LF than after CCH or NA"** —
and **commonly reported complications were digital nerve injury, tendon injury, skin tears, swelling, pain,
and bleeding** `[¶6]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Recurrence of contracture after initial successful treatment**, defined explicitly per study rather than
  left to inconsistent wording `[¶5]`.
- **Need for a repeat procedure (reintervention)** as a distinct, trackable consequence of recurrence `[¶6]`.
- **Digital nerve injury, tendon injury, skin tears, swelling, pain, and bleeding** as the complications
  reported across the pooled studies `[¶6]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Needle aponeurotomy (NA)** — this review's term for the same procedure `nice-htg21-needle-fasciotomy`
  calls needle fasciotomy `[¶4]`.
- **MCP versus PIP joint** — the two joint locations whose recurrence risk this review compares directly,
  independent of which treatment was used `[¶6]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None — figures are given as direct percentages and relative comparisons.

## Source-stated confidence

The review's own clinical-relevance framing resists a simple "LF is best" reading: **"LF offers greater
durability and a lower likelihood of subsequent procedures than CCH or NA... however, the clinician must also
weigh the length of recovery and potential complications between these three treatment options"** `[¶8]`. It
also flags a specific sub-group caution: **"Patients with PIP contractures should be counseled about a higher
recurrence risk and potential need for closer follow-up"** regardless of which treatment they choose `[¶8]`.
A conflict-of-interest statement is disclosed directly in the source rather than omitted: **"Dr. Mark A.
Vitale served on the advisory board for Endo Pharmaceuticals, Inc in 2022"** — Endo being a manufacturer with
a commercial interest in collagenase — **"the other authors have no relevant conflicts to disclose"** `[¶8]`.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Converges tightly with `shaheen-2025-treatment-comparison-review`**, which reaches the identical ordering
(LF most durable, then CCH, then needle-based treatment least durable) via relative risk rather than raw
percentages — two independent systematic reviews landing on the same ranking. **Converges with
`nice-htg21-needle-fasciotomy`**, whose ~50%-at-3-to-5-years figure for needle fasciotomy sits close to this
review's 46.4% at a mean 3.8 years for the same procedure under a different name (needle aponeurotomy).
**Relevant to `detect-2026-progression-rct`**, which finds surgery outperforms needle fasciotomy and
collagenase on progression within treated fingers specifically — consistent in direction, though that trial
measures progression of contracture angle rather than binary recurrence.
