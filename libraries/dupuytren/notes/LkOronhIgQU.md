# Dr. Charles Eaton: "2022 Dupuytren Disease and Research for a Cure" — Full Lecture

## Central claim

**Eaton argues the single biggest obstacle to better Dupuytren's treatment is the total absence of a
biomarker test — without one, testing whether any candidate drug actually works requires clinical trials so
large and so slow that none have ever been affordable — and he demonstrates this concretely by walking through
the actual patient numbers a realistic prevention trial would require, before reporting the real, current
enrollment status of his own group's biomarker pilot study.** He states this directly: **"the lack of a
biomarker test is the single biggest factor [in why] we're not developing better treatments for Dupuytren"**
`[59:18]`.

*Note: this talk shares core material with `kLYBA2hLRPs`, `sAs4b1pNjEU`, and `JJk42abIEeQ` (Eaton's other full
lectures) — anatomy, general epidemiology, systems biology framing, and biomarker rationale. This note
concentrates on what is new or substantially more developed here.*

## Debunking "Viking disease"

`[12:14]`–`[13:15]` — Eaton directly rebuts the popular claim that Dupuytren's is a "Viking disease" or "Celtic
disease," tracing the myth to idle textbook speculation that got repeated uncritically. He cites a **2015
study published in Nature by Leslie**, a genetic-archaeology study tracking migration patterns into the
British Isles: mapping regions with high concentrations of Norwegian- and Danish-derived surnames against a
**2019 study of Dupuytren's prevalence across the British Isles** showed **no relationship between the two.**
He adds that Vikings themselves were not a genetically homogeneous group (they employed mercenaries), and that
"Celtic" itself denotes a **culture, not a genetic lineage** — so neither "Viking" nor "Celtic" heritage
specifically predicts risk. The real risk factor is simply **broad European ancestry.**

## An evolutionary argument: why do Dupuytren's genes persist?

`[16:20]`–`[19:23]` — Eaton poses a deliberately provocative question: since Dupuytren's genes impair health,
why haven't they been bred out of the population? His answer, by analogy: **sickle cell trait** persists in
populations with high malaria exposure because carrying one (not two) copies confers malaria resistance — a
genetic cost of doing business against a bigger threat. He cites a **recent Nature study** finding genes
associated with **inflammatory bowel disease** (including Crohn's) were **enriched in survivors of the Black
Death**, because those same immune-related genes improved survival odds against the plague. Applying the same
logic to Dupuytren's: its persistence at such high population frequency suggests the associated genes likely
conferred **some unknown survival advantage** against an unidentified historical environmental threat. His
framing: **"if you have Dupuytren's, it's not a flaw — it's a badge that your ancestors were survivors."**

## A two-hit model, with epigenetic aging as the dominant trigger

`[19:23]`–`[22:27]` — Genetic risk alone is necessary but not sufficient — Dupuytren's is framed as a **"two-
hit" disease**, like other genetic conditions, requiring genetic predisposition plus a second triggering
factor. The **single biggest second-hit factor is simply the passage of time**: age-related **epigenetic
changes** (which genes get turned on or off as part of normal aging, not just tissue "wearing out") are
proposed as the dominant trigger. Other risk factors move the needle only modestly: **hand injury and chronic
heavy manual labor** (specifically noted to require **at least 10 years of daily heavy labor** before any
measurable effect), plus chronic heavy drinking and smoking.

## Obesity as a protective factor

`[21:26]`–`[22:27]` — A genuinely counterintuitive finding: **the average Dupuytren's patient is somewhat
underweight** compared to age-matched people without the disease, and **multiple overlapping studies have
found obesity to be protective** — obese people are **less likely to develop Dupuytren's and less likely to
need surgery for it.** Strikingly, a study following patients through **successful bariatric (weight-loss)
surgery** found that **losing weight removed this protection**, increasing their odds of developing
Dupuytren's afterward — a finding Eaton flags as still unexplained.

## Natural history, with a specific average timeline

`[22:27]`–`[24:32]` — Restating the natural-history breakdown found in Eaton's other lectures (~1 in 10 nodules
resolve; ~2 in 10 progress to needing treatment; ~7 in 10 show no significant change over 10 years), this talk
adds a specific figure: **when a nodule does progress all the way to a contracture needing treatment, the
average time for that to happen is about 8 years** — with wide individual variation. He is explicit that
**rate of progression after** a contracture has developed remains essentially unstudied, as does the length of
the **pre-clinical phase** before any nodule appears (which could be brief or span decades).

## Why the PIP joint is a "troublemaker" — a geometric explanation

`[27:36]`–`[29:40]` — Beyond simply noting PIP joints are harder to treat, Eaton gives the mechanical reason:
the amount of joint-angle change produced by a given amount of cord shortening depends on the **cord's
distance from the joint's axis of rotation** — the closer the cord runs to the pivot point, the more angle
change results from the same shortening. Cords are generally **closer to the pivot point at the PIP joint than
at the MCP joint**, meaning PIP-joint cords produce disproportionately large angle changes for a given amount
of biological activity — a specific structural explanation for that joint's outsized clinical problems.

## Diathesis reconsidered: no evidence it predicts pre-surgical progression

`[34:49]`–`[35:51]` — Eaton reviews the standard **diathesis factors** (strong family history, early onset,
disease outside the palm) developed to predict **post-surgical** recontracture risk. He directly challenges a
common extrapolation: the assumption that diathesis-positive patients must also progress **faster before**
any surgery. He states plainly: **"there's no evidence to back that up,"** and that in his own studies and
data, **pre-surgical progression rate does not appear to be influenced by family history, age of onset, or
disease outside the palm** — the apparent "break point" for these factors seems to be specifically tied to
having had a corrective procedure, not the natural disease course beforehand. (This is a related but distinct
claim from the rate-of-progression *reversal* finding developed in `sAs4b1pNjEU`.)

## Measurement problems: the tabletop test and a hidden joint

`[36:52]`–`[39:56]` — The standard **tabletop test** ("come back when your palm won't lie flat") has a specific
flaw: if a patient can **hyperextend their MCP joint**, that hyperextension can **mask a significant, already-
worsening PIP contracture** underneath it, causing the patient to miss the 20–40-degree treatment window at
that joint. A larger, previously undiscussed problem: the **carpometacarpal (CMC) joint** at the base of the
ring and little fingers — responsible for cupping the palm and enabling a secure three-point pinch, with about
40 degrees of its own motion — **cannot be measured by standard technique** and is frequently involved when
cords span multiple joints. Eaton demonstrates directly: manually straightening a patient's CMC joint first
reveals a **40-degree MCP contracture** that appeared absent when the CMC joint's own flexion was masking it —
pushing hard enough to visibly **blanch the fingertips.** His conclusion: current goniometer-based measurement
systematically **cannot capture** this joint's contribution to overall hand function.

## The practical treatment ladder actually used

`[42:59]`–`[48:05]` — Given how unreliable both biology (diathesis) and anatomy (angle) measurement are in
practice, real-world triage is more empirical than the "ideal" four-quadrant severity model (mild/mild →
minimally invasive; severe mechanical/mild biology → fasciectomy or skeletal traction; mild mechanical/severe
biology → dermofasciectomy; severe/severe → combined approach) would suggest. In practice: **start with a
minimally invasive procedure regardless of assessed biology or anatomy**, since it's low-risk; if it works and
lasts at least a year, repeat as needed; if it fails or doesn't last a year, **step up to open fasciectomy**;
repeat open procedures carry a **significant complication rate**, so repeating them requires a strong
justification. For recurrent, especially PIP-joint contractures ("they are the devil"), options include:
**accepting the current state** (PIP contracture sometimes self-limits as surrounding ligaments absorb further
stress); the **digit widget** (an external fixation device attached to the middle phalanx, connected via rubber
bands to a hand splint, worn **6–10 weeks** to stretch the tissue — not a permanent fix on its own, since
roughly half recontract quickly if not followed by an open procedure and often dermofasciectomy); **joint
fusion** (a salvage procedure trading full PIP function for a fixed, less-obstructive position — with a real
downside for activities needing a full fist, like racket sports or golf); and, as a genuine last resort,
**amputation.** Eaton repeats his standing argument that **failed Dupuytren's surgery remains the most common
reason for elective finger amputation**, calling this the strongest possible case for needing better treatment
options for what is otherwise a benign, usually painless condition.

## Recontracture statistics, restated

`[48:05]`–`[49:05]` — Annualized recontracture rates: **10–20% per year after minimally invasive procedures,
5–10% per year after fasciectomy, roughly 2% per year after dermofasciectomy** — consistent with the figures
given in `sAs4b1pNjEU`. Full correction (rather than partial) at the time of the initial procedure predicts
lower subsequent recontracture risk.

## Practical, low-risk mechanical advice

`[50:08]`–`[51:08]` — Since no lifestyle factor (aside from very heavy, chronic manual labor) has strong
evidence of changing progression rate, Eaton offers modest, low-risk suggestions grounded in mechanical-stress
avoidance: **padded gloves** for heavy activity, **pull-up bar handles** rather than gripping a bare bar,
**push-up handles or light dumbbells** rather than a flat-hand downward-dog stretch position, and **wider
grips on tennis rackets and golf clubs** to reduce concentrated mechanical stress on Dupuytren's-prone tissue.
He is explicit these are reasonable, low-risk measures, not proven disease-modifying interventions.

## Two contrasting hands, and a research-design analogy

`[52:08]`–`[55:12]` — Eaton contrasts two patients: one with **soft skin and thin, thread-like, well-defined
cords** and nodules but no contracture for years; another with **hard skin fused directly to broad, poorly-
defined cords**, no history of ever having a nodule, actively straining hard enough to blanch the skin. He
frames the unresolved question directly: **what's biologically different between these two presentations**,
and states plainly that **no simple test currently exists** to answer it. He explains this gap using a
**"water on the floor"** analogy: **surgeons are called in for damage control after the mess has already
happened**, while **medical doctors aim to prevent complications by intervening at the biological source** —
and this structural difference is why medical research (built around biomarkers) and surgical research (built
around anatomy and technique) have developed such different tools.

## The concrete case for why current research is impractical

`[55:12]`–`[59:18]` — Eaton works through two hypothetical trial designs in specific numeric detail:

- A **nodule-to-contracture prevention trial**: enrolling **1,000 patients** (500 treatment, 500 placebo),
  waiting **at least 5 years**, then comparing angle-based outcomes — logistically enormous given how many
  nodules simply resolve on their own regardless of treatment.
- A **more targeted post-procedure recontracture trial**, focused specifically on **isolated PIP joints**
  (excluding multi-joint cords), treated with **collagenase** (to remove surgeon skill as a confound), among
  patients who achieved **full initial correction**: since roughly 70% of Xiaflex-treated PIP patients get a
  straight finger and 30% don't, and only about **1 in 5** Dupuytren's patients has an isolated PIP contracture
  in the first place, reaching a usable sample of **220 patients** would require starting from **over 1,400
  patients** — still a large, expensive undertaking.

His conclusion: **of all the drugs and treatments that might in theory modify Dupuytren's biology, none has
been shown to prevent progression or recurrence — not because none of them work, but because testing any of
them properly, without a biomarker, is currently unaffordable.**

## Reframing nodule treatment as a risk/benefit decision

`[59:18]`–`[61:18]` — Eaton distinguishes two different reasons patients seek nodule treatment. For the
minority with **actual symptoms** (pain, tenderness, itching from inflammation), there are treatments with a
**known, predictable benefit** — anti-inflammatory approaches, cortisone injection, and **radiotherapy**,
which he notes is "fairly effective" at reducing these inflammatory symptoms — making a real risk/benefit
calculation possible. For patients seeking treatment purely out of **anxiety about future progression**, no
treatment has a **known benefit** in preventing that progression, making it hard to justify taking on any real
risk. His practical suggestion for anxious-but-asymptomatic patients: favor **lower-risk options** (topical
treatments, nutritional supplements) over riskier interventions, reframing the conversation explicitly around
whether the risk/benefit ratio is actually justifiable.

## Why biopsy- and culture-based biomarker research don't work either

`[61:18]`–`[62:20]` — Beyond blood-based approaches, two other research avenues are explicitly ruled out:
**biopsying a nodule directly is risky**, since the biopsy itself can provoke or aggravate progression, making
it an unjustifiable isolated research measurement; and **growing Dupuytren's tissue in a lab dish** introduces
its own **culture artifacts** on top of the real biology (echoing the culture-reliability concerns raised in
`6aG_CIYTX8A`) — Eaton's own framing: you can't tell whether you're studying **"Bruce Banner or the Hulk."**
This leaves blood-based systemic biomarkers as the remaining viable research avenue, via the same bloodstream/
bone-marrow signaling mechanism described in `sAs4b1pNjEU`.

## The pilot study's actual status

`[65:28]`–`[67:31]` — As of this talk, the **International Dupuytren Data Bank's first blood biomarker
discovery pilot study** — enrolled via the free, crowdsourced online registry — had **45 participants: 25
with Dupuytren's disease, 20 controls**, running a range of gene- and protein-related blood tests. Eaton notes
the study launched **coincident with the onset of COVID-19**, requiring course adjustments, but was proceeding.
He illustrates the scale of the remaining analytical challenge with a protein-interaction diagram deliberately
simplified for the talk, noting the full, fully-documented interaction network would look **"like the sky on a
cloudless night."** He closes by restating that the **National Institutes of Health does not fund discovery
research** — only research building on existing pilot data — leaving the group **entirely dependent on
charitable donations** at this stage.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Fingertip blanching when a clinician manually corrects a patient's hidden CMC joint contracture**, used as
  direct visual evidence of a measurement blind spot in standard contracture assessment `[38:56]`.
- **Pain, tenderness, and itching in nodules**, distinguished explicitly from anxiety about future progression
  as two different reasons patients seek treatment, each warranting a different risk/benefit calculation
  `[59:18]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Two-hit disease model** — genetic predisposition plus a triggering factor (most likely age-related
  epigenetic change), required together to produce disease `[19:23]`.
- **CMC (carpometacarpal) joint** — a previously unmeasured joint at the base of the ring/little finger,
  identified here as a specific, concrete gap in standard Dupuytren's contracture assessment `[37:55]`.
- **Digit widget** — an external skeletal traction device gradually stretching a severely contracted PIP joint
  over 6–10 weeks, typically followed by an open procedure `[45:00]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Sickle cell trait and malaria resistance**, and **Black Death survivors and inflammatory bowel disease
  genes** — both used to argue Dupuytren's genetic persistence likely reflects an unknown historical survival
  advantage `[17:21]`–`[18:22]`.
- **"Water on the floor"** — surgeons as damage control after the fact, medical doctors aiming to prevent the
  spill at its source `[54:10]`.
- **"Bruce Banner or the Hulk"** — the uncertainty of whether cultured Dupuytren's tissue reflects real disease
  biology or lab-culture artifact `[62:20]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Eaton is explicit that the evolutionary "survival advantage" argument is speculative reasoning by analogy, not
a proven finding specific to Dupuytren's — he states directly he doesn't know what the original environmental
threat might have been. He flags the obesity-protection finding as replicated across "several different
overlapping studies" yet still unexplained mechanistically. He directly and explicitly contradicts a common
inferential leap about diathesis (that post-surgical risk factors must also predict pre-surgical progression),
citing his own data against it. The hypothetical trial-design numbers are presented as a deliberately concrete,
worked calculation specifically to justify the claim that current research is financially impractical, not as
an abstract complaint. He reports the pilot study's real enrollment numbers plainly, including the COVID-related
disruption, rather than only describing the project in aspirational terms.
