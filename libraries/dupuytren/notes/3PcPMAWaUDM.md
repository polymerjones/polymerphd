# Dr. Bert Reichert "Favorite Options of German Hand Surgeons" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**A national survey of German hand surgeons found limited fasciectomy dominates treatment across all disease
stages, with needle fasciotomy playing a much smaller role even in early-stage disease — and, in sharp
contrast to the US, collagenase is effectively absent from German practice because Germany's federal health
committee (the GBA) decided in 2011 that treatment costs would not be reimbursed by statutory health
insurance.** Reichert states this policy fact directly: **"other than in the US and other countries, in
Germany there is practically no potential for treating contractures with collagenase, due to the decision of
the GBA that treatment costs are not to be reimbursed by health insurance funds"** `[05:06]`.

## The policy backdrop

`[00:00]`–`[01:02]` — Germany's **Federal Joint Committee (GBA)** issues binding directives determining which
services are reimbursed for over 70 million statutorily insured people. In 2011, the GBA assessed collagenase
for Dupuytren's disease; existing studies had shown **percutaneous needle fasciotomy (PNF) was equally
effective as fasciectomy in Tubiana stages 1 and 2, while being less invasive**, leading the GBA to designate
PNF as the appropriate option for those early stages — a framing Reichert notes is **"certainly an
oversimplification,"** but one his survey adopted as its structuring framework regardless.

## Survey methodology

`[01:02]`–`[02:03]` — An internet-based questionnaire was sent three times during the first quarter of the
survey year to **530 members of the German Society for Surgery of the Hand**, achieving a **20.8% response
rate**, representing **9,761 treatments — about 24.4% of the roughly 40,000 Dupuytren's procedures performed
annually in Germany.** Reichert deliberately kept the questionnaire short and simple to maximize response
rate, asking surgeons for their **favorite treatment option by Tubiana stage.**

## Results by stage

`[02:03]`–`[03:05]` — **45 surgeons reported not treating stage 1 patients at all**, and **9 would not treat
stage 2** either. **Nearly all surgeons favored open surgery for stage 3** — a result Reichert cites as
evidence of the survey's internal reliability, since it matches clinical expectation. In **stage 4, 7
surgeons reported treatment options outside those the questionnaire had proposed**, left unspecified. Asked
for their main decision criterion: **low complication risk mattered specifically in stages 1 and 2**, while
**best possible outcome was the dominant criterion in stages 3 and 4.** Overall, **limited fasciectomy was the
most frequently recommended treatment across the board**, with PNF playing a comparatively minor role even in
the earlier stages it's specifically indicated for.

## Volume-weighted analysis and practice patterns

`[03:05]`–`[04:05]` — Individual surgeon patient volumes ranged from **2 to 400 patients per year**. A
sensitivity analysis weighting results by patient volume (a coefficient per case/year) produced essentially
**no difference** from the unweighted results. Patient numbers were **not normally distributed** — a large
number of surgeons treat about one patient per week, while a small number treat considerably more. Splitting
respondents into **86 surgeons treating up to 100 patients/year** versus **24 surgeons treating more than
100/year** (with broadly similar total patient counts in each group) revealed a practice-setting difference:
**lower-volume surgeons treat more often within a hospital setting**, while **higher-volume surgeons perform
PNF more often specifically in stage 2 cases** — Reichert interprets this as reflecting that high-volume
surgeons see more early-stage, outpatient cases overall.

## PNF's role and history

`[04:05]`–`[05:06]` — **34 surgeons (30.9% of respondents) reported performing PNF at all**; it is well
accepted specifically for stages 1 and 2, though some surgeons also use it in stage 4. Reichert traces PNF's
German history to its introduction in the **1970s**, crediting **Albrecht Meinel** (see `kq4hr06tOtY`) as the
first to popularize the method in Germany, and notes all recent German textbooks now cover PNF as an
appropriate treatment — evidence, he states, that **PNF has increasing importance among German hand
surgeons.**

## Source-stated confidence

Reichert discloses a prior relationship with Pfizer (conducting studies in 2011–2012) as context for his
familiarity with the GBA process, though this survey itself is presented as independent. He is explicit about
the survey's methodological limits — a 20.8% response rate, a deliberately minimal question set to maximize
participation, and treats the near-unanimous stage 3 open-surgery preference as an internal validity check
rather than a novel finding. He is candid that the GBA's stage 1/2-versus-PNF framing is an oversimplification
even though the survey itself was structured around it.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly comparable to `wUK_0ab9HR0`** (Dias's UK epidemiology talk) — both are national practice-pattern
studies from the same general period; Dias's English data shows an 8-fold variation in surgical rate between
hospitals attributed to preference rather than evidence, a similar theme to this talk's finding that German
hand surgeons' treatment choice is often driven by personal criteria (low complication risk versus best
outcome) rather than a single evidence-based standard. **Directly contrasts with `3f0LUyPiPfg`** (Blazar's US collagenase treatment patterns talk) — the two talks
describe starkly different national outcomes for the same drug over the same general period: collagenase grew
to roughly 30% of US Dupuytren's treatment by 2013, while this talk reports it as **practically unavailable in
Germany** due to a specific, named reimbursement policy decision (the GBA's 2011 ruling) — a clear
illustration of how national health-payer policy, not just clinical evidence, shapes real-world treatment
availability. **Directly relevant to `kq4hr06tOtY`** (Meinel's pathogenesis talk) — this talk credits Meinel
directly as the surgeon who first popularized PNF in Germany in the 1970s, adding historical context to that
separate talk's own theoretical and technical contributions. **Relevant to `vOSY4FZEJGM`** (Erne's needle
fasciotomy outcome talk) and `-32nd2vQexQ` (Gan's needle aponeurotomy talk) — both are German-language-region
needle fasciotomy series that sit within the broader German practice-pattern context this survey describes.
