# Heinrich Seegenschmiedt MD, "Radiotherapy for Primary or Recurrent Morbus Ledderhose" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Ledderhose disease is treated far worse by surgery than its hand counterpart — largely because so few
surgeons ever operate on it — and radiotherapy achieves durable, well-quantified benefit that Seegenschmiedt
argues deserves the same attention Dupuytren's already receives.** At a median follow-up of almost 6 years:
**"the primary endpoint, no progression, is achieved in 91% of the cases"** `[11:18]`, with **84% of
patients reporting satisfaction** with treatment `[13:22]`.

## Naming, prevalence, and a cross-specialty blind spot

`[00:03]`–`[02:04]` — Named for Georg Lederhose, the Munich surgeon who first described it. Seegenschmiedt's
own examined population: **about 20% of Dupuytren's patients have Ledderhose disease, and about 50% of
Ledderhose patients have Dupuytren's** — framed as evidence Ledderhose signals a **higher-penetrance
Dupuytren's diathesis**, worth actively checking for. He illustrates the blind spot directly: a colleague
"who knew his hands quite well" didn't know he had Ledderhose disease on his own feet — **"we are not aware
of palpating our own feet."** He notes the specialty divide bluntly: **"have you ever met a podiatrist on a
hand surgeons meeting? And vice versa? They don't communicate."**

## Why surgical outcomes on the foot are worse than the hand

`[02:04]`–`[04:08]` — Standard surgical options (local excision, wide excision, subtotal or total plantar
fasciectomy) are typically performed by podiatrists, not hand surgeons, and Seegenschmiedt states **results
are "even worse than... the hand,"** attributing this to the operation's rarity — **"it's done by lay
people in the periphery, and it's not accumulated in centers"** — and to the practical problem that a
healing foot still has to bear weight. He notes the largest historical surgical series before his own work
was only about 50 cases, reported by Lederhose himself in 1897 and 1902, with most published series since
still under 20–25 cases.

## His cohort and their risk profile

`[05:09]`–`[06:12]` — Demographics differ notably from Dupuytren's hand disease: **roughly a 1:1 male:female
ratio** (versus hand disease's well-documented male skew), **median age 56**, and **substantial bilateral
involvement — 222 feet affected versus only 54 unaffected** among followed patients. Of those with at least
one year of follow-up (138 patients), **91 patients (126 feet) received radiotherapy**; 47 declined and
served as an unrandomized control group (dose concept not yet established at the time). Risk factors in the
treated cohort: **about a third were smokers**, with knuckle pads, keloids, diabetes, epilepsy medication,
and — notably — **seven patients with liver disorders (including prior hepatitis and cirrhosis)**; **about
11–12% reported a prior foot injury or trauma.**

## Measurement and treatment protocol

`[07:13]`–`[10:16]` — Outcomes were tracked via **photographic documentation with a ruler** (an initial
photocopy-machine approach was abandoned as impractical), a **visual analog scale for symptoms**, and MRI or
ultrasound where needed — noting plantar nodules sit **deeper than Dupuytren's palmar nodules**, requiring
radiation depth coverage up to **2cm**. Across the cohort: **324 total nodules counted (1–5 per patient,
median 2×2cm, up to 6.5cm)**, and **142 cords in 46 patients** (cords were less common overall, which
Seegenschmiedt reads as **a marker of less advanced disease** than extensive nodularity). Treatment protocol
mirrored the hand: **10 fractions of 3 Gray, given as two 5-day courses 8–12 weeks apart**, using
orthovoltage photons or a linear accelerator, with lead shielding customized to protect non-target tissue —
**each session lasting about 3 minutes (or as little as 15 seconds on a linear accelerator)**, with setup
being the most time-consuming part; patients were in and out of the department within about 15 minutes.

## Results at ~6-year median follow-up

`[11:18]`–`[13:22]` — **91% achieved the primary endpoint of no progression**; of the **11 cases that did
progress, 50% ultimately required surgery.** Roughly half of stable cases held steady in nodule size/count,
and half showed **objective remission**: **nodule count reduced by 33% on average**, with the most dramatic
individual response in a child treated for a nodule that shrank from 6×7cm to 2×3cm over about 9 years.
Cords showed softening or reduction in **45% of cases**, with measurable shrinkage in length. **Symptom
improvement was the most dramatic outcome category, reported by 60–80% of patients** — Seegenschmiedt
attributes this to both direct symptom relief and the resulting restoration of normal foot arch shape as
nodules shrank. **Overall patient satisfaction: 84%**, closely tracking the stabilization/improvement rate.
Acute toxicity (dryness, erythema, itching) occurred in about 20% and was **more common in previously
operated (recurrent) lesions than untreated primary ones**; chronic skin changes (mild dryness,
discoloration) occurred in about 15%, manageable with moisturizing.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Pressure, tension, and pain while walking or at rest, plus itching sensation and walking difficulty**,
  the primary patient-reported complaints in plantar fibroma disease `[06:12]`.
- **Deep, harder-to-palpate nodules compared to Dupuytren's**, requiring imaging support (MRI/ultrasound) in
  unclear cases `[07:13]`.
- **Foot arch shape distortion from nodule bulk**, described as improving as nodules shrink under treatment
  `[12:21]`.
- **Worse acute skin reaction in previously operated (recurrent) sites** than in untreated primary disease
  `[10:16]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Progression (as a defined trial endpoint)** — explicitly specified as any nodule enlarging or newly
  appearing, any new cord, or any worsening symptom `[08:14]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Most of us stand on our foot, and we don't make handstands"** — a wry closing note on why foot disease
  deserves attention proportionate to, not lesser than, hand disease `[13:22]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Seegenschmiedt is careful to distinguish stabilization from true objective remission ("about a one-to-one
ratio between stabilizing the disease... and... objective remission") rather than combining them into one
inflated success figure, and states the control group was **not randomized** because a dose concept had not
yet been established when the study began. He also explicitly limits his own scope relative to surgery:
**"I don't want to step in [on] a very complicated surgical situation because I probably have no means to
deal with that... scarring"** — positioning radiotherapy as complementary, particularly for recurrent
disease, not a wholesale surgical replacement.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Substantially extends the Ledderhose summary in `oXR_NOAaSa0`** (Seegenschmiedt's other 2010 talk) — the
patient counts there (138 with 1+ year follow-up, 222 involved feet, 54 uninvolved) describe the same
underlying cohort at an earlier, shorter (1-year) follow-up snapshot; this talk reports the same program's
results at a far longer ~6-year median follow-up, with substantially more granular outcome data (91%
no-progression, 84% satisfaction, specific nodule/cord reduction percentages) not given in the other note.
**Consistent with `nLR3nKuAufs`** (Hellstrom's Peyronie's talk) and **`5fqC-xS8WhM`** (Spilken's cryotherapy
talk) — both describe other non-surgical approaches to fibrotic conditions in adjacent anatomical territory,
reinforcing this talk's broader point that minimally invasive, non-excisional treatment options for
Dupuytren's-family fibrotic conditions are consistently outperforming traditional surgery across multiple
independent research groups and modalities.
