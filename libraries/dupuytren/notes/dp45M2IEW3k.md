# Dr. Adrian Chojnowski "Debate: Splinting Confers No Benefit" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Arguing the "no benefit" side of a formal debate, Chojnowski presents three randomized trials — his own
SCoRD study, a similarly designed Collis study, and a stricter Dutch study with no crossover allowed — that
together found no statistically significant benefit to adding night splinting to routine hand therapy after
surgery, though one single non-operative paper suggested modest benefit for motivated, non-surgical
patients.** He states the conclusion directly: **"there is no evidence to support the addition of night
splinting to routine hand therapy practice"** post-surgery, closing with a deliberately blunt framing:
**"this is head over heart — the science says no"** `[05:06]`.

## A laboratory rationale against splinting tension

`[00:00]` — Chojnowski opens with a laboratory finding offered as a conceptual, not clinical, argument:
**pulling on fibroblasts/myofibroblasts in culture causes them to pull back harder** — a tensile-stress
response he explicitly flags as "only a laboratory model" that "may not apply so much clinically," but offers
as an interesting reason to question whether increasing tensile load (as splinting does) is actually
beneficial to this specific cell biology.

## The one paper on non-operative splinting

`[00:00]`–`[01:00]` — For patients **not undergoing surgery**, Chojnowski identifies only one relevant paper —
a study from the London, Ontario unit — using **nighttime splinting combined with exercises and friction
massage** in **13 patients with PIP joint contractures**, followed over roughly a year, showing about **15
degrees of improvement** and some qualitative changes. He notes this aligns with patient-reported benefit
described in the symposium's own patient survey (`LVxj5lEVnz0`). His caveats: this applies specifically to a
**motivated, compliant patient with mild-to-moderate contracture**; the splint was **combined with other hand
therapies**, making its independent contribution unclear; and the study didn't examine what happens after
the splint is discontinued.

## Three randomized trials of post-surgical splinting

`[02:01]`–`[05:06]` — Three RCTs are presented in sequence, each testing whether adding a **night splint** to
standard post-surgical hand therapy improves outcomes:

1. **SCoRD (Chojnowski's own study, East Anglia)**: 154 patients randomized preoperatively to night splint
   (worn 6 months) or no splint, both receiving standard hand therapy, with one-year follow-up. **13 patients
   crossed over** (8 due to surgeon concern despite no actual evidence of recontracture) — about 17% crossover
   — analyzed under **intention-to-treat**. Results: no meaningful difference in DASH scores; **active
   extension showed no statistically significant difference** between splint and no-splint groups; patient
   satisfaction was high in both, and adherence to the splint (when prescribed) was good. Chojnowski's stated
   conclusion from this trial: routine night splinting for every patient isn't necessary — a **wait-and-see**
   approach is safe, with splinting reserved for a small subgroup if needed.
2. **Collis study**: same core methodology, a somewhat smaller sample, splint worn for **three months**, with
   3 patients crossing over. Result: the **hand-therapy-only group did numerically better** (33 degrees better
   active extension than the splint group), though **not statistically significant**; patient satisfaction
   trended **higher in the hand-therapy-only group**, with patients trending toward preferring not to wear a
   splint at all. Roughly 10% required late "wait-and-see" splinting.
3. **A Dutch study** (referred to in the transcript by a name likely garbled by auto-captions — described as
   from a Dutch institution): a stricter design with **no crossover permitted**. Splint options tested: full-
   time wear for weeks followed by nights for three months, versus hand therapy alone. Result: hand therapy
   alone performed numerically better, but again **no statistically significant difference**, including
   specifically at the **PIP joints of the little finger** — a site Chojnowski flags as particularly
   treatment-resistant ("the feared territory"). Conclusion from this study: **no difference at all** between
   splinting and no splinting, both groups receiving hand therapy.

## The unresolved question

`[05:06]` — Chojnowski closes by naming what remains unanswered: **roughly 10–17% of patients do
"recontract"** after surgery across these trials, and **who will, why, and what specifically would help them**
all remain unknown — even though splinting has traditionally been applied by default to this group.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Recontracture following surgery, in roughly 10–17% of patients across trials**, explicitly named as an
  unresolved problem regardless of the splinting conclusion `[05:06]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **SCoRD** — Chojnowski's own randomized night-splinting trial from East Anglia `[02:01]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Head over heart"** — Chojnowski's framing for choosing the evidence-based conclusion over the intuitive,
  emotionally appealing preference for a low-risk adjunctive therapy `[00:00]`, `[05:06]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Chojnowski is explicit this is a formal **debate** talk arguing one assigned side, not a neutral review — he
opens by asking listeners to "leave your prejudices aside" and closes by directly naming the tension between
intuitive appeal and trial evidence. He is careful to distinguish the one favorable non-operative-splinting
study's real limitations (small sample, splint combined with other therapies, no post-discontinuation data)
from the three larger, more rigorous post-surgical RCTs, and does not claim the debate resolves what causes
recontracture or how to identify at-risk patients — he states plainly this remains unknown.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly relevant to `rNihCBbmTx8`** (Lanting's disease clusters talk) — that talk finds Dupuytren's disease
course fluctuates substantially even without treatment, meaning any apparent splinting benefit in a study
without a no-treatment comparison arm could partly reflect natural disease-course variation rather than a true
splinting effect; this strengthens the case for the randomized-trial evidence this talk relies on over
anecdotal or uncontrolled reports. **Directly paired with `2-Ad4vp1EOA`** (Wach's "Debate: Splinting is Beneficial," the opposing side of this
same formal symposium debate) — Wach's anecdotal cases and small study more often describe splinting for
recurrence-prone joints or as a standalone preventive measure, distinct from the routine post-surgical
splinting these three RCTs specifically test; read together, the two talks may be more reconcilable than
opposed. **Directly relevant to `qoktkyD8P_o`** (Degreef & Brauns's tension-versus-compression splinting RCT) — that
trial found both splint types produced significant extension-deficit improvement over three months in a
non-surgical population, a result that sits somewhat in tension with this talk's more skeptical framing;
the two are reconcilable since Chojnowski's most skeptical conclusions are specifically about **post-surgical**
splinting, while Degreef & Brauns study splinting as a **primary, non-operative** treatment — closer to the
one favorable paper Chojnowski himself cites. **Relevant to `LVxj5lEVnz0`** (Wach's patient survey talk) —
Chojnowski explicitly references that survey's patient-reported splinting benefit as part of his discussion of
the non-operative splinting literature, directly linking self-reported patient perception to the single
supporting study he could find. **Relevant to any note discussing post-surgical recurrence prediction** — the
open question Chojnowski names (who recontracts, and why) remains genuinely unresolved across the notes in
this library, worth flagging wherever a note discusses recurrence risk factors.
