# British Dupuytren Society Seven Questions

## Central claim

**Almost nothing about Dupuytren's treatment is evidence-based except the choice between minimally
invasive and open corrective procedures — everything upstream of that (prevention) and much alongside it
(hand therapy, massage, supplements) rests on theory or anecdote, and Eaton is explicit that this is a
structural problem, not a knowledge gap that better studies alone would fix: "we don't have Dupuytren's
biomarkers yet... [without one] we can't predict which patients might benefit and which won't"**
`[00:00]`, `[09:15]`. He opens by naming this directly: **"we're still in an age of profound ignorance about
the basic nature of Dupuytren's disease... we have to resist our natural tendencies to oversimplify and to
see patterns where there are none"** `[00:00]`.

## Who to see, and how to vet them

`[00:00]`–`[03:06]` — **A hand surgery specialist, before contracture is even present**, for two reasons:
ruling out look-alike diagnoses (**tendinitis, flexor tendon sheath cysts, flexor tendon bowstringing,
diabetic stiff hand syndrome, atypical mycobacterial infections, reflex sympathetic dystrophy, giant cell
tumors, sarcomas**, and others — noting tendinitis can coexist with Dupuytren's in the same finger and
change treatment) and speed to diagnosis (**"the average Dupuytren's patient will see two or three doctors
over the course of sometimes several years before getting the right diagnosis"**). Any surgeon who operates
on hands can call themselves a hand surgeon, but **hand surgery specialists have additional post-residency
fellowship training** specifically relevant to handling **complications and recurrence** — which Eaton
calls the real differentiator, not routine outcomes. His concrete advice for vetting a surgeon: **ask where
they trained, what they'd do if the finger loses circulation mid-procedure, and what happens if the
contracture returns worse than before** — and treat vague answers ("don't worry," "nothing can be done,"
deflection to a colleague) as a reason to look elsewhere.

## Preventive treatment: blocked by the missing biomarker

`[04:06]`–`[07:12]` — Eaton states plainly there are **no evidence-based preventive treatments**, and
explains why testing one is so hard without a biomarker: **"you'd need to study at least a thousand people
with early disease, treat half with a preventive treatment and half with a placebo, and then see how bent
their fingers are five years later... that hasn't been done anywhere on this scale, with any treatment,
including radiotherapy."** A partial workaround, credited to Dr. Ilse Degreef: since recurrence after
surgery in high-risk patients is fast and predictable, her trials substitute **post-surgical recurrence**
for **early-disease progression** as the measured outcome, cutting the needed timeline to about a year.
Two results from this approach, both real but both time-limited:

- **Tamoxifen** (an estrogen-blocking breast cancer drug), tested against placebo after surgery in
  high-recurrence-risk patients, **reduced early recurrence** — replicated after percutaneous needle
  fasciotomy too. Eaton calls this **"the first proof that medicine could slow down recurrence"** — but
  flags tamoxifen's side-effect profile as too risky for long-term use, and the benefit **stopped once the
  drug stopped**.
- **Cortisone injections every three months for a year** after percutaneous needle fasciotomy (Dr. Paul
  Binhammer), showed the same pattern: **slower recurrence only while injections continued.**

## Corrective treatment: the actual evidence base

`[07:12]`–`[08:13]` — Two families of corrective procedure. **Minimally invasive** (collagenase, needle
fasciotomy) versus **open** (fasciotomy, dermofasciectomy). Short-term straightening is roughly comparable
between the two, with open surgery somewhat better for severe contractures — but the real differences are
elsewhere: **"fasciotomy lasts over twice as long as minimally invasive procedures"** before recurrence,
open surgery recovery takes **months versus days-to-weeks**, and **"the risk of permanent complications
after fasciotomy is almost 10 times higher than minimally invasive procedures"** — which Eaton states as
the direct rationale for starting with a minimally invasive procedure as first-line treatment. Splinting has
**two studies** showing improvement with several months of around-the-clock use, but Eaton is skeptical the
gains persist once splinting stops. (The "over twice as long" figure is broadly consistent with, though less
severe than, the specific 5-year randomized trial data in `oRdE0UKeJF0` — see that note's Conflicts section
for the discrepancy worth flagging.)

## What's not evidence-based, rated one by one

`[09:15]`–`[14:26]` — Eaton's own opinions, explicitly labeled as such ("remember, I'm not your doctor"):

| Treatment | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| **Hand therapy / splinting** | "A half a dozen well-conducted randomized, prospective, controlled" studies across different countries showed **no long-term benefit** after open surgery; one showed **aggressive splinting performed worse than gentle splinting**. Eaton still believes it helps some patients subjectively, but a biomarker would be needed to predict who. |
| **Radiotherapy** | Kills the living cells in nodules (helps roughly half the time), but **cords are collagen with few cells and don't respond** — so radiotherapy is only a theoretical option *before* cords/contracture form, and its preventive effectiveness is untestable for the same biomarker reasons as above. Complications are "rare but not zero." |
| **Cortisone shots** | Similar profile to radiotherapy — can help nodules, but contracture can still develop; **avoid once contracture is present unless paired with a straightening procedure.** |
| **Massage** | No long-term study either way; theoretically, *aggressive* massage could aggravate the disease by the same mechanical-stress logic that applies to heavy manual work. Gentle massage: "can't hurt, might help, no evidence." |
| **Avoiding heavy manual/strenuous hand use** | Grounded in real data that heavy manual work or sports **"slightly increase the chance of Dupuytren's"** — protective avoidance is theoretical, not tested. |
| **Supplements** (NAC, rutin, sulforaphane, caffeine) | Plausible from anti-fibrotic biology, **no human studies exist** for any of them. |
| **Topical verapamil** | Marketed for Peyronie's and sold for Ledderhose, but **no evidence it works for either**, and Eaton doubts it penetrates palm skin thickness regardless. |

## Diathesis and the decision pathway

`[14:26]`–`[18:30]` — Citing Degreef again, Eaton frames the actual treatment goal as **"minimize the number
of permanent complications from Dupuytren's disease and from its treatment over the lifetime of the
patient,"** not maximize any single procedure's result — because repeat procedures carry higher complication
risk and lower success than the first one. **Diathesis factors** — **onset before age 50, close family
history, knuckle pads, Ledderhose disease, three or more digits involved** — predict faster, more
treatment-resistant recurrence, which is the stated rationale for choosing **dermofasciectomy over
fasciotomy as the first open procedure** in diathesis-positive patients, even though dermofasciectomy is the
more invasive option. Diathesis does **not**, however, predict how fast disease progresses before any
treatment starts — that remains unknown without a biomarker.

## Degree of joint bend as its own independent factor

`[17:29]`–`[18:30]` — Separate from diathesis, **how bent the joint already is changes the odds of a good
outcome.** For the PIP joint: **the effective treatment window is roughly 20–40 degrees of contracture** —
below 20 degrees, the improvement doesn't justify procedural risk; above 40 degrees, tendon damage risk
rises and can prevent a good result. The MCP joint tolerates waiting longer, with **50 degrees** described
as a common (not absolute) threshold to stop waiting.

## The reassurance: natural history after a first nodule

`[18:30]`–`[19:32]` — Eaton gives specific 10-year odds after a first nodule appears: **roughly 1 in 10
nodules regress spontaneously without treatment; about 2 in 10 (1 in 5) progress to needing a corrective
procedure; the remaining ~7 in 10 either don't change or don't progress enough to need treatment.** His own
first nodule, he notes, has shown "little change" over six years of his own watching.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **A new palm nodule, and the specific odds of it regressing, progressing, or staying stable over 10
  years** `[18:30]`.
- **Contracture recurrence after any corrective procedure, framed as a prevention problem rather than a
  treatment-failure problem** `[07:12]`.
- **PIP contracture in the 20–40 degree range as the window where a corrective procedure is worth the
  risk** `[17:29]`.
- **Knuckle pads and concurrent Ledderhose disease as diathesis markers predicting faster recurrence**
  `[16:28]`.
- **Post-procedure loss of circulation in the finger, named directly as a question to ask a prospective
  surgeon about in advance** `[03:06]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Dupuytren's diathesis** — a cluster of risk factors (early onset, family history, knuckle pads,
  Ledderhose disease, multi-digit involvement) predicting faster, more treatment-resistant recurrence
  `[16:28]`.
- **Biomarker gap** — Eaton's own recurring term for the absence of any objective, non-clinical-exam way to
  diagnose or stage Dupuytren's, which he treats as the field's central obstacle `[04:06]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Exceptionally well-hedged throughout — Eaton distinguishes evidence-based claims (the minimally invasive
versus open procedure comparison, the tamoxifen and cortisone recurrence trials) from stated personal
opinion (hand therapy, massage, heavy-activity avoidance, supplements), repeatedly flagging the latter
category explicitly ("that's my opinion," "remember, I'm not your doctor"). He states directly where no
evidence exists at all (massage, supplements, topical verapamil for Ledderhose) rather than implying support
where none exists.
