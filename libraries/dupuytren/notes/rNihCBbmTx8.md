# Dr. Rosanne Lanting: "Dupuytren Disease Clusters" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a 150-patient study with clinical exams every six months over two years, Lanting's group found Dupuytren's
disease is not simply progressive — up to 75% of patients showed a disease course other than steady
progression, including stability, regression, and genuine fluctuation over time, with no association found to
any well-known risk factor.** She states the conclusion directly: **"DD is not always a progressive disease —
up to 75% of patients [have] a different disease course over time, and there's no association with all known
risk factors"** `[05:10]`.

## Why prior studies missed this

`[00:01]`–`[01:02]` — Prior studies found disease progression in **roughly 35% of patients after 18 years of
follow-up**, and in **roughly 50% after about 9 years** — but Lanting notes these studies only had **two
measurement points** (baseline and one follow-up), meaning the actual disease course *between* those points
was never observed. This motivated the study's core questions: **what is the natural disease course of
primary Dupuytren's disease over time**, and **which variables are associated with that course** — with a
starting hypothesis that age, disease severity, gender, and other established risk factors would influence it.

## Study design

`[01:02]`–`[02:07]` — A prospective study with **clinical examination every six months**; this talk reports
**two years of follow-up.** Measurements: **nodule/cord area in square centimeters**, using a device Lanting
calls a "tumor imager," with inter- and intra-observer reliability separately tested (detailed on a
conference poster); **total passive extension deficit**; and **Tubiana staging** for severity classification.
Analysis used **descriptive statistics and latent class models** to identify clusters within patients' change
profiles over time. Cohort: **~150 patients, 370 affected hands with primary disease**, majority male, mean
age 66; **contracture present in almost 60% of affected rays.**

## A stable median hiding large individual variation

`[02:07]`–`[03:09]` — At the aggregate level, the **median** nodule/cord area and median total passive
extension deficit stayed **fairly stable** across the first four measurement timepoints — but with a **large
interquartile range**, indicating substantial variation between individual patients. Lanting's key
methodological point: this **wide between-patient variation is exactly what keeps the median looking stable
over time**, even while individual patients may be changing considerably — making it very difficult to detect
meaningful individual-level change using aggregate statistics alone, and motivating the shift to cluster-based
analysis.

## Disease-course clusters

`[03:09]`–`[05:10]` — Using latent class models on **area (all fingers)**: **up to six distinct clusters**
emerged, showing patterns of **increase (in roughly 5–40% of patients, depending on the specific cluster/
finger), stability in a large group, and decrease over the two-year follow-up** — with **no association found
between cluster membership and any well-known risk factor.** For **extension deficit**, analysis was possible
only for the **ring and little fingers** (other fingers had too little contracture to analyze) — again showing
**up to three clusters**, with progression, stability, and regression all represented. Critically, some
patients showed **genuine fluctuation over time**, confirmed to exceed measurement error — meaning some
patients' disease measurably worsened and then improved (or vice versa) within the two-year window, not just
steadily trending in one direction.

## Comparison to earlier two-timepoint studies

`[05:10]` — Lanting notes that a closer look at the earlier-cited two-timepoint studies also shows evidence of
regression and stability, not pure progression — but **fluctuation over time specifically is the new finding**
made possible by this study's more frequent, six-monthly measurement schedule.

## Why this matters for treatment evaluation

`[05:10]` — Lanting draws out the practical implication directly: when evaluating **early-phase Dupuytren's
treatments such as radiotherapy or splinting**, researchers need to determine whether an observed change is a
**genuine treatment effect** or simply **natural disease course** — a distinction this study's findings make
considerably harder to take for granted, and one she recommends future study designs explicitly account for.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Nodule/cord area and total passive extension deficit fluctuating over a two-year period in some patients**
  — confirmed to exceed measurement error, describing genuine, non-monotonic disease-course variation
  `[04:09]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Latent class modeling** — the statistical method used to identify distinct clusters of disease-course
  patterns among patients, rather than assuming one shared trajectory `[03:09]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk presents epidemiological methodology and results directly.

## Source-stated confidence

Lanting frames her starting hypothesis (that age, severity, gender, and known risk factors would predict
disease course) as explicitly **not supported** by her own data — reporting a null finding candidly rather than
omitting or downplaying it. She is clear about a specific measurement limitation (extension deficit analysis
only possible for two fingers, due to insufficient contracture elsewhere in the cohort) and explicitly
distinguishes true fluctuation from measurement error rather than assuming any observed variability is
meaningful.

an observed improvement during a splinting trial could partly reflect natural regression rather than a true
