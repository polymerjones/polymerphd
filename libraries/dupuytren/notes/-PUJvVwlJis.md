# Dr. Joe Dias "MCID for the Patient Evaluation Measure" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Dias derives a minimum clinically important difference (MCID) of 3 points for the Patient Evaluation
Measure using ROC analysis anchored to patients' own sense of being better or worse — and finds that while the
average score improvement across a 1,200-patient cohort was surprisingly small, surgery still produced three
times the improvement seen with no treatment at all.** He states the core distinction driving the whole talk:
**"you can get a statistical significance if you're testing two groups... but the statistically significant
difference may not be clinically relevant. The smallest change in a score that the patient can appreciate...
is the minimum [clinically] important difference"** `[00:00]`.

## The Patient Evaluation Measure

`[00:00]`–`[02:03]` — The **PEM** is a UK patient-administered outcome measure, similar in spirit to the DASH
but specific to the hand rather than the whole upper limb. It has three sections; Dias focuses on the second,
an **11-question attribute section** using visual-analog-scale items (e.g., pain rated from "non-existent" to
"unbearable"), combined into a total score from **0 (normal) to 100 (severely abnormal).**

## Study design

`[02:03]`–`[02:03]` — **1,200 consecutive Dupuytren's contracture patients** were reviewed; 125 had died.
**878 patients** had PEM scores available at both baseline and final review; **526** additionally completed a
transition question (described below). Mean follow-up exceeded **two years** — Dias notes most recurrence and
change typically occurs within the first year. **Two-thirds of the cohort were operated on, one-third were
not.**

## A surprisingly small average change

`[02:03]`–`[03:05]` — Mean PEM score: **40 at baseline, 32 at 27 months** — a mean improvement of only **8
points**, which Dias calls **"quite shocking,"** noting it should give pause to assuming that improving
clinical measures necessarily tracks with a correspondingly large improvement in what patients themselves
perceive.

## Deriving the MCID from a transition question

`[03:05]`–`[04:07]` — Patients were separately asked a **transition question**: how they considered themselves
now compared to before treatment, on a scale from "cured" to "terrible." This let Dias split patients into two
groups — **improved** versus **no better/worse** — and compare each group's actual PEM score change. Patients
who rated themselves "cured" showed a large mean PEM improvement; the "no change" group showed essentially
none. Using **receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis** (sensitivity and specificity) to find the PEM
score change that best distinguished the "better" group from the "worse" group produced the study's headline
result: **a minimum clinically important difference of 3 points.**

## Operated versus non-operated, against natural history

`[04:07]`–`[05:09]` — Comparing groups: patients who were **not operated on still improved, by roughly the
MCID (3 points), over the same follow-up period** — a natural-history effect. Patients who **were operated on
improved roughly three times as much.** Dias's summary: **patients' perceived wellbeing improves somewhat
regardless of surgery, but surgery adds meaningfully more benefit than natural history alone** — while noting
the absolute improvement from surgery, in this outcome measure, is still fairly modest in scale.
Additionally: **about one in three non-operated patients worsened over time**, versus **roughly one in five
operated patients** who ended up worse than their baseline. Dias states he does not yet have comparable
data for collagenase specifically, though some relevant data exists for percutaneous needle fasciotomy.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Patient-rated pain, on a visual-analog scale from "non-existent" to "unbearable,"** one of the eleven
  attribute items composing the PEM score `[01:00]`.
- **Patients ending up subjectively worse than their pretreatment baseline** — quantified separately for
  operated (~1 in 5) and non-operated (~1 in 3) groups, rather than assumed to be a negligible outcome
  `[05:09]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Patient Evaluation Measure (PEM)** — a UK hand-specific patient-rated outcome measure, structurally
  similar to the DASH but scoped to the hand alone `[00:00]`.
- **Minimum clinically important difference (MCID)** — the smallest score change a patient can perceive as a
  real improvement or worsening, distinct from a statistically significant difference `[00:00]`.
- **Transition question** — a direct patient self-rating (cured to terrible) used to validate and calibrate
  the MCID against the patient's own sense of change `[03:05]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Dias opens with a self-deprecating joke about the previous night's alcohol, then presents the methodology and
results directly and without noticeable hedging on the numeric findings — describing the small mean
improvement candidly as "quite shocking" rather than minimizing it. He explicitly flags a gap in the current
evidence base: comparable MCID-anchored data for collagenase specifically does not yet exist, distinguishing
what is and isn't yet known rather than extrapolating the surgical findings to other treatments.
