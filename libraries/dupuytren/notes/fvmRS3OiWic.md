# Boris Hinz PhD, "Fundamental Aspects of Myofibroblast Contraction" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Myofibroblast contraction runs on two separable pathways with different jobs — one creates brief,
localized events; the other holds sustained tension — and Dupuytren's contracture likely emerges from their
repeated interaction over time, not from either one alone.** Hinz's own synthesis: **"you have two cycles —
one is a long-lasting, low-frequency cycle over probably days, where the cell is contracting strongly and
then remodeling on a local level... and over time would lead to these contractures that you observe in a
clinical situation"** `[15:18]`.

## What a myofibroblast actually is

`[02:06]` — Hinz opens by qualifying the field's usual shorthand: **"Professor Gabbiani, when he discovered
the myofibroblast, uses a functional definition of this cell — a contractile phenotype, and not necessarily
a molecular definition by alpha-smooth-muscle-actin expression — but it's so convenient to have this marker
that most people use it as a defining feature."** As a phenotype rather than a distinct cell type,
myofibroblasts appear across a wide range of conditions beyond Dupuytren's — hypertrophic scarring, burn
wounds, skin fibrosis, scleroderma, fibrotic responses to implants, lung fibrosis, heart failure, and, more
recently studied, atherosclerotic plaque evolution, chronic asthma, and the tumor stroma, where they are
described as actively promoting tumor growth `[02:06]`–`[03:07]`.

## Two contraction pathways, cleanly separated by drug experiments

`[06:11]`–`[15:18]` — Working with cardiac myofibroblasts cultured on soft silicone substrates (chosen to
match scar stiffness, since **"the sheer fact that you put fibroblasts on a plastic dish transforms a good
part of them into myofibroblasts"**), Hinz's group tracked two readouts simultaneously: substrate wrinkling
(a proxy for sustained isometric tension) and bead movement on the cell surface (a proxy for small,
localized contractile events).

| Pathway | Mechanism | Speed/character | Drug used to isolate it | Effect of blocking it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Calcium pathway** | Calcium binds calmodulin, activating myosin light chain kinase, which phosphorylates myosin | Short-lived, high-frequency (**roughly every minute**) | Thapsigargin / 2-APB | Bead movement stops; **wrinkles (isometric tension) persist** |
| **Rho-kinase pathway** | Inactivates the enzyme that removes the phosphate from myosin, keeping myosin constitutively active | Long-lasting, sustained | Y-27632 | Wrinkles disassemble, isometric tension lost; **bead movement is unaffected** |

Blocking both simultaneously with blebbistatin abolished all cell tension within about ten minutes: **"the
beads don't move anymore, and the cells would lose completely this wrinkling behavior."** The two pathways'
effects are cleanly dissociable — each drug knocked out one readout while leaving the other intact — which
Hinz takes as evidence the two pathways genuinely do different jobs rather than being redundant routes to
the same output `[13:17]`–`[15:18]`.

## The rubber-band model

`[15:18]`–`[16:18]` — Hinz's synthesis, offered explicitly as a working model rather than a proven
mechanism: **"imagine you're trying to contract a rubber band — what you have to do is pull it in, and then
you create a kind of slack... the rubber band for the cell is actually the extracellular matrix, the
collagen."** The Rho-kinase pathway produces a **strong, long-lasting contraction of the stress fibers,
which creates slack in individual collagen fibers**; the faster calcium-driven contractile events then
**"work on these stress-released fibers and pull them in, remodel them"** while the fiber is slack; the
matrix **takes over the load again**, and the cell re-spreads to repeat the cycle. Repeated over **days**,
this cumulative local remodeling is proposed as the route from small, reversible single-cell contractions to
the irreversible clinical contracture.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None specific to Dupuytren's presentation — this is in vitro cell mechanics, offered as background for
interpreting clinical contracture rather than a description of patient-facing symptoms.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Myofibroblast (functional definition)** — a contractile cell phenotype, defined by Gabbiani by function
  rather than strictly by alpha-smooth-muscle-actin expression `[02:06]`.
- **Calcium/calmodulin/MLCK pathway** — the fast, short-duration contraction route, shared with smooth
  muscle cell biology `[06:11]`.
- **Rho-kinase pathway** — the pathway sustaining long-lasting, isometric myofibroblast tension by keeping
  myosin dephosphorylated-inhibition removed `[06:11]`–`[07:11]`.
- **Isometric versus periodic-scale contraction** — Hinz's own terms for the two readouts (wrinkling versus
  bead movement) his assay separates `[14:17]`–`[15:18]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The rubber band being pulled taut, creating slack that a second, faster process then takes up and
  remodels** — Hinz's own synthesis model for how brief and sustained contraction combine `[15:18]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Hinz is explicit that this is a working model built from in vitro cardiac myofibroblast data, not confirmed
Dupuytren's tissue biology: **"outside of the body, in a three-dimensional gel... we think that's what's
happening in the Dupuytren's contracture as well"** `[05:10]` — stated as an inference by extension, not a
direct finding. He also flags that the specific mechanism connecting calcium-pathway events to the
Rho-kinase pathway's role in creating the slack they act on is model-level reasoning: **"the idea that we
have... is the following"** `[15:18]`, not a demonstrated causal chain in Dupuytren's-affected tissue.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly relevant to the paired debate talks `QRUnLzXLyjE`** (Nanchahal, "active") **and `wsqTB5LAGYo`**
(Eaton, "passive"). Eaton's passive-contraction argument uses this exact same periodic-versus-isometric
framework and the same Rho-kinase/calcium pathway distinction — this talk is very likely the primary
science source underlying that argument, though Eaton's talk does not cite it directly. Notably, **Hinz's
own two-cycle model does not fully side with either debate position**: it requires both the strong,
Rho-kinase-driven contraction Nanchahal's "active" argument emphasizes (to create slack) and the
matrix-laxity-dependent remodeling Eaton's "passive" argument emphasizes (to lock the slack in) — read as a
synthesis rather than a vote for either side. This talk does not address the cell-to-cell adherens/gap
junction coupling central to Nanchahal's argument, so it neither confirms nor contradicts that part of his
case.
