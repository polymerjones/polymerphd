# Albrecht Meinel MD, "Palmar Fibromatosis — a New Pathogenesis Concept" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Meinel argues, against the traditional model of Dupuytren's disease as pathological shortening (active
contraction) of normal fascial structures, that the bent finger instead reflects tissue *retention*: since
fingers already spend most of the day and night in a flexed position, an extension-blocking fibromatosis
develops specifically within the compressed, mobile fiber tissue of the already-flexed finger, fixing it in
place rather than actively shortening it.** He states the reframing directly: **"fibrosis fixes a compressed
palmar tissue, which loses its extension mobility, and with this the finger is retained in [a] flexion
position"** `[09:36]`.

## Contraction versus retention

`[00:07]`–`[03:19]` — The traditional pathogenesis model, as Meinel frames it, explains the bent finger by
pathological shortening of otherwise normal fascial structures — implicitly treating the healthy baseline as
an *extended* finger that then contracts. Meinel proposes the opposite starting assumption: since a finger
spends the great majority of time flexed rather than extended, disease should be understood as arising within
that already-flexed, mobile tissue — a **new, tumor-like tissue forming in the subcutaneous fibro-fatty
layer**, made of densely packed anchoring fibers with interposed fat, sitting above the layer the flexor
tendons glide within. He stresses that the *mechanical* contraction function belonging to tendons cannot be
attributed to this separate fibro-fatty tissue layer — they are mechanically distinct structures. Using
plastinated cross-sections of the finger in extension versus flexion, he shows the anchoring fiber structure
is only densely compacted in the **flexed** position — consistent with disease developing specifically within
that dynamic, flexion-compressed tissue rather than in stationary mid-palm tissue.

## Nodules as expansion and fixation, not contraction

`[05:27]`–`[07:33]` — Meinel describes the earliest disease nodule as originating **between skin and the
palmar aponeurosis**, growing by **expansion**, and **replacing subcutaneous fat** as it enlarges — a process
he states explicitly shows **no signs of a contraction process** at this early stage. The nodule becomes
attached to the aponeurosis and to **Cleland's ligament**, and as it grows, Meinel argues it **fixes** the
surrounding tissue in place — binding together fibers and adjacent structures into what he calls a
conglomerate that loses its own mobility. Crucially, he argues the digital nerve and artery are **not
displaced from their normal anatomical position** by this process — rather, the finger becomes bent *around*
them, while they remain fixed in place.

## A case example

`[08:34]`–`[09:36]` — A case is shown where a **spiral cord** displaces the neurovascular bundle near the web
space, identified as arising from a **hypertrophic Cleland's ligament fixed in a flexed position** rather than
a newly formed structure — the same hand is shown six years later, reportedly clear of disease.

## Reinterpreting Luck's classification

`[09:36]`–`[11:40]` — Meinel credits **Luck** (J. Vaughan Luck, whose histogenic classification of Dupuytren's
disease phases is referenced elsewhere in the literature) as the first author to recognize the significance of
connective-tissue **stress adaptation** in this disease, and states Luck's staged classification still holds
up. However, Meinel argues Luck's original idea that contraction reflects an **"involutional" process** needs
reinterpretation: his own work suggests the seemingly contracted tissue is, in fact, **tissue retained in a
flexed position** through remodeling — not actively shortening fibers. Following from Luck's related surgical
principle (not excising reactive, altered aponeurosis), Meinel describes his own modified technique, which he
names **"digital fasciotomy"** — an exclusively subcutaneous release — and proposes **static extension
splinting** as a way to prevent tissue and finger retention from developing or progressing. His closing
statement on the myofibroblast: it appears to be **"a reactive cell transformation as part of the connective
tissue['s] stress adaptation" — not an actively contracting cell or fiber** `[11:40]`.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **A bent finger understood as fixed/retained tissue rather than actively shortening tissue** — Meinel's
  central reframing of the disease's basic mechanical presentation `[09:36]`.
- **A spiral cord displacing the neurovascular bundle near the web space**, shown as a case example and
  attributed to a hypertrophic, fixed Cleland's ligament `[08:34]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Tissue retention** (as distinct from tissue contraction) — Meinel's proposed mechanism, where fibrotic
  tissue fixes the finger in an already-flexed position rather than actively shortening to pull it there
  `[09:36]`.
- **Digital fasciotomy** — Meinel's own modified, exclusively subcutaneous release technique, developed
  following from this pathogenesis model `[11:40]`.

## Analogies worth reusing
