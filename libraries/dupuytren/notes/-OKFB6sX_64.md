# Ilse Degreef MD PhD "Highly-Dosed Neo-Adjuvant Tamoxifen" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Surgery treats Dupuytren's contracture, not Dupuytren's disease — "surgeons don't cure a disease, they
just treat it" — and Degreef's trial tests whether adding tamoxifen around surgery can address the disease
process itself, at least in the highest-recurrence-risk patients** `[00:03]`. Her framing of the disease
itself: **"you have the disease, and you will have it the rest of your life. You may become symptom-free
for a while"** — comparing it explicitly to rheumatoid arthritis, and crediting a colleague's line that
**"disease symptoms will occur as long as the patient lives long enough."**

## Why tamoxifen, and why this patient group

`[02:08]`–`[04:14]` — The idea came from **desmoid tumors** (also fibroblast-driven), already treated with
high-dose tamoxifen in some cases, combined with lab evidence that **tamoxifen specifically inhibits
myofibroblast proliferation and contractility in Dupuytren's tissue.** Degreef deliberately selected
**high-recurrence-risk patients (using the Abe risk score)** rather than a general population, reasoning
that a real effect would be easiest to detect where recurrence risk — and therefore statistical power — was
highest. The trial: **randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled**, tamoxifen given **neoadjuvantly** —
starting **six weeks before surgery and continuing twelve weeks after** — combined with standard segmental
fasciectomy and standard splinting. Fertile women and cancer patients were excluded (tamoxifen is an
estrogen-pathway drug); embolism-risk patients were excluded given the drug's clotting risk, which Degreef
states she disclosed to every patient directly: **"there is a risk to medication — you could die from it. I
always say that to the patient."**

## Recruiting and running the trial: real friction, honestly reported

`[07:18]`–`[09:21]` — Degreef reports specific difficulty recruiting men to a trial of **"female hormone"**
at first, improving only with practice explaining it. Notable dropouts: one patient developed pulmonary
emphysema and became inoperable; one patient, convinced the drug worked from his own symptom improvement,
**left the trial, obtained needle aponeurotomy elsewhere instead, and took the remaining study medication
with him** — lost to follow-up; one patient's medication was interrupted by an unrelated severe appendicitis
but the surgical outcome was still good. She also tested whether a **biopsy-based marker** could identify
high-risk patients directly (rather than via risk-factor scoring) and found **no meaningful biopsy
difference** between recurrent and non-recurrent disease, consistent, she notes, with other published
findings.

## Preliminary one-year results

`[09:21]`–`[12:24]` — Assessed via blinded digital photography and anonymized satisfaction scoring. Before
formal unblinding, Degreef could already see **two visually distinct outcome groups**: one group nearly all
achieved complete correction, the other included the two patients with insufficient correction. This
pattern held across **visual analog satisfaction scores, total joint correction, and PIP-joint-specific
correction** — described explicitly as preliminary and not yet statistically analyzed. A striking, honestly
reported false alarm: **three patients developed carpal tunnel syndrome**, which Degreef initially assumed
must be the tamoxifen arm (given the drug's hormonal mechanism) — **"I was relieved" to later learn, on
unblinding, that these were placebo-arm side effects**, alongside other placebo-arm complaints (reported
impotence, sleeplessness, weight gain). Her point: **"if we give placebo to patients, apparently we have to
warn them for the side effects [too]"** — patients report symptoms consistent with what they were told to
expect, regardless of which arm they're in. On unblinding, **one genuine tamoxifen-arm complication** was
identified: a serious wound-healing problem requiring tenolysis and pulley reconstruction, limiting final
flexion despite full extension being achieved.

## A later case report: stopping and restarting

`[13:26]`–`[14:26]` — Outside the formal trial, Degreef describes offering tamoxifen to a patient with
**severe, treatment-resistant recurrent disease** after a fifth-finger PIP arthrodesis — the patient's
disease **"completely disappeared,"** symptom-free, while adherent to the medication. When the patient chose
to stop on her own, **a recurrent nodule appeared within four weeks**; restarting tamoxifen, the nodule had
**shrunk to half its size within a couple of weeks.** Degreef is careful to label this **"preliminary,"**
not trial-level evidence, but treats it as consistent with the randomized data.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Contracture recurrence risk stratified by the Abe score** — Degreef states a roughly 38% recurrence
  risk with zero risk factors present, rising sharply as more risk factors accumulate `[05:16]`.
- **Fifth-finger PIP joint contracture specifically flagged as the hardest to treat**, used as the
  reference joint when multiple digits were affected `[06:16]`.
- **Nodule regrowth within weeks of stopping tamoxifen in a treatment-responsive patient**, and shrinkage
  again on restarting `[13:26]`.
- **Placebo-arm side effects (carpal tunnel syndrome, reported impotence, sleeplessness, weight gain)** —
  presented as a caution about symptom reporting under expectation, not as evidence against tamoxifen's
  real effects `[11:23]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Neoadjuvant** — medication given both before and after the definitive surgical procedure, rather
  than as a substitute for surgery `[03:11]`.
- **Abe (risk) score** — the clinical risk-factor scoring system used to select high-recurrence-risk trial
  candidates; family history is noted as not directly included but statistically correlated with score
  components `[06:16]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Dupuytren's compared directly to rheumatoid arthritis** — a disease surgery/medicine manage but do not
  cure, with symptom-free periods properly understood as remission, not resolution `[00:03]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Unusually candid throughout: results are repeatedly labeled **"preliminary," "not a 100% sure," "I haven't
had any statistics [yet]"** `[10:23]`; the biopsy-marker negative finding is reported plainly rather than
omitted; the placebo-arm side-effect scare is told against her own initial assumption, undercutting rather
than protecting her hypothesis; and the closing case report is explicitly flagged as anecdotal ("I might be
optimistic... we have to look at the outcome later on") rather than presented as confirmed.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Is the primary source behind `-jLfWXX3YUM`'s (British Dupuytren Society Seven Questions) secondhand
summary** — that later video states tamoxifen "reduced the risk of early recurrence compared to placebo...
the first proof that medicine could slow down recurrence," attributing the finding to "Dr. Ilse Degreef;"
this talk is Degreef's own original presentation of that same trial, ten years earlier, with substantially
more methodological detail (patient selection via Abe score, the neoadjuvant dosing window, the placebo
side-effect confound, and the specific tenolysis complication) that the later secondhand summary doesn't
include. **Directly relevant to `AbY1KVhTVAo`** (Eaton's IDDB talk) — Degreef's biopsy-marker negative
finding here (no clear histologic difference between recurrent and non-recurrent disease) is a concrete data
point bearing on the same biomarker gap Eaton's IDDB talk identifies as the field's central obstacle.
**The mechanism behind this trial's clinical result is shown directly in `dMW6VVm4FhU`** (Robson's lab
models talk) — tamoxifen inhibiting Dupuytren's fibroblast contraction and lowering TGF-beta 1/2 levels in
an FPCL model, a plausible molecular explanation for the recurrence reduction reported here.
