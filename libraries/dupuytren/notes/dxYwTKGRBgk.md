# Massimiliano Tripoli MD, "The Jacobsen Flap for Advanced-Stage Little Finger Dupuytren's" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**For advanced-stage (Tubiana III/IV) little finger Dupuytren's, the Jacobsen flap — an H-shaped open-palm
flap technique — produced staging improvement in roughly half of patients with a manageable complication
profile, and Tripoli argues it compares favorably to both needle fasciotomy and dermofasciectomy with
full-thickness grafting for this specific presentation.** His stated conclusion: **"because of the rapid
surgical technique [and] the minor risk of complications... the Jacobsen flap is a better option [than]
fasciectomy and percutaneous needle fasciotomy in treatment of stage three [and] four disease at the little
finger"** `[09:22]`.

## Comparing three surgical options for advanced disease

`[02:08]`–`[04:13]` — Tripoli frames early-stage disease as suitable for standard incisional approaches
(Bruner incision, selective aponeurectomy), but describes three distinct options once skin is contracted at
**Tubiana stage III or IV**:

- **Percutaneous needle fasciotomy** — indicated for advanced stage with contracted skin, elderly patients,
  and a clinically evident cord. Advantages: immediate digital movement restoration, rapid and minimally
  traumatic. Disadvantages (per the literature): neurovascular injury risk and a high recurrence rate.
- **Dermofasciectomy with full-thickness skin graft** — indicated for frequent recurrence or high-diathesis
  patients. Advantages: essentially no recurrence *within* the graft margin (occasional recurrence at the
  graft's own edge). Disadvantages: donor-site scarring, graft failure risk, and stiffness from the
  immobilization the graft requires.
- **The McCash technique** — indicated for fibromatosis in the palmar/metacarpal region with contracted
  skin, requiring additional digital incisions and sometimes skin grafting at the interdigital creases.

## The Jacobsen flap technique itself

`[04:13]`–`[07:19]` — First published by Jacobsen in 1977 (Scandinavian Journal of Plastic and Reconstructive
Surgery), a modification of the McCash approach using **two or three incisions**, specifically for **little
finger disease at Tubiana stage III/IV with contracted skin.** Technique: a first incision at the distal
palmar crease, a second perpendicular incision along the midlateral line of the little finger extending to
the level of contracture; the flap is elevated to **immediately identify the neurovascular bundles**, then a
**selective aponeurectomy** is performed. The resulting **H-shaped flap is advanced distally, leaving the
palm open to heal by secondary intention over about three to four weeks.**

## Outcomes from an 8-year, 133-patient series

`[07:19]`–`[08:22]` — Across two institutions over 8 years: **133 patients operated (88 at stage IV, 45 at
stage III)**, none with prior Jacobsen flap surgery; all completed physical therapy; **123 patients had
adequate follow-up data.** Staging results: of **86 stage IV patients, 38 reached stage 0** postoperatively;
of **37 stage III patients, 25 reached stage 0** at 18 months — combined, **roughly 49% of operated patients
reached stage 0** after surgery.

**Complications**: two nerve injuries, one arterial injury (attributed to a spiral cord), one case of
marginal flap necrosis (managed with escharotomy). **Recurrence occurred in 14 cases**; 10 of these underwent
repeat Jacobsen flap surgery, though some ultimately required amputation due to compromised ("ectopic")
blood supply. **Reflex sympathetic dystrophy (RSD) occurred in 11 patients**, all of whom achieved **complete
symptom remission with analgesics and about three months of physical therapy.**

**Stated advantages**: rapid procedure, open palm reduces hematoma risk, no skin graft required, rapid
movement restoration. **Stated disadvantages**: frequent dressing changes (2–3 times weekly), patient
psychological discomfort from the visible open wound in the first two to three weeks, infection risk, and
delayed healing specifically in patients with metabolic disorders (e.g. diabetes), requiring careful wound
monitoring in that group.

## Three unresolved epidemiological puzzles

`[10:23]`–`[12:24]` — Tripoli closes by naming specific inconsistencies he sees in current risk-factor
explanations, without resolving them:

1. **Diabetes**: cited elsewhere as a major risk factor, yet **Asia has the world's highest diabetes
   burden while having comparatively low Dupuytren's prevalence** — "diabetes is [not] so determinant."
2. **Chronic microtrauma**: proposed to break longitudinal fibers and drive fibroblast collagen deposition,
   yet **children performing daily repetitive manual labor in Tanzania, Pakistan, and India** don't develop
   the disease, while it appears mainly in older patients elsewhere — and separately, **onset commonly
   differs in timing between a person's two hands**, hard to explain by uniform mechanical exposure alone.
3. **Race/genetics**: Caucasians carry the highest risk, with only isolated cases in Asian and Black African
   populations — yet the **TGF-beta polymorphism linked to keloid scarring** (itself common in African
   populations) is plausibly related to Dupuytren's biology, raising the question of why the disease itself
   remains rare in that same population.

His conclusion: **"Dupuytren's disease remains, for now, an idiopathic disease"** and, until better
understood, **surgery remains the necessary treatment.** He closes wryly noting a hoped-for future
anti-TGF-beta topical cream, while admitting he'd rather keep operating on patients a while longer.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Reflex sympathetic dystrophy after surgery**, reported in 11 of 123 followed patients, fully resolving
  with conservative management (analgesics, ~3 months physical therapy) `[08:22]`.
- **Compromised digital blood supply after recurrence surgery**, in some cases severe enough to require
  amputation `[08:22]`.
- **Psychological distress from viewing an open, healing surgical wound** in the weeks after Jacobsen flap
  surgery, named explicitly as a real disadvantage of the technique, not just a physical one `[09:22]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Jacobsen flap** — the H-shaped, open-palm-healing flap technique for advanced little-finger disease,
  described in full above `[04:13]`.
- **Tubiana staging (0 through IV)** — used throughout as the outcome measure for pre- and post-operative
  comparison `[02:08]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk presents technique and outcome data directly.

## Source-stated confidence

Tripoli separates established literature-reported risks (neurovascular injury and recurrence rates for
needle fasciotomy) from his own series' outcomes, and closes by explicitly naming his own explanatory
puzzles as unresolved rather than offering a false resolution — repeating "why" without supplying an answer
for each of the three epidemiological inconsistencies he raises.
