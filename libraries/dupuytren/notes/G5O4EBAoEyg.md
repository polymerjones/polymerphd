# Dr. Dieuwke Broekstra "A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Pooling 32 studies in a formal meta-analysis, Broekstra's group found diabetes significantly associated
with Dupuytren's disease — a pooled odds ratio of 3.4 — and this association held, actually strengthening
slightly, after statistically correcting for age, resolving much of the inconsistency seen across individual
prior studies.** She states the result directly: **"we can say that diabetes is significantly associated with
Dupuytren's disease, and we think that researchers should control for this association when they are doing
research"** `[04:09]`.

## Why prior studies disagreed

`[00:00]`–`[01:00]` — Dupuytren's disease has often been linked to diabetes in the literature, but with
inconsistent results — some studies find an association, others do not. Broekstra proposes three explanations:
**heterogeneity between study populations**, **failure to correct for age** (she notes control groups in many
prior studies were **considerably younger** than the diabetes comparison group, which alone would produce an
apparent higher Dupuytren's prevalence in the diabetes group regardless of any true biological association),
and some studies being **underpowered.**

## Method

`[01:00]`–`[02:03]` — A systematic literature search (MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science) was run on **July 11,
2013**, updated on **July 2 and 17, 2014.** A **random-effects meta-analysis** (via Stata) was used to
determine the pooled association; a separate **generalized linear mixed model analysis** (via SPSS) tested
whether the effect held after correcting for age. Significance threshold: **5%.** Screening: **1,308 initial
articles, 1,217 unique**; after title/abstract screening, **101 entered full-text review**; **69 were
excluded** for reasons including no physical exam used to diagnose Dupuytren's, being a case series or
conference proceeding, lacking original or sufficiently reported data, or not reporting age. **32 articles**
were included in the final analysis.

## Results

`[02:03]`–`[04:09]` — The **pooled odds ratio was 3.4**, with individual study odds ratios ranging from **1 to
51.9** — a wide range, but with **no study showing an odds ratio below 1**, meaning the direction of the
association was consistent across every included study even where the magnitude varied widely. The forest
plot showed one outlier study with an odds ratio near 52; Broekstra attributes this **extreme value to that
study's very small sample size**, which also gave it very low weight in the pooled estimate — explaining most
of the range without meaningfully distorting the pooled 3.4 figure itself. After **correcting for age** via
the mixed-model analysis, the **effect persisted and was, if anything, slightly stronger.**

## Strengths and an unresolved limitation

`[04:09]`–`[05:11]` — Broekstra highlights specific methodological strengths: **no publication-date
restriction** was applied (important, since much of the relevant literature dates to the 1950s and 60s),
**age correction** was applied where prior reviews had not done so, and a **random-effects model** was used
appropriately given known heterogeneity across studies. The clear limitation acknowledged: the review **could
not separate the effect by diabetes type (e.g. type 1 versus type 2)**, since most included papers did not
report which type of diabetes their patients had — an open question Broekstra states remains unanswered.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Pooled (meta-analytic) odds ratio** — the combined association estimate across all included studies,
  here 3.4 for the diabetes-Dupuytren's association `[02:03]`.
- **Forest plot** — the standard meta-analysis visualization showing each individual study's effect estimate
  and weight alongside the pooled overall estimate `[03:04]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Broekstra discloses named co-authors' financial relationships (consultancy for Pfizer and for Merck) upfront.
She is explicit and specific about why prior literature disagreed (age confounding, heterogeneity, underpowered
studies) rather than treating the discrepancy as unexplained, and directly attributes the meta-analysis's wide
odds-ratio range to one small, low-weight outlier study rather than allowing it to appear as broad uncertainty
in the pooled estimate. She states plainly that the diabetes-type question remains genuinely unresolved by this
review rather than speculating an answer.
