# Dr. Dieuwke Broekstra "Vibration and Dupuytren Disease" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Studying elderly Dutch field hockey players — chosen specifically because their vibration exposure comes
from leisure sport rather than occupation — Broekstra's group found a statistically significant, dose-
dependent association between hand vibration exposure and Dupuytren's disease.** She states the core result
directly: **"based on these data, we can say that vibration is significantly associated with DD in this
sample, and we were able to show a dose-response relation, which is in line with some previous studies"**
`[03:03]`.

## Why field hockey players

`[00:00]`–`[01:01]` — Vibration is a frequently cited risk factor in the Dupuytren's literature, but Broekstra
notes nearly all prior studies focus on **occupational** vibration exposure — overlooking that hands are also
exposed to vibration during leisure and sports activities. **Elderly male field hockey players** (Dutch field
hockey, played on grass, not the ice hockey more familiar to American/Canadian audiences) were chosen as a
study population specifically to isolate non-occupational vibration exposure.

## Study design and matching

`[01:01]`–`[03:03]` — **170 elderly male field hockey players** and a control group drawn from a colleague's
(Lanting's) existing dataset were recruited; **manual workers and vibration-exposed controls were excluded**
from both groups. The resulting groups were substantially unbalanced on baseline characteristics, so
**propensity score matching** was used — balancing on age, diabetes, smoking, and alcohol use, with 1:1
matching without replacement — reducing the sample to **40 matched pairs (80 participants total)**. Vibration
exposure itself was measured **continuously, as hours per week multiplied by years of exposure**, rather than
the simple yes/no dichotomy used in most prior literature. Broekstra shows descriptive statistics before and
after matching: before matching, the two groups differed on **almost every variable**; after matching, **most
differences disappeared**, at the cost of losing a substantial number of cases — an expected trade-off of the
matching method, with any differences remaining after matching interpreted as likely related to the exposure
itself rather than confounding.

## Results

`[03:03]`–`[04:04]` — Multiple logistic regression, controlling for the propensity score:

- **Vibration as a dichotomous factor**: odds ratio **3.95**, statistically significant.
- **Vibration as a continuous factor** (hours/week × years): odds ratio **1.006** per unit, still statistically
  significant — demonstrating a **dose-response relationship** consistent with prior studies.

## Limitations acknowledged directly

`[04:04]`–`[05:06]` — Broekstra is explicit that **the role of concomitant hand trauma remains unclear**: the
study did ask participants about hand trauma, but most had difficulty recalling exactly what happened or which
hand/finger was affected, making the variable **too unreliable to include in the analysis.** She also notes
the study lacks any **biomechanical data on what actually happens in the hand during field hockey play** —
meaning the observed effect might partly reflect **hand trauma from play, not vibration exposure alone.** Data
gathered by interview also carries a general **recall bias** risk.

## Strengths acknowledged directly

`[04:04]` — Field hockey is described as an elitist sport in the Netherlands, meaning nearly every participant
was a **white-collar worker** — producing an unusually **homogeneous, non-occupational vibration exposure
group**, minimizing confounding from occupational vibration specifically. All participants were **physically
examined by experienced physicians**, and propensity score matching was used deliberately to balance otherwise
very unequal groups.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None beyond the presence and severity of Dupuytren's disease itself, tracked as the primary study outcome

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Propensity score matching** — a statistical technique for balancing two observational study groups on
  measured confounders, used here to compare hockey players and controls fairly `[01:01]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk presents epidemiological study design and results directly.

## Source-stated confidence

Broekstra is unusually direct about limitations that could undermine her own headline finding: she explicitly
flags that concomitant hand trauma during hockey play could confound the vibration-DD association, and states
outright the study cannot rule this out due to the lack of biomechanical data and unreliable trauma recall.
She frames the sample's homogeneity (white-collar, non-occupational) as a genuine strength while still
flagging recall bias as a real limitation of interview-gathered data, rather than presenting the study as
methodologically flawless.
