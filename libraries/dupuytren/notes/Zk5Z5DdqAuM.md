# Dr. Marie Badalamente "Collagenase for Adhesive Capsulitis" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a 50-patient phase 2 dose-ranging trial, extra-articular collagenase injection into the frozen-shoulder
capsule — at the same 0.58mg dose used clinically for Dupuytren's — produced substantially larger gains in
active and passive range of motion, function, and pain than exercise alone, with a safety profile consistent
with collagenase's known mechanism.** Badalamente states the headline comparison directly: the 0.58mg/1mL
group's forward flexion went **"from about 103 [degrees]... popped right up to almost... 146 degrees, and
that's almost a forty-two percent improvement"** against a control group that "didn't get too much better,
only about 12 degrees" `[07:06]`–`[08:06]`.

## Why frozen shoulder

`[00:00]`–`[02:01]` — Badalamente frames this as testing whether collagenase — developed at Stony Brook for
Dupuytren's cords and already FDA-approved for that use — has merit across a broader category of
**fibro-proliferative disorders**. Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis) was specifically chosen because it
also involves **excess collagen deposition**, this time in the shoulder capsule, and — the stated motivating
observation — **it is about eight times more common in Dupuytren's patients** than in the general population.
The question: could collagenase lyse collagen in the shoulder capsule the way it does in a Dupuytren's cord,
restoring range of motion and relieving what Badalamente describes as a miserable, painful condition.

## Groundwork before the reported trial

`[02:01]`–`[04:02]` — Two prior studies preceded this one. First, an anatomical/safety study: extensive
cadaveric work identified an **anterior injection site between the bicipital groove and the tip of the
coracoid process**, with the arm passively externally rotated, using a **blunt-tipped spinal needle**;
ultrasound-guided saline injection in ten volunteers without frozen shoulder confirmed **reliable
extra-articular delivery**. Second, a **blinded dose-ranging study** against placebo, starting from the same
units used in Dupuytren's development work and escalating to the clinically used 0.58mg Dupuytren's dose, with
a single injection plus standard home physical therapy for all patients — establishing **0.58mg as the
estimated minimum safe and effective dose** for the trial reported here.

## Trial design

`[05:04]`–`[06:05]` — **50 patients (40 female, 10 male), 11 sites**, 32 of whom had already failed prior
conservative management (steroid injection, physical therapy, or both). Inclusion required unilateral frozen
shoulder, 3–12 months' duration, and a restricted active range-of-motion deficit of at least 60 degrees total
(and at least 30 degrees in one motion plane) compared to the unaffected shoulder. **Five dose/volume
cohorts** (0.29mg/1mL, 0.58mg/2mL, 0.58mg/1mL, 0.58mg/0.5mL) were compared against an exercise-only cohort, 10
patients per cohort, followed to **92 days**.

## Results

`[06:05]`–`[09:06]` — The **0.58mg/1mL dose showed the largest statistical significance** in active forward
flexion and abduction, with a **mean of 2.58 injections per patient** (most patients used close to the
allowed maximum of three). Forward flexion: control rose only ~12 degrees (113→~125) versus the 0.58mg/1mL
group's ~42% improvement (103→146). Abduction: exercise-only rose ~17 degrees (92→108) versus the
0.58mg/1mL(-equivalent) group's ~66% improvement (82→136). **Passive range of motion** also showed
statistically significant improvement in forward flexion, abduction, and internal rotation, mostly in the
0.58mg 1mL and 2mL cohorts. **Pain and function**, measured via the American Academy of Orthopaedic
[Shoulder and Elbow] Surgeons questionnaire, likewise showed statistically significant improvement in the
0.58mg 1mL and 2mL groups.

## Safety

`[09:06]`–`[11:08]` — Adverse events were **mild to moderate, resolving without intervention over a mean of
14 days**, mirroring the Dupuytren's collagenase safety profile (pain, swelling, contusion, ecchymosis).
**No patient discontinued due to an adverse event.** Baseline and end-of-study **MRIs found no pathology
changes**, specifically ruling out rotator cuff injury — a question Badalamente notes was frequently asked.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Restricted active and passive shoulder range of motion**, quantified separately across three specific
  motion planes (forward flexion, abduction, internal rotation) `[08:06]`, `[09:06]`.
- **Shoulder pain and functional limitation**, tracked via a validated orthopedic questionnaire alongside
  range-of-motion measures, not just range of motion in isolation `[09:06]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)** — the collagen-related shoulder condition being repurposed as a
  collagenase treatment target `[00:00]`.
- **Fibro-proliferative disorders** — the broader disease category Badalamente frames collagenase as
  potentially useful for, beyond Dupuytren's contracture specifically `[01:01]`.

None stated — the talk presents trial design and results directly.

## Source-stated confidence

Badalamente discloses the same financial relationships noted in her Dupuytren's collagenase talks and credits
her orthopedic-surgeon collaborator explicitly, framing teamwork as essential to this kind of study. She is
clear that **this is not yet an approved treatment** and that this is a **phase 2 study**, distinguishing it
from the FDA-approved Dupuytren's indication; she notes a larger, placebo-controlled phase 2b study of over
300 patients across 35 US and Australian sites had "just recently completed" at the time of this talk,
signaling this 50-patient result as preliminary rather than final.
