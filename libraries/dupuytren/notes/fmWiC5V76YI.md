# Roger Khouri MD, "Extensive Percutaneous Aponeurotomy Plus Fat Grafting" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Standard percutaneous release alone gives "very good but... limited relief," and open fasciectomy gives
better release at the cost of "high morbidity and long recovery" — Khouri's answer is to combine an
extensive, multi-nick percutaneous release with fat grafting into the space it creates**, arguing this adds
back tissue the disease itself has depleted: **"fat atrophy is really intrinsic to the pathology of
Dupuytren's"** `[00:04]`. He is explicit the fat-grafting idea is not novel in principle: **"fat grafted
Dupuytren's [was described] in the German literature... in the 1600s. We're not reinventing anything
new"** `[01:05]`.

## The technique and its three principles

`[01:05]`–`[05:08]` — Khouri distinguishes his **extensive percutaneous aponeurotomy (EPA)** from standard
needle release by intent: rather than a few limited cuts, **"why don't we shred this cord?"** — many small,
superficial nicks rather than fewer larger ones. Three stated principles:

1. **The "violin string" effect**: a small nick under strong tension will cut cleanly, but the same nick
   in slack tissue will not — and **"the nerves are not under as much tension as the cord itself,"** making
   the technique selective for the taut cord over adjacent structures `[02:05]`.
2. **Stay superficial**: treating the cord like a woven material, **"if you just cause attrition cuts at
   multiple levels, it's going to fall apart"** without needing to cut deep — including near the spiral
   cord, where staying superficial matters most for safety `[03:06]`.
3. **Create space for the graft**: the multiple nicks also separate the cord from the dermis, creating
   vascularized "finger-like extensions" that increase the surface area available for the fat graft to
   revascularize and be incorporated `[03:06]`.

Instruments: an 18-gauge needle ("Dupuytome 1"), used bevel-up so **"the bevel is your depth gauge... never
completely buried,"** and a second bent needle ("Dupuytome 2") used with a "windshield-wiper" motion to
separate the cord from the dermis before fat is injected through a 14-gauge cannula `[04:07]`–`[05:08]`.
Post-procedure care: a splint incorporated into the dressing for a week, then a night splint from his hand
therapist.

## Outcomes from a 75-patient combined series

`[09:11]`–`[11:12]` — Run jointly with Steve Hovius, with **up to 42 months of follow-up, mean about 1.5
years**. Twenty patients had prior open surgery on the contralateral hand, giving an internal comparison
group; some patients had severe diabetes. **Overall satisfaction was high**, with essentially all patients
back to normal hand use, including golf, within two to four weeks. **Of 13 patients with recurrent disease
on the treated hand, all 13 preferred the new technique to their prior open surgery.** At a (short) 10-month
follow-up, looking at the worst-affected finger per patient: **96% improvement at the MP joint**, and
**PIP-joint residual contracture improved from 67% to 33%.** Compared informally against open-surgery
recurrence data (Khouri flags this comparison as not fully fair, given differing follow-up lengths), EPA
"seemed to be, if anything, slightly better," since open treatment showed more recurrence.

## Complications and one detailed failure case

`[10:12]`–`[15:19]` — **One nerve injury**, occurring specifically in a recurrent-disease case where prior
surgical scar had bound the nerve to the cord, degrading Khouri's ability to differentiate them while
cutting. **His one serious complication**: a young woman he identifies as high RSD (reflex sympathetic
dystrophy) risk, whose hand turned red immediately post-op and whose fat graft **"completely melted
away."** A second attempt at grafting also failed. He also describes cases where old surgical scar tissue
tore open during release (new, unoperated skin did not tear, only prior scar did) — managed with a small
local flap plus fat grafting.

## Broader applications shown

`[17:21]`–`[20:28]` — Khouri extends the same mesh-and-fat-graft principle to **burn/scar contractures**
(shown on a child's MCP-joint scar contracture), to **radiation-damaged tissue after lumpectomy**, and to a
**post-mastectomy, post-radiation shoulder contracture** in a physician patient in her 60s who could not
raise her arm — released and grafted with a documented one-month recovery sufficient to return to work
within days. He separately mentions a small series of **rheumatoid arthritis hands** treated the same way,
stating explicitly he is **not confident the technique affects the underlying disease** in that context,
only that "the hand sure looks better."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Residual finger contracture at the MP and PIP joints after percutaneous or open treatment**, with
  specific improvement figures reported `[10:12]`.
- **Palpable hard nodules softening ("melting") after mechanical disruption**, described as visible during
  the procedure itself `[12:14]`.
- **Post-procedure erythema (redness) as an early warning sign of fat graft failure**, in his one flagged
  complication case `[13:16]`.
- **Recurrence after fat grafting**, in at least one documented case, attributed by Khouri to insufficient
  graft volume in that area rather than a technique failure generally `[13:16]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Extensive percutaneous aponeurotomy (EPA)** — Khouri's multi-nick percutaneous release technique,
  distinct from standard limited-cut needle aponeurotomy `[01:05]`.
- **The "violin string" cutting principle** — a small nick cuts cleanly only in tissue under tension,
  giving selectivity for the taut cord over slack adjacent structures `[02:05]`.
- **RSD (reflex sympathetic dystrophy)** — named as the risk factor behind Khouri's one serious
  complication and his stated patient-selection caution `[13:16]`, `[21:29]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Meshing a skin graft** — the cord is opened with many small superficial nicks the way a skin graft is
  meshed, rather than with one large cut `[07:09]`.
- **The violin string** — tension, not blade force, determines what gets cut `[02:05]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Khouri is candid about the technique's limits: operative time is **not** reduced compared to open surgery
("it really takes a while"); the rheumatoid arthritis application is explicitly not claimed to modify
disease; and he states a personal, unproven impression rather than a tested finding when he says the
technique **"seems to alter the course of disease... rejuvenates the hand."** He flags a formal prospective
randomized trial as in progress (Hovius, ~60 patients enrolled at the time of the talk) rather than citing
it as completed evidence. His closing recommendation — **avoid the technique in young, female, high-RSD-risk
patients** — is stated as personal bias drawn from his one complication, not a established contraindication.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Extends `nice-htg21-needle-fasciotomy`**, which reports standard needle fasciotomy's recurrence at
roughly 50% at 3–5 years — Khouri's EPA-plus-fat-grafting series reports outcomes he describes as
comparable or better, though over a shorter and less rigorously matched follow-up period, so this should be
read as a promising uncontrolled series rather than a head-to-head refutation of that recurrence figure.
**Relevant to any note drawn from Eaton's or Hurst's talks on percutaneous techniques** — Khouri explicitly
positions EPA as a deliberate technical departure from the more limited-cut needle release those talks
describe, developed "gradually over five years" specifically because he judged the standard technique's
release to be insufficient.
