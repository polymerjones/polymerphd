# Dupuytren's Contracture

## Central claim

**Dupuytren's contracture is fascia in the palm turning into thickened, static cord tissue that can pull the
fingers into a bent position — it is not curable, and every current treatment removes or breaks the cord
without preventing it from coming back.** `[p.1]`, `[p.2]`.

## What it is

`[p.1]`

**Fascia is the fibrous tissue that normally covers nerves, blood vessels, muscles, and tendons in the hand,
and stabilizes the skin.** In Dupuytren's disease, "this fascia can become abnormal. It becomes thicker,
forming cords." The source is explicit about what distinguishes a cord from a tendon: "Unlike a tendon that
is moved by a muscle that shortens and lengthens, cord tissue is not connected to a muscle. Cord tissue is
static and does not move."

**There may be one cord or several, separate or connected**, and most people also develop **nodules or
bumps** in the hand. Course is unpredictable: "these nodules and cords may not change for a long time. They
can also have a slow or fast change."

## Who is affected

`[p.1]`

The source states the cause plainly as unresolved: **"The exact cause of Dupuytren's contracture is unknown
and very complex."** What is stated as known:

- **It is a hereditary disease** — "family history and ancestry play a role."
- **More common** in men, people over age 40, and people of Northern European descent.
- **Less common** in African and Middle Eastern descent.
- **Related**: smoking, diabetes, alcohol, lower body mass index, and aging.
- **Not related**: "There is no evidence that hand injuries or specific jobs lead to a higher risk of
  developing Dupuytren's contracture." A mild relationship to trauma is allowed only for someone already at
  risk, and occasionally "after a distal radius (wrist) fracture, a patient may develop a single nodule in
  the palm" that "often does not progress to result in a bent finger joint."

## How it's noticed and how severe it gets

`[p.1]`–`[p.2]`

Symptoms are **"lumps, nodules, and bands or cords on the palmar side of the hands,"** firm and stuck to the
skin, with the skin itself often thicker and puckered. The source's own analogy for this: **"Think of the
Dupuytren's palm skin like a road. Some areas are swollen and puffy like a speed bump. In other areas the
skin is puckered and pulled down like a pothole."**

**The ring finger and little finger are most commonly involved**, either hand can be affected, and each hand
can progress on its own pattern and timeline. **It is usually not painful**, though lumps can be uncomfortable
in some people. It's often first noticed as **difficulty placing the hand flat on a surface** (the tabletop
test, Figure 3) or opening the hand fully — with downstream effects on washing hands, wearing gloves, holding
large objects, using pockets, and (if the right hand is involved) shaking hands.

**Severity is hard to predict**, but the source names concrete risk markers: earlier age of onset predicts
more severe disease; men develop more severe symptoms than women; more affected relatives predicts higher
risk; and involvement elsewhere in the body — **"called Dupuytren's diathesis"** — also predicts higher risk.
That elsewhere-involvement can appear as **plantar fibromatosis** (soles of the feet), **Peyronie's disease**
(the genital location, in men), or a **knuckle pad (or Garrod's pad)** on top of the knuckles.

## Evaluation

`[p.2]`

**Not every palm lump is Dupuytren's**, so the source recommends a hand surgeon examination. "Sometimes a
history and examination is all that is needed," and imaging (x-ray, ultrasound, or MRI) is used at other
times. Factors weighed: **size, "feel," location, presence of pain, movement of the mass or skin around it,
family history, and other medical conditions.**

## Nonsurgical treatment

`[p.2]`–`[p.3]`

Two office-based options, both described as *reducing* the cord rather than removing it:

| Option | What happens | Recovery | Recurrence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Needle fasciotomy (needle aponeurotomy)** | Local numbing, then a needle cuts the cord in several places below the skin; the hand is then slowly stretched to break the cord | "Only a few days" | **"Roughly one third of patients will see the cord eventually come back after this procedure"** |
| **Collagenase injection** | Two visits: an injectable drug dissolves the cord at visit one; at visit two the numbed hand is stretched to break the cord | "Only a few days to a few weeks" | **"One third of patients will see the cord come back"** |

Both can cause small skin tears that heal over a few weeks, and both are explicitly stated to **not remove
the cord fully from below the skin** — which the source ties directly to the recurrence figure it gives for
each. Splinting and therapy may follow either, aimed at keeping the hand straight and rebuilding the ability
to make a fist.

## Surgical treatment

`[p.3]`

Two procedures, distinguished by how much tissue is removed:

- **Fasciotomy** — the tight cords are cut but not removed. "This is less invasive as only a small cut or
  cuts are used," but **"because all the tissue is not removed, there is possibly a higher chance the
  contracture could return."**
- **Fasciectomy** — cuts the cords *and* removes as much of the nodule, cords, and diseased skin as needed.
  "This often has longer wounds with more extensive dissection."

Surgery is outpatient/same-day, under a variety of anesthesia approaches, with either stitched or
intentionally-open wounds to reduce recurrence risk. **Fasciectomy recovery is about 6 weeks**, with hand use
continuing during that time, and the source frames post-surgical effort as directly determining outcome:
**"The more the patient does soon after surgery, the more successful the final result will be."** Waiting too
long to start therapy "may result in stiffness."

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Firm lumps or nodules in the palm, stuck to the skin** `[p.1]`.
- **Thickened, puckered palm skin** ("speed bump" / "pothole" pattern) `[p.1]`.
- **A cord running from the palm into a finger, pulling it toward the palm** `[p.1]`.
- **Inability to lay the hand flat on a table, or open it fully** `[p.2]`.
- **Difficulty washing hands, wearing gloves, holding large objects, using pockets, or shaking hands** `[p.2]`.
- **Lumps on the soles of the feet, or thickening on the knuckles, alongside hand symptoms** `[p.2]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Cord** — thickened, non-contractile fascia; "not connected to a muscle... static and does not move" `[p.1]`.
- **Dupuytren's diathesis** — a pattern of disease appearing elsewhere in the body (feet, genitals, knuckles)
  alongside the hand, associated with more severe disease `[p.2]`.
- **Needle fasciotomy / needle aponeurotomy** — an in-office needle procedure that cuts the cord without
  removing it `[p.2]`.
- **Fasciectomy** — surgical removal of the cord, nodule, and affected skin, as distinct from a fasciotomy
  that only cuts the cord `[p.3]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Think of the Dupuytren's palm skin like a road... a speed bump... a pothole"** `[p.1]`.

## Source-stated confidence

This is a patient-education handout from a professional hand-surgery society, not a study — its hedges are
plain-language limits on certainty rather than statistical caveats, and they are preserved here rather than
smoothed into confidence the source does not claim:

- **Cause: "unknown and very complex"** `[p.1]`.
- **Course: "It is difficult to predict how the disease will progress"** `[p.2]`.
- **Cure: "Even with treatment, the disease is not fully curable. The nodules and cords may come back in the
  same or a different location"** `[p.2]`.
- **Both nonsurgical options are stated with a specific, matching recurrence figure (roughly one third)**,
  and both are explicitly described as not removing the cord fully `[p.2]`–`[p.3]`.
- **Surgical improvement is stated as not guaranteed to last**: "this improvement may not maintained forever"
  [sic in source] `[p.3]`.
- **Splinting/stretching is explicitly stated as non-preventive**: "typically does not prevent worsening of
  the contracture but is safe to try" `[p.2]`.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Converges closely with `nice-htg21-needle-fasciotomy`**, which independently states needle fasciotomy's
recurrence at "approximately 50% at 3 to 5 years" — consistent with this source's "roughly one third" for
both nonsurgical options, though NICE's figure runs somewhat higher; both agree recurrence, not cure, is the
realistic expectation. **Converges with `nocek-2026-recurrence-systematic-review` and
`shaheen-2025-treatment-comparison-review`**, both of which independently rank needle-based treatment as
recurring more than collagenase, and collagenase more than surgical fasciectomy — the same ordering this
source's "roughly one third" figure for both nonsurgical options (without distinguishing between them) is
consistent with, once the sharper split is available. **Converges with `girft-bssh-dupuytrens-pathway`**,
whose surgical referral threshold (30° at the MCPJ or 20° at the PIPJ) gives the precise numeric version of
what this source states only qualitatively as "the contracture prevents the hand from lying flat on a
table."
