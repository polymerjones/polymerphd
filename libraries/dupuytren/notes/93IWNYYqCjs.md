# Dr. David Warwick "Collagenase — What We May Never Know" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Warwick argues, deliberately provocatively, that no valid, generalizable, unbiased randomized trial can be
designed to compare collagenase against needle fasciotomy, surgery, or skin grafting — and that clinicians
already know enough to make good individual treatment decisions without one.** His stated hypothesis:
**"the valid, generalizable, unbiased randomized trial cannot be designed to compare collagenase with either
[needle fasciotomy], fasciectomy, or even skin grafting"** `[00:00]`.

## Nine reasons, each independently sufficient

`[00:00]`–`[07:06]`:

1. **Disease heterogeneity**: a thin, skinny cord suits needle fasciotomy; a broader cord suits collagenase;
   heavy skin involvement pushes toward surgery or grafting. **"No one's going to waste money using
   collagenase on a tiny skinny cord"** — comparing treatments across a general population is comparing
   fundamentally different problems, **"twigs... with logs... apples and oranges."**
2. **Equipoise violation**: an ethical randomized trial requires genuine clinical uncertainty from both
   doctor and patient about which arm is better. In practice, **"four out of five patients requesting
   [collagenase] would never have agreed to surgery"** — patient preference alone makes real equipoise
   unattainable, and **recruitment itself becomes biased** by which patients even show up asking for which
   treatment. Surgeons carry their own individual biases about "the right treatment for the right cord" too.
3. **No validated outcome measure**: QuickDASH "doesn't work that well... not particularly sensitive"; the
   Southampton score has limited standing; **URAM is a French instrument not properly validated for English
   use.** Critically, none of these questionnaires **correlate well with either patient satisfaction or
   degree of deformity** — undermining their use as a primary trial endpoint regardless of which is chosen.
4. **Incomplete cost accounting**: citing UK NICE's own assessment, Warwick argues **societal cost of time
   off work is typically omitted** — a patient back at work within four days pays enough tax to **"pay for
   four buckets of [collagenase]"** or many needle procedures, while six weeks off work after open surgery
   carries real, uncounted societal cost, especially for self-employed patients.
5. **Incomparable complications**: a rare-but-severe complication (anaphylactic shock) can't be meaningfully
   weighed against a common-but-minor one (a skin split healing within ten days) or a surgical complication
   (nerve injury, an unhealed surgical wound at the same ten-day mark) on a single outcome scale.
6. **Recurrence isn't a clean outcome either**: recurrence after needle fasciotomy or collagenase is
   **"dead easy"** to retreat with another injection or a simple repeat procedure; **recurrence after
   surgery is "difficult and hazardous," takes longer, and puts nerves at risk** — even though surgery's raw
   recurrence *rate* is lower. Equal-sized recurrence events carry very different real burdens depending on
   the original treatment, making a shared "recurrence rate" outcome misleading on its own.
7. **Funding incentives don't align**: no pharmaceutical company would fund a trial that risks showing its
   expensive drug is no better than an inexpensive needle procedure; state or charity funders are unlikely
   to fund a study whose result, given all the biases above, **won't even be generalizable** to the next
   patient anyway.
8. **Blinding is impossible**: injecting a drug, sticking in a needle, and performing surgery are
   **obviously distinguishable** to both patient and clinician — there's no way to blind any comparison
   between them.
9. **Follow-up duration is impractical**: a proper recurrence-focused study needs **five years plus roughly
   two more to write up — seven years total** — during which patients are already demanding treatment now,
   and Warwick doubts industry sponsors could or would wait that long before commercializing.

## The conclusion

`[07:06]`–`[08:07]` — Warwick's stated position: **"I don't think you're going to design a proper randomized
study... to look at the average cord and [determine] the best treatment"** — and he believes the field
**already knows enough**, from accumulated clinical experience, to choose the right treatment for the right
patient in the right situation, without waiting for **"a probably useless randomized trial"** that couldn't
actually resolve the heterogeneity and bias problems described above.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Skin splitting during collagenase manipulation, typically healing within ten days**, contrasted directly
  with a comparably timed but categorically different surgical wound-healing complication `[05:05]`.
- **Anaphylactic shock as a rare but serious collagenase complication**, referenced as an example of a
  severity tier that resists direct comparison to more common, milder complications `[04:03]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Equipoise** — the ethical requirement that both clinician and patient hold genuine uncertainty about
  which trial arm is better before randomization is justified `[02:02]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Twigs and logs, apples and oranges"** — for why comparing treatments across genuinely different cord
  presentations produces a study that can't generalize to any individual patient `[01:01]`, `[02:02]`.
- **"Different bikes for different purposes"** — the same heterogeneity point, restated `[02:02]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Warwick discloses his own financial interest upfront ("I've made lots of money out of Dupuytren's") before
making an argument that, notably, cuts *against* his own commercial incentive to promote more studies
validating collagenase — he's arguing against the value of exactly the kind of trial that might otherwise
justify his product's use. He frames the talk explicitly as **"my hypothesis," "my opinion,"** not a
settled methodological consensus, and invites disagreement rather than presenting this as fact.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly answered by `t8kzlpSJLj0`** (Zhou's collagenase-vs-fasciectomy talk, presented the following day) —
Zhou explicitly cites this talk's argument by name and offers propensity score matching on a real-world cohort
as a practical alternative when true randomization is impractical, rather than accepting that no comparison
can be made at all. **Directly in tension with `oRdE0UKeJF0`** (Van Rijssen's randomized PNF-vs-fasciectomy trial) — that trial
is precisely the kind of comparison Warwick argues can't be done validly; notably, Van Rijssen's own trial
does grapple with several of Warwick's objections (patients were randomized rather than self-selected,
recurrence was defined with a specific quantitative threshold), so this talk is best read as a challenge to
weigh that trial's conclusions against, not a claim that no such trial has ever been attempted.
**Consistent with `Gms1cs6bxoE`** (Coleman's international trials talk) — Coleman's own account of the
collagenase trials' cost and logistical burden supports Warwick's skepticism about how feasible a truly
comprehensive comparative trial would be. **Relevant to `1BURdqs3a4U`** (Pess's technique talk) and
**`-jLfWXX3YUM`** (Seven Questions) — both implicitly rely on cord characteristics (size, location, skin
involvement) to guide treatment choice, consistent with Warwick's heterogeneity argument that treatment
selection is inherently patient-specific rather than reducible to one "best" answer. **Warwick's own debate
talk, `StgtqY8hqpw`, lives out this uncertainty directly** — arguing for collagenase over PNF while
explicitly admitting he doesn't know whether it actually produces less recurrence, a real-time example of
the comparison problem this talk describes in the abstract. **`R4WPhcOpAMo`** (Selles's consensus recurrence
definition talk) is the field's direct answer to one of Warwick's specific objections — that no validated
outcome measure exists — via a formal Delphi consensus process establishing a single standard recurrence
definition, though it doesn't address Warwick's other objections (heterogeneity, equipoise, funding).
