# Ilse Degreef MD PhD, "Surgical Outcome of Dupuytren's Disease" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**A patient-perception survey of her own first-time-surgery patients found no evidence that more extensive
surgery (total strand resection, full-thickness grafting) reduces recurrence compared to minimal, segmental
fasciectomy — if anything the reported pattern ran the other way — and Degreef's own investigation into why
uncovered a real indication bias in the underlying data.** Her stated finding: **"there was no higher
recurrence reported in minimal invasive techniques, and on the other hand there was no lower recurrence
reported in the maximal invasive techniques — and this was statistically significant"** `[06:17]`. When
grouped as total strand resection versus minimally invasive: **"it was the other way around — you had more
recurrences reported in the total strand resections, and this was statistically significant"** `[07:19]`.

## Framing: recurrence of contracture, not of disease

`[01:07]`–`[04:14]` — Degreef restates the point made elsewhere in this library: **"disease recurrence is an
inevitable consequence after surgery, as long as the patient lives long enough."** She's careful about
terminology: **"a recurrence is really a recurrence of contracture, not of disease — the disease was never
gone."** She also flags why recurrence studies are hard to compare across the literature: results depend
heavily on **follow-up duration** (2-year versus 10-year recurrence rates look completely different) and on
**how recurrence itself is defined** — extension loss, new nodules, or contracture specifically — with **"no
consensus"** on which definition is correct. Her own operational definition: **a positive Hueston tabletop
test** — an objective contracture the patient can no longer flatten against a surface.

## The study and its finding

`[04:14]`–`[07:19]` — A **retrospective survey of her own first-time-surgery patients**, with a minimum of
**two years of follow-up** (actual range 2–9 years), asking simply whether the patient believed their
contracture had recurred. Demographics matched expected Dupuytren's patterns (male:female skew, mean surgery
age 58). **Overall, 57% of patients reported recurrence.** Techniques used, by proportion: segmental
fasciectomy via various incision approaches (40% and 32% combined across two incision styles), Bruner
incisions (14%), and full-thickness grafting (14%). Comparing patient-reported recurrence by technique found
**no advantage for the more invasive approaches** — a result Degreef calls **"a little bit surprising."**

## Her own explanation, and the bias she found in it

`[07:19]`–`[08:21]` — One candidate explanation offered: **Dupuytren's is itself a fibrotic disease, and more
extensive surgery creates more scar tissue, which may itself stimulate myofibroblasts and drive
recurrence** — i.e., more aggressive surgery could be actively counterproductive, not merely unhelpful. A
second dataset (specifically on full-thickness grafting) showed roughly **50/50 outcomes in primary surgery**,
but in **secondary (revision) surgery — where surgeons deliberately chose full-thickness grafting after a
prior recurrence, reasoning "this time I'll do it right"** — **recurrence was reported universally.**

Rather than stop there, Degreef checked her own data for **indication bias**: using the **Abe risk score**,
she found a **clear correlation between the surgical technique chosen and the patient's baseline risk
score** — confirming that **surgeons were, consciously or not, selecting bigger surgery for higher-risk
patients**, which could fully explain why more invasive surgery didn't show a recurrence advantage (it was
being used on the patients most likely to recur regardless). She reran the analysis after **removing all
high-risk patients** — the same directional pattern held (segmental fasciectomy still showed no higher
recurrence, bigger surgery still showed no guaranteed benefit), but with the reduced sample size, **the
statistical significance was lost**, which she describes candidly as **"to my big frustration."**

## Her stated conclusion despite the inconclusive statistics

`[10:24]` — **"There was no higher recurrence report in segmental fasciectomy, and... maximal surgery does
not guarantee remission."** Given that, and the established practical advantages of minimally invasive
surgery (less scar tissue, faster rehabilitation, easier revision surgery if needed), she states her own
position: **"we should at least consider minimal invasive surgery if it's feasible... there is no real
argument for higher recurrence"** with the more limited approach. Her closing framing: **"the surgeon can
cut out the strands, [but] the surgeon can[not] cut out the disease itself."**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **A positive Hueston tabletop test as her operational definition of recurrence** `[04:14]`.
- **Patient-perceived recurrence versus surgeon-perceived success** — explicitly framed as potentially
  divergent, with patient perception treated as the outcome that actually matters `[04:14]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Indication bias** — the specific statistical confound Degreef uncovered in her own data: surgeons
  systematically choosing more invasive technique for higher-risk patients, which can make a genuinely
  neutral or even beneficial minimally invasive technique look artificially worse (or, as here, make a
  bigger-technique advantage disappear) in unadjusted comparisons `[09:22]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk presents study design and self-critique directly.

## Source-stated confidence

Exceptionally rigorous, deliberate self-critique — Degreef lists her own study's weaknesses unprompted
(retrospective design, reliance on patient perception rather than measured contracture data, no data on
baseline contracture severity or original surgical indication), actively hunts for and finds a bias that
would undermine her own headline finding, reruns the analysis to test it, and reports honestly that doing so
cost her statistical significance rather than hiding that result. This is one of the most self-scrutinizing
methodological talks in this library.

## Conflicts with other sources

**In tension with `L3kjpUIwHIQ`** (Leclercq's severe PIP contracture talk), which reports dermofasciectomy
recurrence at only 21% versus "a much higher rate" in her non-grafted series, framed as clear evidence
skin grafting protects against recurrence — though the two studies aren't directly comparable (Leclercq's
comparison is specifically within a severe-PIP-contracture population by technique received, not a general
first-surgery population adjusted for baseline risk the way Degreef's is). Read together, they suggest the
"more extensive surgery reduces recurrence" claim may hold specifically for severe, high-risk contracture
requiring grafting, without holding as a general rule across surgical severity — exactly the kind of
indication-bias-sensitive claim Degreef's talk warns needs careful adjustment before trusting. **Consistent
with `9LY5fyLw618`** (Hurst's collagenase talk) and **`-jLfWXX3YUM`** (Seven Questions) — both favor
minimally invasive treatment for its lower complication burden; this talk adds that minimally invasive
surgery specifically may not even carry the recurrence penalty conventionally assumed to be its tradeoff.
