# Dr. Dominic Furniss "How Should We Fund DD Research" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Research funding should be patient-driven, not just researcher-driven, and different stages of the
research pipeline require genuinely different funding sources — Furniss maps out both, and closes by
proposing an unconventional, crowdsourced funding model as worth considering.** His framing: **"we need to
have patient-driven research, as well as [work] driven by surgeons and scientists and therapists"**
`[00:00]`.

## The James Lind Alliance process

`[00:00]`–`[02:00]` — A UK organization that brings together **all stakeholders in a condition — patients,
carers, doctors, scientists** — to generate a large list of open questions, cross-checks that list against
the existing literature to remove already-answered questions, and through a structured group discussion
process, narrows it to a **"top 10" list of research priorities.** Furniss cites cleft lip and palate as a
prior example, and states this kind of list is **"very powerful"** leverage with funding bodies specifically
because it demonstrates genuine stakeholder engagement, not just researcher preference. A James Lind
Alliance process for **hand surgery generally, including Dupuytren's, was about to begin in the UK** at the
time of this talk.

## Mapping funding sources to research stage

`[02:00]`–`[06:07]` — Furniss walks through the research pipeline with UK-specific (but internationally
analogous) examples at each stage:

- **Basic science**: funded by government bodies and charities — he cites his own funding from the Wellcome
  Trust (covering salary, PhD students, and experiments directly), alongside the Medical Research Council,
  Arthritis Research UK, and the pan-European Research Council (ERC), the latter specifically requiring
  **international collaboration** to be competitive.
- **Early translational/pilot studies**: professional surgical societies often have small **"pump-priming"**
  grants suited to this stage, used to generate preliminary data before applying for larger trial funding;
  pharmaceutical companies can also help here, particularly when they already have a relevant product.
- **Full randomized clinical trials**: **very expensive**, citing the prior day's collagenase talk as **"a
  really instructive lecture about how long it takes and how expensive it is"** — notable, Furniss points
  out, because that was for a drug **already approved**, implying a genuinely new treatment would cost even
  more and take even longer. The UK's National Institute for Health Research is the prime funder at this
  stage (with equivalents in the US and Canada); pharmaceutical companies are experienced funders of
  pill-based trials specifically, but **"not quite so experienced... when you're looking at a disease that's
  mainly... surgically treated"** — for example, injecting a drug into a Dupuytren's cord — which Furniss
  frames as an opportunity for closer academic-industry collaboration rather than a dead end.

## An unconventional funding idea: crowdsourced citizen science

`[06:07]`–`[08:14]` — Furniss describes the **American/British Gut Project**, a citizen-science microbiome
study: participants pay **$75** for a kit, submit a sample, and receive their own microbiome sequencing data
in return — the project had raised **almost $900,000 from just over 9,000 participants** at the time of the
talk (with volume discounts and even a pet-microbiome option). He connects this to a separate, more informal
observation about medical crowdfunding (citing an example a colleague described of families raising money
for a child's cancer treatment via social-media-style fundraising platforms), proposing — half-seriously —
that Dupuytren's research consider a similar **direct-to-public, modern funding model**, while acknowledging
he doesn't yet know how such a project would translate to hand-tissue research specifically.

None directly — this is a research-funding-strategy talk, not a discussion of clinical presentation.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **James Lind Alliance** — the UK stakeholder-engagement process for jointly setting research priorities
  across patients, carers, and clinicians `[00:00]`.
- **Pump-priming funding** — small, early-stage grants (often from professional societies) used to generate
  preliminary data before pursuing larger trial funding `[04:03]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The Gut Project's citizen-science funding model** — a concrete precedent for direct-to-public,
  pay-to-participate research funding, offered as a template worth considering for Dupuytren's `[06:07]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Furniss frames this talk explicitly as a starting point for audience discussion rather than a finished
proposal — **"I thought actually I need to get the members of the audience to do most of the work"** — and
closes by openly admitting uncertainty about how the crowdsourced model would even work for hand-tissue
research ("I'm not quite sure how we get samples of a finger"), rather than presenting it as a ready-made
solution.
