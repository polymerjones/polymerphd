# Debbie Larson BScOT, "Splinting After Contracture Release for Dupuytren's Disease" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**A well-powered, multi-center randomized trial found no benefit from routine night splinting after
fasciectomy — and when the analysis was narrowed to only truly-adherent splint users versus truly-unsplinted
patients, splinting looked significantly *worse*, not better, for extension and satisfaction.** The study's
stated conclusion: **"a policy of wait and see, as opposed to splinting everyone, is equally effective but
perhaps much cheaper"** `[09:14]`.

## Trial design

`[00:03]`–`[02:06]` — A **pragmatic, multi-center randomized controlled trial**, UK-charity-funded (Action
Medical Research), run through the North Dupuytren's Group across **five centers in the East of England**,
recruiting **October 2007 to January 2009.** Eligibility: patients listed for **fasciectomy or
dermofasciectomy** (fasciotomy patients and first-webspace contracture excluded, the latter for measurement
difficulty). Patients were **randomized after surgery, at the first hand therapy appointment**, to **hand
therapy alone** or **hand therapy plus a night splint for six months**, stratified by center and surgical
procedure. **Surgeons were blind to group allocation at the time of operation**; both surgeons and therapists
used whatever technique they normally preferred, with data collected on modalities, frequency, and duration
used. The splint itself: a **static thermoplastic, hand-based, volar splint**, applied at the first therapy
visit (~5–10 days post-op), **worn at night only**, with **no tension applied for the first three weeks** to
allow wound healing.

## A built-in safety net for the no-splint group

`[02:06]` — To address clinician concern about withholding splinting from patients who might actually need
it, the trial used a **pre-agreed, standard operating procedure**: if a patient in the no-splint group showed
a contracture increase of **15 degrees at the PIP or 20 degrees at the MCP** from their post-op baseline, a
splint would be applied to them too — a criterion **agreed in advance by all surgeons and therapists
involved**, not left to individual judgment mid-trial.

## Sample size and recruitment

`[03:07]`–`[04:08]` — Primary outcome: the **DASH** (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand)
questionnaire; secondary outcomes included active range of motion (MCP, PIP, DIP across all four digits, with
only affected digits analyzed), patient satisfaction, and splint-diary-recorded adherence. Sample size was
calculated using a **minimally clinically important difference of 15 DASH points, 90% power, 5%
significance, allowing 20% dropout** — yielding a target of **128 participants.** The trial **exceeded this,
recruiting 154 with only 6 lost to follow-up** (from 218 invited, 172 consented, 46 excluded post-consent) —
a notably well-powered, low-attrition study for this literature. Randomization split evenly: **77 splint, 77
no-splint.**

## Three analyses, three different pictures

`[05:09]`–`[08:11]` — Baseline demographics and scores were closely matched between groups (age, sex,
occupation, procedure type, digits operated, prior surgery, baseline DASH ~16, similar starting range of
motion), supporting a fair comparison. Three separate analyses were run:

1. **Intention-to-treat (ITT)** — analyzing patients by their *assigned* group regardless of actual
   adherence (77 vs 77, even though some crossed over). **No statistically significant difference** in DASH
   score, total active flexion, total active extension, or satisfaction at 12 months. Described as **"the
   most appropriate analysis in a pragmatic trial,"** most generalizable to real clinical practice, but one
   that can understate a true effect if adherence is imperfect.
2. **Per-protocol analysis** — restricted to patients who actually followed their assigned protocol
   (≥50% splint adherence in the splint group; only protocol-triggered crossovers included from the
   no-splint group). **Still no statistically significant difference** between groups.
3. **Explanatory analysis** — the strictest comparison: **truly adherent splint-wearers versus patients who
   truly never received a splint**, excluding all crossovers and non-adherent patients from both sides. This
   analysis **did find statistically significant differences — but in favor of *not* splinting**, for total
   active extension and patient satisfaction at 12 months.

The presenter is explicit about why the third result should be read cautiously: **"the groups are no longer
equal, and we wonder if we can truly compare them"** — once adherence-based exclusion is applied, the
remaining groups are no longer the product of pure randomization and may differ in unmeasured ways (e.g.
disease severity, motivation, or the reasons a patient needed a "rescue" splint in the first place).

## Adherence data

`[07:10]`–`[08:11]` — Of 77 patients assigned a splint, **12 did not meet the adherence criterion**; of the
22 no-splint-group patients who ultimately received a splint, **5 did not meet it either** — meaning even
protocol-triggered splinting wasn't always followed through consistently.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Contracture increase after fasciectomy, quantified with explicit trigger thresholds (15° PIP / 20° MCP)
  for adding a rescue splint** `[02:06]`.
- **Patient satisfaction and DASH-measured disability**, tracked as primary/secondary outcomes independent
  of raw range-of-motion numbers `[03:07]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Pragmatic trial** — a trial design evaluating an intervention under real-world clinical variation
  (surgeon preference, therapist technique, patient adherence) rather than under tightly controlled
  conditions `[00:03]`.
- **Intention-to-treat versus per-protocol versus explanatory analysis** — three progressively stricter ways
  of analyzing the same randomized data, explicitly shown here to produce different conclusions `[05:09]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk presents trial methodology and results directly.

## Source-stated confidence

Unusually transparent about limitations: **patient and researcher blinding were both explicitly not
feasible** given the nature of a splint intervention; the explanatory analysis's favorable-to-no-splint
result is presented with an immediate caveat about the loss of true randomization once adherence-based
exclusion is applied; and the presenter closes by **questioning whether DASH was even the right primary
outcome measure** for this population, inviting collaboration on better outcome measures rather than
defending the choice already made.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Is very likely one of the specific studies referenced secondhand in `-jLfWXX3YUM`** (Eaton's Seven
Questions video), which states **"a half a dozen well-conducted randomized, prospective, controlled"** studies
showed no long-term benefit of hand therapy and splinting after open surgery — this talk's ITT and
per-protocol results (no significant difference) match that description closely, and its explanatory-analysis
finding (splinting performing *worse*) is consistent with Eaton's further claim that "one [study] even showed
worse results with aggressive splinting compared to gentle splinting." **Directly relevant to `MO1w1Ed-Yqc`**
(Skirven's hand therapy talk), which cites Gelberman's dynamic-splinting compliance data showing a strong
benefit for compliant patients — the two findings are not necessarily contradictory (different splint types:
static night splint here versus continuous dynamic splinting there), but should be read as testing different
interventions, not the same one. **Relevant to `1BURdqs3a4U`** (Pess's technique talk), whose own
3–4-month splinting practice is stated as personally held rather than evidence-based — this trial is a
concrete example of exactly the kind of rigorous evidence that practice currently lacks.
