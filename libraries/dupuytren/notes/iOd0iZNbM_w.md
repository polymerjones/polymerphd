# Dr. Lee Osterman, "Cline's Contracture: Dupuytren Was a Thief" 2010 Miami Dupuytren's Symposium

## Central claim

**Henry Cline described and operated on what we now call Dupuytren's contracture roughly 50 years before
Dupuytren's famous 1831 lecture, and Cooper's writings predate it by a decade — Dupuytren neither
acknowledged either predecessor nor even published his own lecture himself, yet the disease bears his name.**
Osterman's closing line states the thesis directly: **"how can we find truth when the namesake condition is
founded on a lie? Therefore my proposal for this meeting is that Dupuytren's contracture should in fact be
called Cline's contracture"** `[19:27]`.

## The evidence for Cline and Cooper's priority

`[04:08]`–`[07:12]` — **Henry Cline**, a prominent London surgeon (trained under John Hunter, president of
the Royal College of Surgeons), **dissected two contracture-affected cadavers in 1777** and recorded palmar
fascia involvement, proposed it was primarily a disease of laboring people, and described an operative cure
— recognizing the risk to vessels and nerves. Cline never published this himself; it survives only because
his student **John Windsor published Cline's teaching, in quotation, in 1808**: *"one or more of the
tendinous columns of the palmar aponeurosis sometimes becomes contracted and thickened; fingers are bent
into the palm of the hand. The treatment is easy and efficacious: it consists in cutting through the
[cord]... performing the operation carefully, dissecting fiber by fiber, [avoiding] the blood vessels and
nerves beneath. Lastly, the finger or fingers may be kept extended afterwards by a splint, [without which]
the process might recur"* `[05:10]`. Osterman notes this already contains the basic tenets still recognized
today.

Cline's student **Astley Cooper** — later also president of the Royal College of Surgeons — carried on the
tradition for nearly a quarter century, but held a more conservative view once the PIP joint itself was
involved: **"when the [joint] is contracted, nothing should be attempted for the patient's relief, as no
operation or other means will succeed"** — reserving surgery for cases where a single band or MP-level
disease was the driver `[06:11]`. Cooper likewise linked the condition to repetitive manual work — "the
hammer, the oar" — the same occupational theory Cline had proposed.

## How Dupuytren still got the credit

`[07:12]`–`[16:25]` — Dupuytren visited Cooper in 1826, and was separately visited by Cooper at least twice
before his own famous lecture. Osterman documents specific, damaging overlaps: Dupuytren **misquoted Cooper
as saying the disease was "incurable"** (Cooper had only said this of contractures once they'd extended to
the fingers) and **used Cooper's own "hammer toe" foot-variant observation without attribution.** Dupuytren's
first operation was performed **June 12, 1831**, on a wine merchant referred to him; his second, more famous
demonstration was on his own coachman, presented in a **December 5, 1831 lecture** — in which he **reviewed
and dismissed prior treatments (fumigation, leeches, sulfur) as ineffective** and argued against dividing
the flexor tendons. Critically, **Dupuytren never published this lecture himself** — it was transcribed
and published verbatim by his assistants, Paillard and Marx, in a Paris weekly journal, and from there
republished in Dupuytren's own multi-volume *Leçons*, then in *The Lancet*, then back in England by 1834.

Osterman's explanation for why this worked: **Paris was, at the time, the center of medical publishing and
"medical journalism"** — three weekly and two monthly journals were active there — while **Cline predated
organized medical journalism entirely, and London was comparatively "isolated"** as a medical publishing
center. Dupuytren's version reached a far wider, faster-moving audience than Cline's or Cooper's ever had,
despite Dupuytren's own lecture adding relatively little beyond what Cline had already described: **cause
attributed to trauma/occupation, dismissal of a tendon-based explanation, disease described as progressive,
predilection for the ring finger noted, joint preservation noted, palmar fascia identified as the tissue
involved, open transverse incision with post-operative extension splinting** — Osterman calls this list
Dupuytren's "stolen contributions."

## Why the challenge to Dupuytren's priority failed

`[16:25]`–`[17:25]` — Contemporary rivals (Jean Blaise, Alfred Velpeau, and others) disputed Dupuytren's
account, and some argued that a case of *bilateral* disease in a coachman disproved Dupuytren's occupational
"whip hand" causation theory outright. Osterman's assessment: they were **"wrong"** on the underlying cause
even while right to challenge Dupuytren's originality — Cline and Dupuytren, he argues, both correctly
identified the palmar fascia as the source tissue, regardless of the credit dispute.

## The modern lineage, briefly traced

`[17:25]`–`[19:27]` — Osterman closes with a fast succession of later figures, credited by name for specific
contributions: **MacIndoe** (popularized limited fasciectomy with Z-plasties), **Charles McCash** (the open
palm technique), **John Hueston** (Australian plastic surgeon, early work connecting genetic predisposition
to disease prevalence, and — quoting him directly — **"I have a dream that one day Dupuytren's disease will
be treated without surgery"**), **Robert McFarland** (anatomical organization of the field, via the *Green*
textbook chapter), and finally **needle aponeurotomy and collagenase (Xiaflex)**, credited to the modern
symposium's own presenters. Osterman's closing statistic: **"despite 200 years of scientific study,
despite over 2,100 publications since 1900... the cause and the cure remain elusive."**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Progressive bending of the ring and little fingers into the palm, historically documented as early as
  the 17th century** `[02:05]`–`[03:07]` (Felix Platter of Basel, 1614).
- **PIP joint contracture specifically flagged by Cooper as a marker of poor surgical candidacy** — "when
  the joint is contracted, nothing should be attempted... no operation... will succeed" `[06:11]`, an early
  clinical observation resonant with later, quantified treatment-window findings elsewhere in this library.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Occupational causation theory** — Cline's and Cooper's shared early hypothesis linking the disease to
  repetitive manual labor (the hammer, the oar), predating any genetic explanation `[05:10]`, `[07:12]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The entire talk as historical narrative** — Osterman frames the priority dispute explicitly through a
  theatrical lens (naming the historical figures "our cast," structured "like a school for Dupuytren's
  scandal") `[03:07]`, a reusable framing device for presenting the eponym controversy to a lay audience.
- **"How can we find truth when the namesake condition is founded on a lie?"** `[19:27]`.

## Source-stated confidence

This is explicitly argumentative history, not a clinical or scientific claim — Osterman states his own
thesis as a **"proposal"** at the close, not an established fact, and acknowledges the counter-evidence
(the bilateral-coachman case used to attack Dupuytren's occupational theory) rather than omitting it, even
while disagreeing with the conclusion his contemporaries drew from it.
