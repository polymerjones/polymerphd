# Dr. Wolfgang Wach Discusses the Dupuytren Society at the 2010 International Symposium

## Central claim

**The surgeon's and the patient's definition of a "successful" treatment can genuinely diverge, even for the
same procedure and the same person — Wach illustrates this from his own case, not a hypothetical.** After a
fasciectomy he calls technically successful (no recurrence at the treated site 10 years later), he developed
**"a rapid extension in other areas of both hands... six new nodules within four weeks"** starting almost
immediately after surgery: **"although the surgery itself was certainly very successful, as a patient I felt
horrible... it's an example that the perception of the surgeon and of the patient can differ quite [a
lot]"** `[02:11]`–`[03:13]`.

## A patient's own case history, told in detail

`[01:10]`–`[05:17]` — Wach's timeline: first nodule ~28 years before the talk, treated with **radiotherapy**,
with **no recurrence** at that site since — "I consider that a success." A second nodule appeared about 8
years later on the other hand; **uncertain whether radiotherapy or spontaneous factors had helped the
first case, he chose to observe rather than treat** — in retrospect calling himself "not very educated" at
the time. That nodule progressed to require **fasciectomy roughly 10 years before this talk**, with no
recurrence at the surgical site, but triggering the rapid multi-nodule flare described above. He also
reports **Ledderhose disease** (two small, minimally bothersome plantar nodules).

Separately, as a **preventive measure**, Wach wore a night splint on a PIP-joint nodule — convenient to use,
but he developed **a new fibrotic nodule specifically at the edge of the splint**, thickening over three to
four months until he stopped wearing it. He connects this directly to the **"exaggerated wound healing"**
theory of Dupuytren's: **"I didn't have a wound, but maybe something nagging a little bit"** was enough
mechanical irritation to trigger a new fibrotic response — offered as a personal, uncontrolled observation,
not a study finding.

## Two persistent myths, checked against real data

`[05:17]`–`[07:22]` — **Male:female ratio is not a fixed multiple**, despite frequent publication claims of
a specific "X times more common in men" figure: **the true relationship is that men develop disease earlier**,
so the apparent ratio depends entirely on what age you measure at — closer to 10:1 around age 40, but
reported as close to **1:1 at age 80 and above** in some publications. Wach states plainly that **why men
present earlier remains unexplained**, despite various attempted explanations, none of which he finds
convincing. Separately, he challenges the **"Viking disease"**/Nordic-descent framing directly with
geographic case data: **documented cases in Japan, Spain, Norway, and Bosnia** — **"a global disease,"** not
one confined to regions of Norse historical contact, even as prevalence clearly varies by population.

## What the Dupuytren Society does

`[00:10]`–`[12:28]` — Founded **2003**, based in Germany "for historical reasons," structured explicitly as
a **joint physician-and-patient organization**, run entirely by **unpaid volunteers** and funded by
donations alone — "we are chronically short of money." Concrete numbers: **English- and German-language
forums with about 50,000 registered users, roughly 600 daily forum visits**, and an estimated **50,000–
100,000 patients reached per year** (explicitly labeled an estimate — "we don't count them"). Activities:
**patient support** (local groups, forums, representation to bodies like NICE and NHS); **advocacy for
staged treatment**, citing a roughly-50-year-old publication (attributed to "Luck") that already called for
classifying disease stage and matching therapy to it — **"that's still true today, unfortunately"**, implying
limited practical progress since; **research support**, including informal patient surveys run through the
Society's own mailing list (~1,500 patient email addresses) — one example finding **niacin use showing "some
effect... but not dramatic"** on disease when taken long enough; and a database initiative, **IDOP**,
intended to pool clinic data across countries into one shared, statistically stronger dataset, open to
contributing clinics for their own research use. The Society also administers the **Dupuytren Award**,
recognizing exceptional research or clinical-management publications, carrying a €1,000 prize, restricted to
lead applicants under 45 (to specifically support early-career researchers), judged jointly by the Society's
advisory and management boards.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Rapid appearance of multiple new nodules across both hands shortly after a technically successful
  fasciectomy** — described as Wach's own post-surgical experience `[02:11]`.
- **A new fibrotic nodule triggered by localized mechanical irritation from a night splint**, absent any
  actual wound `[04:16]`.
- **Male:female presentation age gap**, with the apparent sex ratio narrowing sharply by advanced age
  `[05:17]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **IDOP** — the Dupuytren Society's proposed international shared database pooling data across
  participating clinics `[11:26]`.
- **"Viking disease"** — named explicitly as a misleading, geographically inaccurate label the Society
  pushes back against `[07:22]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated beyond the direct personal narrative — the talk relies on Wach's own case as illustration rather
than metaphor.

## Source-stated confidence

Wach is unusually careful to separate his own uncontrolled personal observations from anything
evidence-based: the splint-nodule connection is framed as "I found that remarkable," not a claim of
causation; the niacin survey finding is explicitly hedged ("some effect... not dramatic"); and the
patient-reach estimate is labeled directly as an estimate the Society doesn't actually measure. He states
outright that the cause of the male-earlier-onset pattern is unknown despite attempted explanations.
