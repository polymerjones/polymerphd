# Dr. Clayton Peimer "PIP Joint Minimally Invasive Methods" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Whatever treatment is chosen, recurrence reflects the underlying disease persisting, not treatment
failure — and collagenase can be used repeatedly for recurrence after essentially any prior treatment,
including prior collagenase itself, provided technique is careful and patient expectations are managed.**
Peimer's closing summary: **"IP joint correction at both levels is very possible with enzyme. The disease
doesn't change — recurrences are recurrences, the disease is still there — and individual results will
vary"** `[12:43]`.

## Why straight isn't automatically better

`[01:03]`–`[02:04]` — Citing the literature (with reference to Badalamente's presentation the prior day),
Peimer notes **"successful" surgery for the PIP joint is typically defined as a 24-degree improvement and a
50% contracture reduction** — not full correction — and that prognosis worsens with **long-standing
deformity, severe deformity, and combined (multi-joint) disease.** He makes a specific functional point with
a concrete example: **a retired blue-collar patient who golfs** — getting his hand fully straight but leaving
him unable to grip a golf club is not, in Peimer's framing, a satisfied patient, regardless of the
measured angular correction.

## Comparing recurrence and complication rates across treatments

`[01:03]`–`[03:06]` — Open fasciectomy/fasciotomy: **painful, prolonged recovery, complications in up to half
of cases per the published literature**, and some patients end up **straighter but functionally worse** —
able to extend the hand but unable to make a fist. Needle fasciotomy: **less expensive, quicker correction,
but quicker and more frequent recurrence.** He cites a **needle recurrence rate of 85% at 5 years** —
consistent with the randomized trial data reported elsewhere in this library — and, after an audience
correction mid-talk, a **collagenase (enzyme) recurrence rate of 46%**, with **roughly 80% of his own
enzyme-treated patients never requiring retreatment.** Some additional recurrence figures are given for
post-surgical deformity (30 degrees or more of recurrence at specific follow-up intervals) but the exact
percentages are not confidently legible in this transcript and are omitted here rather than guessed at.

## Repeat collagenase for recurrence, regardless of prior treatment

`[06:11]`–`[08:17]` — Peimer walks through several real patient cases (shown as video/photo material not
independently verifiable from the transcript alone) illustrating **collagenase retreatment after a prior
surgical correction recurred**, and **collagenase retreatment after a prior collagenase treatment itself
recurred**, in one case administered in stages when a patient specifically declined further surgery. His
framing of why this is reasonable, addressing an implicit objection that repeat injection is somehow
incomplete treatment: **"we leave matrix, we leave cells, we leave both — we do radical surgery, we still
leave both. We're not doing a cancer operation; we're not taking out every cell."** In one case with prior
extensive surgery and skin grafting, a severe boutonnière-pattern recurrence was treated down to full (zero-
degree) correction and remained stable at four years of follow-up, to the point the patient was followed
until his death from unrelated causes.

## Practical technique pearls

`[11:41]`–`[12:43]` — Stated directly as lessons learned: **adequate anesthesia both before injection and
before manipulation is critical**; correction requires **repetitive, persistent effort** — not just pulling
one finger once, but a combination of **massaging and pulling**, sometimes requiring a forceful, deliberate
effort ("a significant, substantial... effort") especially for thick cords; and **splinting in the first
three months matters**, based on his own anecdotal observation that his worst outcomes tend to be patients
who didn't wear a splint during that window (explicitly flagged by Peimer as anecdotal, not data-backed). He
also describes **double dosing** — splitting one collagenase visit's dose or anesthesia across two cords or
sessions — as something at least one patient specifically waited to have available before agreeing to treat
his second hand.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Loss of functional grip (e.g., inability to hold a golf club) despite a straighter finger** — used to
  argue that angular correction alone is an incomplete outcome measure `[01:03]`.
- **Recurrent contracture presenting as "almost the same cord" reappearing after collagenase**, illustrated
  directly with a before/after/recurrence case sequence `[06:11]`.
- **Bruising, swelling, and hematoma as expected but manageable side effects of injection**, shown in one
  case as more pronounced than typical `[08:17]`.
- **Combined multi-joint (MP/PIP/DIP) contracture presenting together**, framed as the norm rather than the
  exception in real patients: **"patients don't present often with isolated PIP joints... you have to treat
  what comes"** `[04:08]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Double dosing** — splitting a collagenase treatment visit's dose or timing across two cords/sessions
  rather than treating everything at once `[09:18]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"We're not doing a cancer operation"** — used to argue that leaving some residual matrix and cells
  behind after collagenase (rather than surgically excising everything) is an appropriate, not incomplete,
  standard for this disease `[06:11]`.
- **The golf-club patient** — illustrating that a straight finger without functional grip is not a true
  treatment success `[01:03]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Peimer is explicit that his splinting observation is **"entirely anecdotal"** despite being a strongly held
personal impression, and distinguishes cited published literature (recurrence rates, complication rates)
from his own case-based teaching points. He closes by reiterating that **"individual results will vary"** —
a caveat he repeats rather than treating his own case videos as generalizable proof.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly corroborates `oRdE0UKeJF0`** (Van Rijssen's randomized PNF-vs-fasciectomy trial) — Peimer's cited
85%-at-5-years needle recurrence figure closely matches that trial's own finding, independent corroboration
of an unusually high needle-fasciotomy recurrence rate from a separate clinician's cited literature review.
**Directly echoes `1Xqmh80rYPQ`** (Degreef's surgical outcome talk) — both frame recurrence explicitly as
"recurrence of contracture, not of disease," treating the underlying condition as never actually resolved by
any treatment. **Consistent with `1BURdqs3a4U`** (Pess's technique talk) — both emphasize that achieving a
functional, not necessarily perfect, outcome is the realistic goal, and both describe hands-on technique
details (massage, persistent manipulation) as decisive for durable correction.
