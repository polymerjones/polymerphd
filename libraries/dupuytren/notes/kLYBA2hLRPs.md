# Dr. Charles Eaton: "Dupuytren Awareness" — Full Lecture

## Central claim

**Eaton argues Dupuytren's disease is really two parallel, largely non-overlapping problems — a biologic
disease process and its anatomic consequences — and that surgery, no matter how refined, only ever addresses
the anatomic half; without a way to measure the underlying biology directly, treatment is applied by guesswork
rather than matched to each patient's actual disease behavior.** He frames this directly: **"it's like an egg
with two yolks — it's got two different things going on. It has a biologic aspect and it has an anatomic
aspect, and these run in parallel but don't overlap that much... currently the only thing that surgeons have
to offer" only "partly impacts the risk of going from a straight finger back down to a bent finger"**
`[12:13]`–`[13:14]`.

## Anatomy: the palm as a quilt

`[00:00]`–`[04:05]` — Dupuytren's most visible sign is hand deformity, but the disease can involve areas
beyond the hand and is often misdiagnosed, especially early. Physical findings fall into three categories:
**nodules** (lumps in the palm, often mistaken for calluses), **cords** (string-like tightening under the
skin), and **contracture** (a cord tight enough to prevent full finger extension). Related findings can appear
on the **backs of the knuckles** (knuckle pads) and in the **arch of the foot** (Ledderhose disease). Eaton
describes the palm's anatomy as unique in the body (shared only with the foot's arch): layered like a
**quilt** — cloth, padding, cloth, stitched through all three layers — anchoring the skin to the bone beneath
so it doesn't slide during gripping. This explains the **palmar creases** (points of attachment) and the
**palmar mounds** — normal, unconstrained fat pads between the fingers that can look like bumps once adjacent
diseased tissue retracts, even though the mounds themselves are entirely normal tissue.

## Genetics, triggers, and disease progression

`[05:07]`–`[06:07]` — The single biggest risk factor is **genetic**: a parent or sibling with the disease gives
a **one-in-four to one-in-three chance** of developing it. Not every genetic carrier develops disease — Eaton
frames the open question as why some carriers get it and others don't, with the leading hypothesis being some
**metabolic or physical trigger** (injury or heavy hand use) that starts the process, though the triggering
mechanism itself remains unknown, supported only by anecdotal evidence. Progression follows a **predictable
sequence of stages** (nodule → cord → contracture → treatment → possible recurrence) even though **whether or
when** any individual progresses through that sequence is not predictable.

## Detailed epidemiology

`[06:07]`–`[09:09]` — In the US, prevalence follows a clear ethnic gradient: **most common in Caucasians, less
common in Hispanic Americans, less common still in African Americans, and least common in Asian Americans.**
By **age 50, 1 in 10 Caucasian men** show some evidence of disease in the palm; by **age 70, that rises to 1
in 4.** Women lag due to delayed onset but reach **1 in 10 by age 70**, with numbers continuing to rise after
that. The **male-to-female ratio changes dramatically with age**: in people in their **40s, men outnumber
women roughly 30 to 1**; by **age 85, the ratio flattens to roughly 1 to 1**, and past that age it becomes
**more common in women than men** (partly because men have shorter average lifespans). Overall, roughly **5%
of the entire US population** — 1 in 20 Americans — show some evidence of disease; among the **at-risk group
(age 35+), that rises to about 9%**, comparable to the prevalence of diabetes in that same age range. Scaled
across the population: **roughly 15 million Americans age 35+** show some evidence of disease; **most of this
group has early disease without bent fingers**; **about 3 million have bent fingers**; and **about three-
quarters of a million have a severe form** with a high recurrence rate after treatment, referred to as
**diathesis.**

## Diagnosis difficulty

`[09:09]`–`[10:11]` — Despite affecting so many people, Dupuytren's is often **misdiagnosed or missed**,
especially early. Patients with an affected family member are often correctly identified by that relative;
patients without that context frequently **misdiagnose themselves as arthritis or tendinitis.** Patients whose
first diagnosis comes from a physician (not a family member) typically **see more than two doctors** before
getting an accurate diagnosis — notable because the condition is currently diagnosed **purely by look and
feel**, with **no blood test or other objective diagnostic test** available.

## Personal, social, and systemic impact

`[10:11]`–`[12:13]` — Patient surveys describe impacts extending well beyond bent fingers: **pain, stiffness,
and hand fatigue** that are hard to articulate — nothing life-threatening individually, but cutting across
daily life broadly enough that some patients describe the condition as simply **disabling.** Socially, **the
hand and face are the only body parts routinely visible to others**, and the handshake — the standard first
social interaction — becomes an **ordeal** rather than a natural gesture for someone with visible hand
deformity. Beyond the hand itself: **failed Dupuytren's surgery is the most common reason for elective (non-
emergency, non-trauma) finger amputation** — a direct index, in Eaton's framing, of how far current treatment
still has to go. More broadly, patients with bent fingers from Dupuytren's have, **on average, shorter life
expectancy** and an **increased risk of a variety of cancers**, for reasons not currently understood — evidence,
Eaton argues, that Dupuytren's is not merely a local hand problem but a **systemic medical condition.**

## Biology versus anatomy — the "two yolks"

`[12:13]`–`[17:20]` — This is the lecture's organizing framework. **Biology** governs the risk of developing a
nodule in the first place, and the risk of progressing from nodule to cord to contracture — but biology has
little effect on whether a bent finger can be straightened by treatment. **Anatomy/procedure** (the specific
surgical technique and its technical execution) governs the risk of successfully straightening a bent finger,
and partially affects the risk of recurrence afterward. Surgeons currently only have tools that act on the
anatomic side. Two categories of correction procedure exist: **open surgery** (fasciectomy — removing diseased
tissue directly; dermofasciectomy — fasciectomy plus a skin graft, aimed specifically at reducing recurrence
risk without improving straightness beyond what fasciectomy alone achieves) and **minimally invasive
treatment** (no tissue removed; tight tissue is weakened or cut, then the finger is pulled straight). Trade-
off: **open surgery lasts longer on average before recurrence**; **minimally invasive treatment recovers
faster, has a lower complication rate, and allows safer repeat treatment if needed.** More radical procedures
attempt to control the underlying biology through bigger surgery, but Eaton is explicit this is treating
biology with a tool (surgery) not really designed for it. Long-term outcome depends heavily on the patient's
own biology: **mild biology with severe contracture still tends to have good long-term outcomes**, while
**severe biology, regardless of contracture severity, predicts worse long-term outcomes and more repeat
procedures** — a direct illustration of the difficulty of controlling a medical condition with a surgical tool.

## The outcome-measurement problem, and a "lucky" case

`[17:20]`–`[20:23]` — Current outcome measures (achieving a straight finger, or needing retreatment; the
measured joint angle) are precise but incomplete — they don't capture pain, stiffness, or functional
awkwardness independent of the raw angle, and there is currently **no way to measure biologic outcome at
all** — whether a treatment actually affected the disease process, as opposed to just the visible deformity.
Eaton illustrates this with his own patient: a man with severe bilateral contractures who refused
fasciectomy/dermofasciectomy and instead had needle aponeurotomy — **13 years later, with no further
treatment, he looks great.** Eaton's own assessment: **"this is due to luck... I didn't have a good way to
assess what his biology was. I thought he would do much worse than this"** `[19:22]` — a direct, personal
illustration of why treatment guidelines remain uncertain: without a way to measure biology, physicians risk
both under-treating (false hope, unexpected retreatment) and over-treating (unnecessary complications and
recovery time with no added benefit).

## A systems-biology argument

`[20:23]`–`[25:32]` — Reviewing standard disease categories, Dupuytren's resists easy classification: it has
**inflammatory characteristics** and involves wound-repair biology; no evidence of a circulation or clotting
problem; **genetic aspects**; immune system involvement that remains poorly characterized; it is **not
cancer, not a neoplasm, not an independently growing tumor** — it is a **reactive process**, a response to a
trigger rather than autonomous growth; no evidence of infection, environmental cause, or nutritional
deficiency; it is a disease of aging, not of children; and, to the extent the palm functions like an organ,
it is a **disease of organs.** Eaton argues this resistance to easy categorization reflects that Dupuytren's,
like many chronic diseases, is a **systems biology** problem — not reducible to one cause — contrasting this
with a century of research seeking the single causal "spigot" to turn off (a logic he traces to historical
success treating infectious disease with a single targeted medicine). His analogy: studying Dupuytren's tissue
one component at a time (**"that purple cell," that one protein**) is like trying to explain a **traffic jam**
by studying one car's nuts and bolts, or the asphalt — you need the big picture. His conclusion: **Dupuytren's
probably isn't one disease, probably doesn't have one cause, and probably won't have one solution.**

## The current triage flowchart, and a car-without-brakes analogy

`[24:29]`–`[27:36]` — As currently practiced: a patient diagnosed with an early nodule/cord can ignore it or
try an unproven treatment (effectiveness hard to quantify without good long-term outcome measurement);
treatment generally isn't considered clinically until contracture exceeds **20 degrees**; once treated, there
is **no "off-ramp"** — no cure exists, so the patient returns to watching for recurrence in the treated finger
or new disease elsewhere. Eaton's analogy: **"Dupuytren's is like a car that needs brakes — we need something
to slow down and stop the progression of disease."** To compare which "brakes" (treatments) work, you'd need
a **speedometer** — but current tools amount only to an **odometer** (how bent the finger is, i.e., how far
the disease has already traveled), not a measure of how fast it's currently progressing.

## Trait, state, and rate biomarkers

`[27:36]`–`[28:38]` — Eaton defines three biomarker categories: a **trait biomarker** (e.g., DNA) indicates
lifetime *risk* without indicating current disease status ("what's on the menu," not "what's being served");
a **state biomarker** (the current protractor/angle measurement) indicates *cumulative* change to date, not
current activity; a **rate biomarker** — which doesn't yet exist for Dupuytren's — would indicate *current*
disease activity and near-term risk. Developing a **Dupuytren rate biomarker panel** is framed as essential
for predicting long-term outcomes, matching treatment to disease, and — most importantly — making it possible
to actually **test preventive treatments**, since a medicine's effect can't be evaluated without some
quantitative way to measure it.

## Why a biomarker changes what's testable

`[28:38]`–`[30:41]` — Testing a hypothetical Dupuytren's medicine today would require measuring finger angle,
administering the drug, waiting **years** given the disease's slow progression, remeasuring, and then guessing
whether any change reflected the drug — research Eaton says is effectively unfundable given its cost and
interpretive uncertainty. He offers cholesterol as the model: a measurable biomarker lets clinicians test an
intervention (diet, exercise, medication) and remeasure cholesterol shortly after, using it as a **proxy** for
long-term heart attack/stroke risk rather than waiting for the actual event. A Dupuytren's biomarker would let
researchers do the same — measure, treat, remeasure the biomarker shortly after, as a fast proxy for whether
the treatment affected the underlying biology.

## The Data Bank's research plan and cost

`[30:41]`–`[33:47]` — The stated strategy: find biomarkers, use them to identify drug targets, then use them
to test whether candidate drugs actually work — with **finding the biomarkers as the essential first step**,
the core mission of the **International Dupuytren Data Bank.** Method: online self-report surveys stratify
enrollees by self-reported contracture severity and recurrence risk factors; once funding and enrollment
benchmarks are reached, blood specimens are compared across severe-, mild-, and no-disease groups to find
biomarker differences. A **systems approach** is planned, combining DNA data with a range of blood biomarkers.
Sample size requirements differ sharply by data type: DNA analysis, given Dupuytren's genetic complexity and
commonality, requires **at least 10,000 affected and a similar number of unaffected samples** — a large but
individually inexpensive assay; blood biomarker panels (e.g., TGF-beta or other growth factors) need **smaller
sample sizes but are individually more expensive** to run. The stated **entry-level budget for this research is
$5–7 million** — described as an extremely low figure by pharmaceutical industry standards, but still the
necessary starting point.

## A proposed future care model

`[33:47]`–`[36:50]` — Eaton sketches a future scenario: a patient noticing a nodule, having a family history,
or getting a suggestive signal from a consumer DNA service (he names Ancestry.com and 23andMe) would see a
**rheumatologist or medical doctor first**, who would run a **biomarker panel** to assess current disease
activity. Active biology would lead to biomarker-guided preventive treatment; inactive biology could still
prompt a DNA-based lifetime-risk assessment and longitudinal biomarker monitoring. **Only if and when a
physical deformity actually develops** would a surgeon be consulted — and even then, specifically for the
**simplest, safest option to correct the deformity quickly**, not to attempt curing the underlying biology
surgically. Eaton explicitly models this on how **rheumatoid arthritis, Raynaud's disease, and gout** are
currently managed, with surgery entering only for discrete anatomic problems, "like a SWAT mission."

## The case for awareness

`[36:50]`–`[39:53]` — Eaton names **lack of general awareness — public and medical — as one of the biggest
obstacles** to research funding and patient recruitment. He addresses, and rejects, the idea of renaming the
disease to something more recognizable (he mentions suggestions like "bent finger disease" or naming it after
a famous affected person): the real problem isn't the unusual name, but that **people simply aren't talking
about it**, contrasting this with conditions like Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and epilepsy that became familiar
specifically through sustained public advocacy campaigns despite equally unusual names. His practical
recommendations: talk about the disease at home, work, and with friends; be vocal if personally affected;
make clear to affected people that it's a **family condition** even without a known affected relative, since
their children and grandchildren could be affected, potentially more severely; use social media; bring
brochures to doctors; contact government representatives (he notes the CDC's website did not, at the time,
mention Dupuytren's by name, and that no Dupuytren-specific NIH grants currently exist). He states plainly:
**"the Dupuytren Research Group is the only group that's doing this kind of research."**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Loss of the ability to make a fist as a surgical complication**, offered as a direct counterexample to
  why a "straight finger" measurement alone doesn't capture a good outcome from the patient's perspective
  `[17:20]`.
- **Elective finger amputation following failed Dupuytren's surgery**, presented as the clearest possible
  index of current treatment's real limits `[11:11]`.
- **Pain, stiffness, and hand fatigue that are difficult to describe**, named directly from patient survey
  data as impacts extending well beyond visible finger bending `[10:11]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Diathesis** — Eaton's term for the roughly three-quarters-of-a-million-person US subgroup with severe,
  high-recurrence-rate disease `[09:09]`.
- **Trait, state, and rate biomarkers** — three distinct categories of disease measurement, with a rate
  biomarker (measuring current disease activity) identified as the missing piece needed for Dupuytren's
  `[27:36]`.
- **Systems biology** — a research approach treating disease as an interaction between multiple biological
  systems rather than a single causal factor, contrasted with older single-cause research models `[21:25]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"An egg with two yolks"** — biology and anatomy as two parallel, largely non-overlapping dimensions of the
  same disease `[12:13]`.
- **The quilt** — palm anatomy as three stitched layers anchoring skin to bone `[02:01]`.
- **The traffic jam** — why studying one cell or protein in isolation can't explain a systems-level disease
  `[23:28]`.
- **The car needing brakes, with only an odometer and no speedometer** — why current measurement (cumulative
  bend) can't show whether a treatment actually slows disease progression `[26:33]`.
- **Cholesterol as a proxy biomarker** — how a fast, measurable marker allows testing interventions without
  waiting years for the actual disease event `[29:39]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Eaton is candid throughout about the limits of current knowledge: the trigger mechanism is explicitly
"not really known," the cancer-risk association's cause is stated as unknown, and he uses his own 13-year
"lucky" patient case specifically to illustrate a failure of predictive ability rather than a treatment
success story ("this doesn't mean he had a great procedure... this doesn't mean he had a great surgeon"). He
is explicit that the proposed future care model and the $5–7 million research budget are current best
estimates and near-term goals, not settled facts, and consistently distinguishes established findings (US
prevalence figures, sex/age patterns) from open questions (why some carriers never develop disease, why cancer
risk is elevated).
