# Ilse Degreef MD PhD, "Cellulose Implant Improves Surgical Outcome After Segmental Fasciectomy" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Placing a thin, resorbable cellulose implant within a standard segmental fasciectomy incision — as a
physical barrier between the cut cord ends — measurably reduced the 12-week rebound that otherwise erodes
minimally invasive surgery's results, in a randomized trial of deliberately high-recurrence-risk patients.**
Degreef's own framing of the standard problem: **"the surgical goal we have is... to correct the finger
contracture and to maintain the correction as long as possible... as a surgeon we can cut out the strands,
[but] not the disease itself"** `[00:04]`.

## Why this mattered enough to pursue

`[01:05]`–`[02:06]` — Degreef's center performs about **100 Dupuytren's surgeries a year**, and averages
**one finger amputation per year** — **roughly 1 in 100** — almost all occurring during **revision surgery**
specifically, directly motivating a focus on reducing recurrence rather than accepting it as inevitable. To
gauge how common the disease actually is in her own region, she conducted a **street-survey prevalence
study across marketplaces in every province of Flanders** — using the same methodology independently
developed for the Bosnia study elsewhere in this library — examining people over 50 directly rather than
relying on hospital patient counts. Her result closely replicates that Bosnian data: **roughly 1 in 3 people
(1 in 3 males specifically) had nodules, and about 1 in 10 had contractures** — an independent confirmation,
from a different country, of very similar prevalence figures.

## The "fire break" concept

`[03:08]`–`[04:11]` — Degreef frames segmental fasciectomy as creating a **"fire break"** — a small
interruption in the strand to correct contracture, analogous to a firebreak preventing fire from spreading
between sections of forest. Her idea: **improve that fire break** by implanting a resorbable cellulose
material at the interruption site — an idea she admits her own PhD supervisor initially thought was a bad
one ("she said, 'now you're going to be in trouble'"). A second, independently motivated rationale came
from her own histology: **alpha-smooth-muscle-actin staining showed myofibroblasts oriented in a direction
that actively retracts the overlying skin**, sitting very close to the surface — suggesting a physical
barrier placed between skin and deeper scar tissue might also help **loosen** the skin's involvement in
recurrent contracture, not just block strand-to-strand recurrence. The cellulose material itself is
**borrowed, not invented** — already used in gynecological/fertility surgery (opening blocked fallopian
tubes) and spine surgery (preventing post-surgical scar adhesion), and previously applied to tendon release
surgery by another researcher.

## Trial design

`[04:11]`–`[06:14]` — **Randomized: 15 patients with implant, 15 without**, including **four
intra-individual controls** (one hand treated with the implant, the other hand without, in the same
patient). All patients were deliberately selected with **high Abe risk scores** — the same design logic
Degreef used in her tamoxifen trial, chosen specifically because expecting worse outcomes in this subgroup
makes a real treatment effect detectable with a much smaller sample than would otherwise be needed.
Technique: following Moermans's approach, a small (0.5–1cm) strand segment is excised, and a thin layer of
cellulose — which **liquefies within 48 hours and gradually disappears thereafter** — is placed at the site.
Outcomes tracked via a **validated digital photography system** (patients photographed in maximum
extension) plus satisfaction scores and DASH — though Degreef reiterates, consistent with her other work,
that **DASH doesn't correlate well with the geometric contracture problem** and stays below 10 (the normal
population baseline) regardless.

## Results

`[06:14]`–`[07:15]` — Using the **Tubiana index for relative, not absolute, correction**: the implant group
showed a **60% improvement in correction**, with a corresponding improvement in patient satisfaction. A
striking, if unquantifiable, qualitative finding: **"how soft the tissue was"** post-operatively in the
implant group — Degreef states directly she has no instrument to measure this softness objectively, but
found it clinically remarkable regardless. The most concrete finding: comparing full correction immediately
post-op against results at **12 weeks**, the **rebound (recurrence of contracture) was "almost unmeasurable"
in the implant group**, versus **about 10 degrees of rebound in the non-implant group** — notable given both
groups were deliberately high-risk patients expected to do poorly.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Rebound contracture appearing within 12 weeks of segmental fasciectomy**, quantified and directly
  compared between implant and non-implant groups `[07:15]`.
- **Dimpled skin overlying a contracture**, described as a sign surgeons associate with a more stubborn,
  skin-involved case — connected here to direct histological evidence of myofibroblasts retracting the skin
  itself `[03:08]`.
- **Post-operative tissue softness**, described as a real but currently unmeasurable clinical observation
  `[07:15]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **The "fire break" concept** — Degreef's framing for what a segmental fasciectomy interruption is meant
  to accomplish, and what the cellulose implant is meant to reinforce `[03:08]`.
- **Tubiana index (as a relative correction measure)** — used here specifically to track relative rather
  than absolute correction, a distinction Degreef treats as methodologically important `[06:14]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The fire break itself** — a small interruption meant to stop disease from "catching" across the strand,
  reinforced by a physical barrier material `[03:08]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Degreef repeatedly flags the limits of her own measurement tools rather than overstating findings — she
states directly that she has **no way to objectively measure the tissue softness** she found remarkable, and
frames the trial's deliberate selection of high-risk patients as a methodological choice made explicitly
easier to detect a real effect, not as representative of the general Dupuytren's population. She also raises
an honest, unresolved tension for future work: if the cellulose implant were impregnated with a
myofibroblast-inhibiting active substance (her stated next research direction), **it might also impair wound
healing** — a tradeoff she flags without yet having an answer to.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly corroborates `-OKFB6sX_64`** (Degreef's own tamoxifen trial talk) — both use the same high-Abe-
risk-score patient selection strategy to make a small trial statistically informative, and both report
results with the same careful, self-critical hedging characteristic of her work in this library.
**Independently replicates the prevalence methodology and figures in `W_Tbwi613Qw`** (Finsen's Bosnia
epidemiology talk) — a genuinely valuable cross-national confirmation of similar prevalence rates using
similar street-survey methods. **Relevant to `klWbjOcG3Kk`** (Ketchum's dermofasciectomy talk) — both pursue
the same underlying goal (reducing recurrence after minimally invasive or limited surgery) through
mechanically distinct means: removing more tissue (Ketchum) versus placing a barrier at less tissue removed
(Degreef) — worth reading as complementary strategies rather than competing claims.
