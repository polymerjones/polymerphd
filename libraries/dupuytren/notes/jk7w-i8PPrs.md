# Dupuytren Severity (Dr. Charles Eaton, 2017 ASSH Annual Meeting)

## Central claim

**"Severity" in Dupuytren's isn't one risk — it's six separable milestones, each governed by a different mix
of biological and anatomic factors, and conflating them produces bad predictions.** Eaton's own framing:
**"the factors that put people at risk for developing Dupuytren's disease in the first place, or progressing
to the need [for treatment], are primarily biological. The risk factors for getting a straight finger from a
procedure, or losing some of the initial correction, appear to be primarily anatomic and structural. And the
risk of... recurrence... [is] a combination of both"** `[10:10]`.

## Milestone 1: developing any sign of the disease

`[01:01]`–`[02:01]` — **Genetics dominates**: a parent or sibling with Dupuytren's gives a **25–33% lifetime
risk.** Race gradient in the US: **Caucasian Americans most affected, then Hispanic, then African American,
then Asian Americans, in decreasing order.** Having a related "spectrum" condition (Ledderhose, frozen
shoulder) raises lifetime risk further. Age/gender data, precisely quantified: **by age 70, 1 in 4 Caucasian
men and 1 in 10 women** show some sign of the disease. The *absolute* number gap is striking and shifts with
age: **at 40, men outnumber women with the disease 30:1; by 70, that narrows to 2:1; by 85+, women actually
outnumber men in absolute count** — explained not by a reversal of risk but by **women living longer**,
so more of them survive into the age brackets where the disease has had time to appear.

## Non-genetic risk factors, including two that lower risk

`[03:03]` — Weaker than genetics, and mainly visible in patients **without** a family history: mechanical
factors (**acute trauma within the past year, chronic heavy manual labor**) and biological factors
(**diabetes, psoriasis, being underweight, hyperlipidemia, chronic tobacco or alcohol use**) all **increase**
risk. Two factors, less expected, **decrease** lifetime risk relative to the general population: **rheumatoid
arthritis and being overweight.**

## Milestone 2: progressing from a nodule to treatment-requiring contracture

`[03:03]`–`[05:05]` — **80–90% of everyone with Dupuytren's never develops contracture at all.** Within 10
years of a first nodule: **1 in 10 spontaneously regress, 2 in 10 progress to contracture bad enough to need
treatment, and the remainder are stable or don't progress enough to need treatment** — precisely matching
the natural-history figures given elsewhere in this library. High-risk groups for progression: **family
history, dose-dependently** — patients with Dupuytren's in *both* family lines have their first procedure
**over 10 years younger** than those with no family history, with one-family-line patients falling in
between. **Prior contracture on one hand carries a 40% risk of the other hand developing disease within five
years** of the first treatment. External factors (tobacco, alcohol, vibration exposure) do increase
progression risk, but with a **much smaller effect than family history or prior contracture.**

## Pseudo-Dupuytren: a clinical mimic

`[05:05]`–`[06:05]` — A distinct entity — **traumatic palmar fasciitis**, clinically indistinguishable from
early Dupuytren's — appears specifically in **diabetes or within a year of hand trauma**, and typically in
**"the wrong demographic"**: young women with no family history. It carries a **relatively low risk of true
contracture**, and Eaton states plainly it's currently unclear whether it represents genuine Dupuytren's
biology or a separate look-alike process — **"we'll know when we get a biomarker."**

## Milestone 3: achieving full correction

`[06:05]`–`[07:06]` — Correction likelihood **falls as baseline contracture increases**, with specific
breakpoints: **composite contracture over 90°, MCP over 50°, PIP over 40°.** Citing collagenase (Xiaflex)
outcome data as an index: **MCP joints below the 50-degree threshold reach full straightening in about 80% of
cases, versus 40% above it; for the PIP joint, those figures are 50% versus 25%.** PIP joints are generally
harder to fully correct than MCP; repeat fasciectomy is less likely to achieve full correction. Critically,
**biologic factors (including diathesis) don't meaningfully influence correction likelihood** — this
milestone is governed by anatomy and mechanics, not disease biology.

## Milestone 4: losing some correction in the first few months

`[07:06]`–`[08:08]` — Risk is higher for **PIP joints generally, and specifically for PIP contracture over
60 degrees** (linked to extensor mechanism incompetence — consistent with the anatomical mechanism described
in `iD3mYMmfN-Q`), **redo procedures, open (surgical) PIP procedures particularly if pinned, small finger
involvement, and multiple rays involved.** Again, **biologic factors — diathesis, age, external factors, and,
"sadly," splinting — don't affect this risk**, a blunt claim stated without elaboration ("that's a whole
other talk").

## Milestone 5: recurrence after a year or more of stability

`[08:08]`–`[09:09]` — The main predictor here is **diathesis, combined with anatomic factors** (small finger,
PIP procedure). **Fasciectomy lasts "over twice as long" before recontracture compared to minimally invasive
procedures (collagenase or needle fasciotomy)** — restated verbatim from Eaton's other public materials.
Operative predictors of earlier recurrence: **less-than-full initial correction, and proliferative
histology** — though Eaton notes these aren't things you'd choose a procedure based on, just things that
predict outcome after the fact. Lifestyle and comorbidity factors — diabetes, frozen shoulder, Peyronie's,
manual labor — **don't show a clear influence on recurrence rate** specifically (as distinct from their role
in initial disease risk).

## Milestone 6: long-term biological inactivity

`[09:09]`–`[10:10]` — Two distinct mechanisms proposed for why some contractures simply stop progressing:
**mild, self-limited biology** that was provoked once and then stopped being provoked; and, more
counterintuitively, **aggressive biology that becomes mechanically stress-shielded** — a **fixed PIP
capsuloligamentous contracture, or disuse from severe contracture**, can itself prevent the mechanical
tension on the cord that would otherwise keep driving the disease process, effectively switching it off by
removing its mechanical trigger.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **The specific contracture thresholds (composite >90°, MCP >50°, PIP >40°) marking a sharp drop in
  correction likelihood** `[06:05]`.
- **A young female patient with no family history developing apparent early Dupuytren's after hand trauma or
  in the context of diabetes** — flagged as possibly pseudo-Dupuytren rather than the genuine disease
  `[05:05]`.
- **PIP contracture over 60 degrees as a marker of likely extensor mechanism incompetence**, predicting early
  loss of correction specifically `[07:06]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Pseudo-Dupuytren (traumatic palmar fasciitis)** — a clinical mimic of early Dupuytren's occurring after
  trauma or with diabetes, in a demographic atypical for true disease `[05:05]`.
- **Stress shielding** — a fixed joint contracture or disuse removing the mechanical tension that would
  otherwise sustain active disease progression `[09:09]`.
- **Dose-dependent family history** — Eaton's term for the finding that Dupuytren's in both family lines
  predicts a substantially earlier first procedure than in one line or none `[04:03]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Consistently precise about which claims are quantified versus qualitative, and explicit about what remains
unresolved — pseudo-Dupuytren's true nature is stated as unknown pending a biomarker, and the "sadly,
splinting doesn't affect" early-correction-loss claim is flagged as deserving its own separate talk rather
than fully justified here. Figures are drawn from named contexts (the Xiaflex/collagenase trial experience
specifically, for the correction-likelihood breakpoints) rather than presented as universal constants.
