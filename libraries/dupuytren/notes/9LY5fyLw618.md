# Dr. Larry Hurst "Collagenase Treatment" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Collagenase injection reached patients only after a 25-year, multiply-stalled development effort, and
even its developer is explicit that it does not cure the disease.** Hurst closes with the same caveat he
says he gives every patient: **"recurrence happens. I tell all patients you can't cure this, whether you do
surgery, needle, or collagenase — it's a question of what you can do and how you use it"** `[29:35]`.

## From burn debridement to a Dupuytren's idea

`[00:00]`–`[04:03]` — Collagenase itself long predates its Dupuytren's use: identified through gas-gangrene
research in 1937, isolated and characterized in 1953 by Ines Mandl (described as the first woman to earn a
PhD from the Polytechnic Institute of Brooklyn), then licensed in 1957 for burn debridement — the existing
FDA approval for human burn use is what let Hurst's later work skip the standard three-species toxicology
program, which he notes **"cost three million dollars"** at the time. Hurst credits his department chair,
Roger Daly, with pushing him into Dupuytren's research at all, over his own initial objection that
McFarland's myofibroblast work had already settled the field. The specific idea to try collagenase on
Dupuytren's cords, rather than nerve-repair fibrosis (its originally proposed use), was Hurst's own
practical judgment: **"if you put a little too much, the nerve repair's gonna fall apart... but we have
this other disorder... where basically getting rid of the scar is the goal, so maybe we should try it
there first."**

## The regulatory path

`[04:03]`–`[16:20]` — Told largely through specific obstacles and how each was cleared:

- **In vitro proof-of-concept (1996)**: cords excised at fasciotomy, incubated with collagenase overnight,
  then mechanically pulled apart — **less force was needed to separate them than the force of a normal
  finger's own extension**, which Hurst treated as the signal to proceed `[06:07]`–`[07:10]`.
- **Rat-tail toxicology study**: required before human use, to confirm collagenase near tendon didn't cause
  neurovascular damage or tail necrosis `[07:10]`.
- **First human dose (~500–600 units, roughly 1/20th the eventual dose)**: produced no effect in any
  patient. A dose-escalation protocol followed; the first patient on a higher dose had his cord audibly pop
  during a post-treatment handshake `[07:10]`–`[08:11]`.
- **Dose-finding repeated at the FDA's request** (2,500 / 5,000 / 10,000 units) confirmed **10,000 units
  (~0.58 mg) as the most broadly effective dose** `[09:14]`.
- **Manufacturing hold**: some trial vials showed a "milky glass" appearance instead of the expected cake;
  the FDA halted the study until the cause (moisture ingress during manufacturing) was identified, a
  nine-month delay `[13:16]`–`[14:19]`.
- **CORD study**: the pivotal trial, run across 16 US sites and 5 Australian sites, reported **64% of
  treated MCP joints reaching full extension**; PIP joints "did okay, but not as well." Published in the
  *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2009 `[13:16]`–`[15:19]`.
- **FDA advisory panel**: voted 12–0 to recommend approval; full FDA approval followed in **February 2010**,
  one of only six new compounds approved that year `[15:19]`–`[16:20]`.
- **Post-approval reimbursement gap**: despite FDA approval, insurers initially treated it as experimental
  and would not pay — Hurst could not actually treat patients again until October 2010, by which point 852
  were waiting at his own institution `[17:21]`.

## Hurst's own practice data since approval

`[19:22]`–`[23:26]` — Since February 2010: **955 Dupuytren's patients seen**; of these, **467 had a nodule
only and were simply observed**, **443 had cords treated with collagenase**, and **45 had surgery**. Across
research studies and standard practice combined, Hurst had personally given **611 injections to 443
patients** by the time of the talk. Joint involvement in his collagenase-treated group: **MCP (MP) 81%, PIP
66%**, with central cords contracting both joints roughly half the time. He states directly that **31% of
his collagenase patients had prior surgery** — i.e., he did not restrict the treatment to surgery-naïve
presentations.

## Technique and off-label choices, stated explicitly as such

`[19:22]`–`[27:32]` — Hurst describes injecting at the point where the cord bowstrings, using local
anesthesia for essentially all manipulations, performed the following day in-office. He explicitly flags
two off-label uses in his own practice at the time of this talk: **treating DIP joints**, and **splitting a
single dose across two cords when each is thin enough** — noting the legal distinction directly: **"as a
clinician, I can decide legally in the United States to use it off-label, but if I were talking for the
company they'd kill me if I told you that... I'm just telling you my personal experience."** (Full
two-dose treatment in a single joint, by contrast, he notes had since become FDA-approved, not off-label.)
For recurrent disease with damaged skin, he recommends **fasciotomy combined with split-thickness skin
grafting** rather than repeat collagenase, reasoning that collagenase-induced skin tears in already-damaged
skin heal in a way that re-contracts the finger. He also reports treating patients on **Coumadin
(anticoagulation) without stopping it**, describing one case with alarming initial bruising that resolved
normally within three weeks.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Cord palpable well enough to bowstring on finger extension, used as the injection landmark** `[19:22]`.
- **Skin tearing during the manipulation/extension step, minimized by gentle technique** `[29:35]`.
- **Recurrence after any treatment modality — surgery, needle aponeurotomy, or collagenase** `[29:35]`.
- **Residual PIP contracture from joint stiffness even after successful cord rupture** `[25:28]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Orphan drug status** — regulatory designation Hurst's team obtained, providing seven years of use
  exclusivity and access to FDA research grants `[06:07]`.
- **CORD study** — the multi-site pivotal trial (16 US, 5 Australian sites) underlying FDA approval
  `[13:16]`.
- **Bowstring** — the cord standing proud of the skin on finger extension, used to guide injection
  placement `[19:22]`.
- **Digit widget** — an external device Hurst references using for severe joint contracture, without
  detailing its mechanism here `[22:26]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk is a first-person historical and clinical account rather than built around
illustrative comparisons.

## Source-stated confidence

Hurst is candid about limitations throughout: the 64%/PIP-underperforming split from the CORD study is
stated plainly rather than smoothed over; he flags his off-label uses explicitly as off-label, not as
tacitly approved; and he closes by stating recurrence as a certainty regardless of treatment choice, not a
risk specific to collagenase. This is a first-person account of his own experience and trial involvement,
not a systematic review — the practice-data figures (955 patients, etc.) come from one surgeon's records.

## Conflicts with other sources

treatment development as chronically underfunded and slow-moving — Hurst's 25-year account is a concrete
