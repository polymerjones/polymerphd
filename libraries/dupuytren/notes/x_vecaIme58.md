# Dr. Heinrich Seegenschmiedt "Radiotherapy for Dupuytren & Ledderhose" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Radiotherapy, given early enough and dosed correctly, measurably reduces the need for later surgery — but
only in a specific disease window, and Seegenschmiedt is explicit it is not a competitor to surgery, only a
delaying treatment for progressive early disease.** His 30-year, over-1,000-patient dataset shows a clear
result: **"the control group had to a much larger amount required hand surgery, versus the 8% in Group A
and 14% in Group B"** — a statistically significant difference at five years `[13:17]`–`[14:19]`.

## Why radiation works: a specific cellular mechanism

`[03:03]`–`[05:07]` — Radiotherapy is described as acting on **both the inflammatory and proliferative
components** of active disease: reducing lymphocyte adhesion to endothelial cells (inflammation), and — the
mechanism given the most attention — **forcing proliferating myofibroblasts directly into a post-mitotic,
non-dividing state.** Nodules and cords show markedly higher cellularity than tendon or scar tissue, and
that cell population is specifically radiosensitive because its proliferation is **TGF-beta driven**, and
**"TGF-beta is a very sensitive factor to radiotherapy."** Cited lab evidence: irradiated (5 Gray)
TGF-beta-cofibroblasts showed **"a completely down-regulating of the surviving fractions"** compared to
non-irradiated samples, regardless of application method. The practical effect: **"by interfering with
radiation, we basically do a shortcut from [myofibroblast stage] MF1 directly to the post-mitotic
fibroblast"** — at whatever proliferative stage the cells are caught.

## The treatment window: why staging matters more than anything else

`[06:07]`–`[10:14]` — Seegenschmiedt frames candidacy for radiotherapy entirely around **disease stage and
progression, not diagnosis alone**. He proposes tracking specific progression signals over time: **increasing
number of nodules, increasing number of cords, new symptoms (itching, sensitivity changes), loss of
extension, and loss of practical hand function** — illustrated with patients who have strong functional
stakes in preserving exact motion (orchestra musicians, magicians). His staged framework: **radiotherapy is
appropriate for minor extension deficits up to roughly 10 degrees with proven progressive nodules/cords, and
still has a role up to about 20–30 degrees**, but beyond that, **"this is the length of the surgeon"** —
radiotherapy is not indicated for established, more advanced contracture. Reviewing published data by stage,
he reports **25–50% progression despite radiotherapy in more advanced staged disease, and 50–75% in even
later stages** — concluding directly: **"it doesn't make sense to treat these cases."** The explicit
message: **"radiotherapy and surgery are complementary and not competitive."**

## The trial: design and five-year results

`[10:14]`–`[14:19]` — Two research groups are credited: Erlangen (single-schedule experience, no controlled
trial) and Essen (Seegenschmiedt's own group), which ran a controlled comparison — patients who were
counseled and **declined radiotherapy** served as an observational control group followed over the same
period, while treated patients were randomized between two dose schedules: **7 fractions of 3 Gray versus
10 fractions of 3 Gray.** Total cohort reported: **612 patients (511 receiving radiotherapy, 101 controls),
880 hands, followed to a final evaluation in November 2014** — Seegenschmiedt notes this is a longer
follow-up than he could find in most surgical literature. Baseline comparability: **no significant
difference between groups in family history, Ledderhose disease, Peyronie's disease, frozen shoulder, or
keloid scarring** — with one exception (a modestly larger share of more advanced-stage patients in one
group). Side effects: **acute effects in about 25% of patients, fading over time; persistent dry skin in
about 15%, manageable with creams; no cancers observed in the cohort.** Outcomes: **139 treated sites showed
nodule remission**, most others were stable, and **about 30% showed progression despite treatment** — but
the surgery-avoidance difference against the untreated control group was large and statistically
significant. **The recommended dosing concept, stated directly: 10 fractions of 3 Gray, given in two series
about 12 weeks apart** — noted as producing better results than the alternative, lower-dose schedules some
practitioners use.

## An ongoing extension: radiotherapy alongside surgery itself

`[15:20]`–`[16:22]` — A newer collaborative study, in progress at the time of the talk, is testing
radiotherapy **combined with surgery** in severe cases (specifically noted: pinky-finger contracture of 90
degrees or more) to help surgeons avoid early relapse — described as still years from results, but already
supported by hand surgeons interested in an added tool for long-term control.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Increasing nodule count, cord count, itching, and sensitivity changes as tracked progression signals**
  that inform the decision to treat `[08:09]`.
- **Loss of fine hand function in high-stakes users (musicians, performers)** cited as a reason to intervene
  earlier rather than wait for surgical-threshold contracture `[08:09]`.
- **Dry skin as the most common persistent side effect of radiotherapy, at about 15%**, manageable with
  topical creams `[13:17]`.
- **Whitish skin areas, altered skin folding, and V- or U-shaped nodule patterns** described as visible
  clinical signs a patient photograph alone can reveal, sufficient to suggest radiotherapy candidacy
  `[11:14]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Post-mitotic shortcut** — the proposed mechanism by which radiation forces proliferating myofibroblasts
  directly out of the cell cycle, regardless of what proliferative stage they're caught at `[04:06]`.
- **MF1/MF2/MF3** — Seegenschmiedt's staged terms for progressively differentiated myofibroblast states
  along the proliferation pathway `[04:06]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Seegenschmiedt is direct about the treatment's limits: he states plainly that treating more advanced-stage
disease **"doesn't make sense"** given the reported progression rates in that population, and frames the
20–30 degree upper boundary for radiotherapy candidacy as a genuine, not-yet-fully-settled line ("this
length is still not fully [decided]"). He explicitly declines to recommend radiotherapy for a specific
patient shown mid-talk, stating the disease had already progressed past the point where radiation would
help. The combined surgery-plus-radiotherapy study is flagged as ongoing and years from results, not cited
as established practice.
