# Dr. Steven Hovius: Percutaneous Aponeurotomy and Lipofilling — 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a randomized trial against limited fasciectomy, percutaneous aponeurotomy and lipofilling (PALF)
produced a much faster early recovery — full fist within fifteen minutes for one patient shown on film —
with no significant difference in one-year joint correction at the MP joint, though the PIP joint corrected
somewhat less well with PALF.** Hovius states the technique's core move directly: **"then use a hooked needle
to release the skin from the subcutaneous tissue, then taking a lipo aspirate and putting a lipofilling —
because fat, as well as [being] a filler, also has wound healing capacities, as we've proven many times now"**
`[01:01]`.

## The technique

`[00:00]`–`[02:04]` — Multiple needle nicks are made along the cord, proximal to distal, **softening the cord
without letting the needle's bevel pass under the skin** — Hovius notes needles get blunt with use and a
single ray can require up to 100 nicks. A hooked needle then releases the skin from the underlying
subcutaneous tissue, after which a **lipoaspirate is injected as lipofilling**, using a different needle track
each time (up to ten passes), accepting some fat loss along the way. A one-year post-procedure film is
described as showing **hardly any visible scarring**, and a five-days-post-op patient is shown able to make a
full fist within fifteen minutes of the procedure.

## Trial design

`[02:04]`–`[04:07]` — Primary patients with **MP contracture >20 degrees and/or PIP contracture >30 degrees**
were randomized by envelope after being shown an explanatory film, to either PALF or limited fasciectomy.
Measurement used a **blinded Semmes-Weinstein test** for sensibility and blinded observers distinct from the
treating team. A methodological point Hovius stresses: **only the single most-affected finger per patient was
used for analysis**, rejecting the common practice in prior literature of analyzing multiple fingers per hand,
which he calls **"not correct from a statistical point of view."** Power analysis called for two groups of 40
hands; the trial reached **44 hands (39 patients) in the PALF arm and 32 hands (30 patients) in the LF arm**,
with drop-out on the surgery side attributed to patient reluctance, cancer diagnosis, or long waiting lists.
Eight patients with bilateral disease received one treatment per hand, serving as **their own within-patient
controls**.

## Results by joint

`[04:07]`–`[06:11]` — Follow-up reached 42 of 44 PALF hands and 30 of 32 LF hands at one year.

- **MP joint**: consistently better corrected than the PIP joint in both arms; **no significant difference
  between PALF and LF at one year.**
- **PIP joint**: a trend toward less correction with PALF than LF, though **not statistically significant**
  in this trial.
- **Total passive extension deficit**: same pattern — a non-significant trend favoring LF.
- **Early fist-making**: a clear, visible difference favoring PALF at two weeks post-treatment; **by six
  months the two groups converge and remain equivalent through one year.**

## Complications and patient-reported outcomes

`[06:11]`–`[07:13]` — PALF: **two transient CRPS cases**, both fully resolved by one year. LF: **one
non-transient CRPS case and one nerve injury** — complications Hovius flags as more durable than PALF's.
**Median return to activities of daily living: 9 days (PALF) versus 19 days (LF)**, a significant difference.
Patient satisfaction — "would you choose the same operation again," "would you recommend it to family and
friends" — showed **no difference between the two groups.**

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Full fist achieved within fifteen minutes of PALF**, shown on film as the marker of early functional
  recovery `[02:04]`.
- **Transient versus non-transient complex regional pain syndrome**, distinguished explicitly by which arm of
  the trial the patient was in `[06:11]`.
- **Median days to resume activities of daily living**, quantified and compared directly between treatments
  `[06:11]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **PALF** — percutaneous aponeurotomy and lipofilling, the combined needle-release-plus-fat-grafting
  technique presented here `[01:01]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

None stated — the talk presents technique and trial data directly.

## Source-stated confidence

Hovius discloses no conflicts for this study. He is explicit that **"there's no clear definition on
recurrence — everybody's saying something different in literature,"** which is why the talk deliberately
reports contracture correction and complication figures rather than claiming a recurrence rate. He also
flags a specific methodological limitation candidly: the PROM measure used **"is not specific enough,"**
noting the group has since moved to the Michigan Hand Questionnaire for later work, even though it showed no
significant difference at the time point reported here.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly relevant to `fmWiC5V76YI`** (Khouri's EPA talk) — both combine a minimally invasive release with
autologous fat grafting, sharing the premise that fat contributes wound-healing benefit beyond simple
volume-filling; Khouri's EPA technique adds a pre-expansion device this trial's PALF technique does not use.
**Directly comparable to `oRdE0UKeJF0`** (Van Rijssen's randomized PNF-vs-fasciectomy trial) — both are
randomized trials of a minimally invasive needle-based technique against limited fasciectomy; this trial's
finding of no significant MP-joint difference and a non-significant PIP trend toward better surgical
correction sits alongside Van Rijssen's finding of significantly higher PNF recurrence, though the two trials
measure different endpoints (contracture correction here versus recurrence there) and PALF is not identical to
plain PNF. **Consistent with `93IWNYYqCjs`** (Warwick's "what we may never know" talk) — Hovius's own
admission that no agreed recurrence definition exists is a live example of exactly the outcome-measure problem
Warwick's talk argues makes trial comparison across techniques so difficult.
