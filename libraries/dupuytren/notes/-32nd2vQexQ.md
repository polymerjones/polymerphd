# Bing Siang Gan MD PhD, "Needle aponeurotomy for MCP contractures" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Gan had publicly and repeatedly stated he would never perform needle aponeurotomy, calling it unsafe — then
adopted the technique after his mentor Bob McFarlane, a lifelong opponent of the procedure, died in 2006, and
found in his own prospective series that MCP contracture improved from a mean 46 degrees to 14 degrees,
maintained at six weeks, with results consistent with prior published literature.** His closing, self-directed
lesson: **"my real lesson for this test was actually that never is not very long, and I think that applies to
Dupuytren's contracture"** `[08:15]`.

## The reversal

`[01:05]`–`[03:08]` — Gan trained under **Bob McFarlane**, founder of London, Ontario's Hand and Upper Limb
Centre and, separately from his medical career, a celebrated Canadian university football athlete (four Yates
Cup championships, the 1951 Lou Marsh Trophy for Canada's top athlete — a trophy later won by figures
including Bobby Orr and Wayne Gretzky). McFarlane was **adamantly opposed to needle aponeurotomy**, published
against it before the technique's more recent popularization (by Lermusiaux) in the early 1990s, and argued
instead for fasciectomy and radical fasciectomy, becoming well known for detailed anatomical descriptions of
the disease. Gan states plainly: **"one of the other legacies of Dr. McFarlane was that he was adamantly
against needle aponeurotomy... he died in 2006, and shortly after that I started doing this needle
aponeurotomy — the master was gone, I was allowed to do it"** `[02:06]`.

A 2002 newspaper article is shown directly on screen: a patient publicly complained he'd had to travel to
Europe for a needle aponeurotomy procedure unavailable in Canada, and had raised the issue with federal
health officials. Gan is quoted twice in that article stating **"I would never do that procedure because I
think it's an unsafe procedure"** — a position he shows himself now having fully reversed `[07:14]`.

## Technique

`[03:08]`–`[04:09]` — Gan's variant: minimal local anesthesia (roughly 0.3–0.5cc, just enough to anesthetize
the skin), then the needle is moved **radial to ulnar to slice the cord rather than repeatedly puncture it**.
He describes it as a safe procedure in the palm given good direct contact with a palpable cord, typically
requiring two to four needle-stick sites, taking roughly three minutes per procedure.

## Study design and results

`[04:09]`–`[06:14]` — Prospective series of patients enrolled from **September 2009**, inclusion focused on
**MCP contractures**, excluding recurrences; most patients presented with a **palpable pretendinous cord**.
MCP, PIP, and DIP contracture were diagrammed preoperatively and tracked as total digital extension deficit,
followed at 6–8 weeks. **62 fingers**, predominantly ring, small, and long fingers, matching the
demographic pattern reported elsewhere in the literature. Results: **total extension deficit fell from 46
degrees preoperatively to 14 degrees**, maintained at six weeks — **most correction occurred at the MCP
joint**, with meaningfully less improvement at the PIP joint. **No neurovascular injuries** occurred, but
**one flexor tendon laceration** was identified when a patient stopped flexing his fingers three days
post-procedure and did not return until the six-week follow-up; the laceration was at the zone I/zone II
border and was successfully repaired primarily.

## Why MCP specifically

`[06:14]` — Gan explains his joint preference directly: at the MCP joint, the relevant cord is usually a
**pretendinous cord relatively far from the neurovascular bundle**, whereas closer to the neurovascular
bundle he remains cautious — a stated rationale for restricting the technique's use rather than a limitation
of the needle method itself.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Loss of active finger flexion appearing three days post-procedure**, the presenting sign that led to
  discovering the flexor tendon laceration `[06:14]`.
- **A palpable cord near the web space with 45–50 degrees of contracture**, shown as a specific
  preoperative/postoperative case example `[04:09]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Gan discloses no financial relationships and is unusually candid about the limitations of his own study: a
**small series, very short-term follow-up (six to eight weeks only)**, and he frames his conclusion modestly
— the technique "may be beneficial for a select group of patients," possibly useful as an adjunct before open
fasciectomy in more severe cases, rather than a broad endorsement. He reports his one serious complication
(flexor tendon laceration) openly rather than omitting it, and explicitly walks through his own prior public
opposition to the technique before explaining his change of position.
