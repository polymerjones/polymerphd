# Dr. Roel Ophoff "Genetics in Dupuytren Disease" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Dupuytren's is genuinely one of genetics' rare success stories for a complex trait — a small sample
identified an unusually large number of disease loci — but the variants found so far explain only a small
fraction of the disease's overall heritability, and the rest remains genuinely unaccounted for.** Ophoff
states this gap directly: **"if I look at all the SNP variants... it explains roughly 14% of the
heritability... but we know [total] heritability is roughly 80%"** `[12:14]`.

## How heritable Dupuytren's actually is

`[06:09]`–`[07:09]` — A **Danish twin study**, population-based (which Ophoff flags as important
specifically because it avoids selection bias), found **heritability of roughly 80%**, with a **population
incidence of 0.6%.** A separate study found disease severity correlating with family history. Together with
a 2005 Swedish family linkage study (a single locus with sufficient evidence in one large pedigree, though
the causal mutation itself was never identified), Ophoff treats these as firmly establishing that genetic
factors are involved — **"Dupuytren's in general can be considered a heritable trait — there's no doubt
[about that]"** `[16:18]`.

## Why the GWAS result is a genuine outlier

`[07:09]` — The genome-wide association study Ophoff references identified **nine distinct genomic loci**
from **fewer than 1,000 cases** — a sample size he calls **"unheard of for any other complex trait,"**
contrasting it directly with schizophrenia, where a comparable finding would have required roughly **20,000
cases.** He attributes this efficiency to Dupuytren's genetics being unusually tractable relative to most
complex diseases, not to any special feature of the study design.

## The common-versus-rare-variant framework, and the missing heritability

`[08:10]`–`[12:14]` — Ophoff frames genetic variants along two axes: **allele frequency** (common versus
rare) and **effect size** (small versus large). Using a 2012 study sequencing ~200 drug-related genes across
14,000 people as an illustration, he notes **common variants are visibly few relative to the much larger
number of rare, often family-unique variants — "an iceberg [where] only the top... we can see... are common
ones, and the rare ones are underwater."** Most genetic studies, including the Dupuytren's GWAS, have only
been able to detect **common variants of modest effect**, because rare-variant, large-effect cases require
multi-generation pedigrees that are hard to find and hard to prove causal — what he calls **"the unlucky
corner"** for a researcher. The quantified gap: the **statistically significant GWAS loci alone explain only
about 1% of heritability**; **all common SNP variation together explains roughly 14–16%**; against an
**overall heritability of ~80%**, this leaves the large majority of Dupuytren's heritability **unexplained
by anything found so far** — implying rare variants likely carry real, currently invisible weight.

## The Dutch pedigree study: linkage finds nothing, sequencing finds a little

`[13:16]`–`[16:18]` — Ophoff's own group's rare-variant search, using **six Dutch pedigrees, 32 affected
individuals**: a **linkage study found no evidence of a major shared locus across or within any of the
families** — "no major gene explaining Dupuytren's" in this dataset, despite disease clustering within
families. A follow-up **exome sequencing** approach (sequencing all coding regions in 2–3 affected
individuals per family, searching specifically for previously unreported, predicted-deleterious variants)
found **four candidate rare variants across two families** — one family alone contained three different
variants, in different genome locations, all co-segregating with disease in that pedigree (making it
impossible to distinguish which, if any, is truly causal). **One of the four overlapped with a gene already
implicated by the GWAS** — flagged as worth pursuing further, not yet confirmed.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None directly — this is a genetics-methodology talk, not a discussion of clinical presentation.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **SNP (single nucleotide polymorphism)** — a common single-base genetic variant, explained here as
  occurring roughly once per 1,000 nucleotides but numbering in the millions across a genome `[08:10]`.
- **Missing heritability** — Ophoff's own framing (not necessarily his coined term, but his explicit focus)
  for the large gap between total estimated heritability (~80%) and the much smaller share (~14–16%)
  explained by identified common variants `[12:14]`.
- **Exome sequencing** — sequencing all protein-coding regions of the genome at once, used here to search
  for rare causal variants within affected families `[14:16]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **The iceberg** — common genetic variants as the small visible tip, with a much larger population of rare,
  family-unique variants hidden "underwater" `[09:10]`.
- **"The unlucky corner"** — a researcher's position when studying a rare variant of small effect: hard to
  find, and hard to prove causal even once found `[11:13]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Unusually rigorous and self-limiting throughout — Ophoff repeatedly quantifies exactly how much of
heritability current findings explain (1% from significant loci, ~14–16% from all common variants) rather
than letting the GWAS "success story" imply more than it shows. The rare-variant pedigree findings are
explicitly labeled as **not yet distinguishable from one another causally** ("we cannot distinguish one from
another") and the shared-gene overlap with the GWAS is called something "we need to pursue further," not a
confirmed link. He also flags a general risk in pedigree-based rare-variant research from his own
schizophrenia genetics experience: families initially thought to fit a clean Mendelian inheritance pattern
"may not be Mendelian after all."

## Conflicts with other sources

**The "9-locus GWAS" referenced here is very likely the same study shown mid-analysis in `-3a_yiOu_3I`**
(Dolmans's 2010 talk) — that note shows the first two loci (chromosome 7 and 22) emerging from the same
Dutch multicenter cohort this talk cites five years later as an established finding. **Directly updates and
refines `g40N2M2C6qM`** (Bayat's 2010 genetics talk) — both cover Dupuytren's
genetics broadly, but this 2015 talk gives cleaner, more current figures (80% heritability from a
population-based Danish twin study, a specific 9-locus GWAS, and a quantified 14–16% common-variant
explained-heritability figure) than the more transcript-degraded 2010 talk was able to convey. Both agree
genetic association findings so far have not converged on a single confirmed causal pathway, though this
talk specifically flags the **Wnt signaling pathway** as one promising GWAS-implicated candidate worth
further study — a lead not clearly recoverable from the 2010 talk's degraded transcript. **Consistent with
`AbY1KVhTVAo`** (Eaton's IDDB talk) — Eaton's proposed genetic/systemic versus exposure/benign disease
subsets remain, per this talk, still unconfirmed at the single-gene level, consistent with Eaton's own
framing of that split as hypothesis rather than established fact.
