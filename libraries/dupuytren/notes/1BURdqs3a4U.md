# Dr. Gary Pess "Percutaneous Needle Fasciotomy and Collagenase (Xiaflex)" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**There is no cure for Dupuytren's contracture, so Pess's stated priority is not a perfect finger but fast,
low-risk functional improvement — which argues for treating early, aggressively, and with minimally invasive
technique rather than waiting.** His opening philosophy: **"achieving perfection is not necessary, though
it's always our goal. Patients appreciate rapid recovery [and] improvement of function... even if the finger
is not perfectly straight"** `[00:00]`. He explicitly rejects the older wait-and-see convention: **"I do not
believe the old paradigm of [waiting until] 30 degrees, and then come back — patients should be treated
early and aggressively,"** ideally **before 45 degrees** of PIP contracture, which he associates with
**higher efficacy, lower recurrence, and less PIP joint scarring.**

## Shared principles before either procedure

`[01:01]`–`[02:04]` — Confirm a **palpable cord actually causing the contracture** (as opposed to scar or
flexor tendon insufficiency allowing tendon bowstringing to mimic contracture); set **realistic patient
expectations**, using educational materials/videos so patients understand risks regardless of which
technique they choose; and **map every cord with a marker** before injecting, including natatory,
commissural, and ADM cords — all of which sit **superficially, only 2–4mm beneath the skin.** He emphasizes
listing every possible side effect up front: **"if you list them all to the patients, then when they only
get one or two, they're a lot happier"** than surprised by an unmentioned one (his specific example: a
collagenase-related axillary nodule prompting a 3am call).

## Injection technique and the safe zone

`[02:04]`–`[05:07]` — Confirm the drug is actually in the syringe (visually distinguishing a small air
bubble from an air-filled syringe — "air does not release a cord"). Use adequate local anesthesia (wrist,
palmar, digital, dorsal, or PIP blocks as needed). **Maintain constant tension on the cord** to maximize
bowstringing, and inject at the **point of maximum bowstringing**, angled away from the flexor tendon
sheath. His **pinch-and-poke technique**: pinch the cord between two fingers, palpate it, and inject directly
between them. For wide cords, inject **transversely in a T-shape** rather than the usually-taught
longitudinal approach; for Y-shaped cords, inject at the base and up both limbs to release both fingers with
one dose. The stated **safe zone: 4mm from the proximal digital crease, 2–3mm in depth** — he notes deeper
injection is possible with experience but this is the conservative baseline. Critical safety point: **watch
the distance between the needle hub and skin continuously**, since needles tend to creep deeper during
injection — **if you penetrate dorsally, do not pull back and reinject; withdraw fully and reposition**, to
avoid driving the drug toward the flexor tendon.

## Manipulation, 24 hours later

`[05:07]`–`[06:10]` — Manipulation is done **24 hours after collagenase injection**, not sooner, since
delay reduces swelling, bruising, and tenderness at the time of manipulation. Skin tears are common —
**averaging 14% in his own experience, up to 22% in multi-center studies** — managed simply, without
sutures. Manipulation should move the finger through **extension, abduction, adduction, pronation, and
supination**, not extension alone, and should include the **adjacent unaffected fingers**, which he says
increases overall release.

## Needle fasciotomy technique specifics

`[06:10]`–`[09:12]` — Using a **20–25 gauge, 5/8-inch needle**, changed frequently — **15–25 needles per
finger** is normal, since dulling needles don't cut well and "you're not looking to save a dollar or two."
Distinguish the anesthesia used for numbing (intradermal, not a nerve block) from procedural blocks, and
maintain constant verbal check-in with the patient ("do you feel an electric shock?") to detect
neurovascular proximity. The needle bevel is kept **perpendicular to the cord** for cutting action; flexing
and extending the finger periodically confirms the needle isn't caught in the flexor tendon. Three named
techniques: **"clear, perforate, and slice."** Narrow lateral and natatory cords in particular respond well
to needling followed by **massage** — "you can feel the crackling and the release" even without a sense of
total mechanical release. After finishing, **reassess the palm proximal to distal** for residual cords
regardless of technique used, and check specifically for a **hidden or retracted cord** if a joint isn't
releasing as expected — Pess describes a recent case where an unsuspected retracted cord was the actual
block to full PIP release.

## Splinting and wound care, stated candidly

`[09:12]`–`[10:12]` — Pess states his own splinting practice directly, without claiming it's evidence-based:
**"I'm a splinter. I have no evidence. I splint for three to four months. I admit it, I'm ashamed, even
though I'm in a minority."** Static night splinting is his default; dynamic splinting (per what he calls "the
Philadelphia technique") is added specifically when there's an active extension lag at the PIP suggesting a
stretched central slip. For skin tears/lacerations from either technique, his approach is simple: **no
suture, no graft, no flap — local wound care, can get wet in the shower**, healing within two to five weeks
if kept moist. His memorable rule for an exposed flexor tendon: **"if you don't let it dry, it won't die"**
— keep it moist and it will not be damaged by exposure alone.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Skin tears during manipulation**, quantified at 14% (his practice) to 22% (multi-center data) `[05:07]`.
- **An active extension lag at the PIP joint after release**, flagged as a likely sign of central slip
  stretching requiring dynamic splinting `[09:12]`.
- **A hidden/retracted cord preventing full PIP release** despite apparently complete treatment of the
  visible cords `[08:12]`.
- **An unexplained axillary lump after collagenase**, cited as a real but easily-alarming side effect if not
  pre-disclosed `[02:04]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Pinch-and-poke technique** — pinching the cord between two fingers and injecting directly between them,
  used for both needle fasciotomy and collagenase `[02:04]`.
- **Safe injection zone** — 4mm from the proximal digital crease, 2–3mm in depth `[04:06]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"If you don't let it dry, it won't die"** — his rule for managing an exposed flexor tendon after skin
  tear `[10:12]`.
- **"Clear, perforate, and slice"** — his three-part naming of the needle fasciotomy cutting technique
  `[07:10]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Notably candid about the limits of his own evidence: he explicitly labels his 3–4 month splinting practice
as unsupported by evidence ("I have no evidence... I admit it, I'm ashamed"), distinguishing it clearly from
the technique-safety points he presents as learned, reproducible experience. He also states his overall
conclusion modestly: **"there's a couple of bad collagenase cases, [a] couple of bad needle cases — no
matter which technique you use, if you get good at it, you can get phenomenal results,"** avoiding a claim
that either technique is categorically superior.
