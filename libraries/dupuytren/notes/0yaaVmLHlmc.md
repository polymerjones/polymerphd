# Dr. Eva Maria Baur "Collagenase vs. PNF" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a non-randomized prospective comparison, collagenase (CCH) and needle fasciotomy (PNF) produced closely
matched extension-deficit outcomes at three weeks and comparable, lower-than-expected recurrence rates at 24
months — with the choice between them driven in practice by patient preference and practical factors like
cost, procedure count, and typical complication profile, rather than a clear efficacy advantage for either.**
Baur states the closeness of the result directly, comparing the two groups' extension deficit at the PIP
joint: **"it's really very narrow together — it's 16 and 23 degrees, so it's not so big [a] difference"**
`[03:07]`.

## Why the study isn't randomized

`[00:00]`–`[01:04]` — Baur explains the study proposes all available treatments (needle fasciotomy, surgery,
radiotherapy, and collagenase) to each patient, with the final treatment decision made jointly between
physician and patient — a design choice made explicitly because many patients arrive already asking for a
specific treatment (collagenase), making formal randomization impractical in this clinical setting. Enrollment
began **November 2011**. Of 93 candidate patients, **48 remained in the study**: 23 in the PNF group, 25 in
the CCH group. The clinic sees roughly 180 Dupuytren's patients a year overall (a mix of information-only
visits, PNF, and surgery).

## Study design

`[01:04]`–`[02:05]` — Follow-up occurred at 3 weeks, 6 weeks, 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months, with
complications tracked at one week and three weeks. Outcome measures: **range of motion / extension deficit**
of the treated fingers, and the **QuickDASH** questionnaire. Baur's PNF technique uses local skin
anesthesia followed by a **vertical/horizontal needle fasciotomy in a fan-shaped pattern**; the CCH group
received the standard **0.58mg dose**. A case example is shown maintaining correction from 3 to 24 months.
Post-CCH manipulation, Baur notes patients are advised to wear a splint, though she is candid that **it's
unclear whether continuous splint-wear is actually necessary**, and some patients instead adapt a soft
bandage's tension themselves.

## Results

`[02:05]`–`[04:09]` — The PNF group had somewhat more severe baseline cases and more multi-digit involvement,
yielding **58 treated fingers in PNF versus 32 in CCH**. At the 3-week post-treatment baseline: extension
deficit was closely matched between groups, including at the PIP joint (16° PNF vs. 23° CCH — "not so big a
difference"). **Clinical success** was defined as a straight finger post-treatment (under 10% persisting
extension deficit); **recurrence** was defined as more than 20 degrees of deficit increase from the 3-week
post-op baseline. Reported recurrence: **25% or 16%** (the two figures given without full disambiguation in
the transcript) — Baur notes she was surprised recurrence was *lower* after two years than she expected, more
so at the PIP joint, broadly consistent with rates reported elsewhere in the literature. QuickDASH scores were
nearly identical between groups. **No major complications occurred in either group**; typical complications
differed by type — **skin laceration for PNF**, versus **ecchymosis and hematoma for CCH** — and one flexor
tendon rupture is noted in a CCH-treated digit.

## Candid uncertainty about the improving results

`[04:09]`–`[05:12]` — Baur offers several possible, unconfirmed explanations for why results improved over the
study period: possibly more severe cases entering later, possibly a **learning curve effect**, and she notes
this candidly as an open question requiring longer follow-up. She also flags a specific selection bias: the
treatment ratio shifted from roughly 60% CCH / 40% PNF at the study's start toward more PNF later, which she
attributes partly to a colleague's report elsewhere of a flexor tendon rupture following CCH.

## Practical trade-offs

`[05:12]`–`[07:15]` — In favor of PNF: **lower cost, ability to treat multiple digits and joints in one
session**, patients returning to work or hobbies within days with no restriction, and generally softer/less
painful skin than after collagenase injection — though Baur notes PNF becomes personally "a little bit
boring" for the surgeon after 15–20 minutes on a difficult case. She also flags that **surgery performed after
a failed PNF is sometimes more difficult (more scarring) than surgery after failed collagenase.** One
practical caution she offers, contrasting with another speaker's guidance the previous day: she avoids doing
**severe-case PNF in wintertime**, because bandaging (not splinting) is harder to manage well on a cold hand,
citing one case with a poor outcome from this specific timing issue.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Skin laceration as the characteristic PNF complication, versus ecchymosis and hematoma for CCH** — the
  two treatments' complication profiles explicitly distinguished by type, not just rate `[04:09]`.
- **A flexor tendon rupture following CCH**, cited as a factor that shifted the clinic's later treatment mix
  toward more PNF `[05:12]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Baur discloses no conflicts of interest and is explicit and repeated about this study's limitations: it is
**prospective but not randomized**, specifically because patient preference for a named treatment made
randomization impractical in her clinical setting; she is candid that she does not have a full explanation for
why recurrence was lower than expected at 24 months, and flags the shifting CCH/PNF treatment ratio over the
study period as a real source of bias rather than omitting it.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly relevant to `93IWNYYqCjs`** (Warwick's "what we may never know" talk) — Baur's own study is a live
example of exactly the equipoise problem Warwick describes: patients arriving with a treatment preference made
true randomization impractical, precisely the obstacle his talk argues makes valid comparative trials
essentially undoable. **Directly comparable to `oRdE0UKeJF0`** (Van Rijssen's randomized PNF-vs-fasciectomy
trial) and `0MBhLab1KUI` (Peimer's CORDLESS collagenase data) — Baur's 24-month PNF and CCH recurrence figures
sit in the same general range as those other studies' reported rates, though methodology and follow-up length
differ enough that direct numeric comparison should be made cautiously. **Relevant to `R4WPhcOpAMo`**
(Selles's consensus recurrence definition talk) — Baur's own recurrence definition (20+ degrees increase from
a 3-week post-op baseline) is close to but not identical to that consensus definition's 20-degree,
6-week-baseline standard.
