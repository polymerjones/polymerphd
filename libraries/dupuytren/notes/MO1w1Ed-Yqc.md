# Terri Skirven OTR/L CHT, "Hand Therapy for Dupuytren's Contracture" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Post-surgical hand therapy for Dupuytren's is "accepted practice" despite a thin formal evidence base —
and the clearest evidence that does exist points to a specific, demanding mechanism: outcomes track splint
*compliance*, not just splint prescription.** Skirven cites Gelberman and colleagues (1992): patients who
kept a dynamic splint on continuously, day and night, for four weeks post-op, then weaned over months,
showed **59% improvement in PIP extension at two years among compliant patients versus 25% among
non-compliant patients** `[04:09]`–`[05:11]`.

## Pre-operative splinting: modest but real

`[00:03]`–`[02:05]` — Several **surgically implanted continuous-elongation devices** (Messina and Messina's
"TEC" device; a lower-profile version called the "Pipster") were tested to pre-stretch contracture before
surgery. Reported benefits when followed by surgery: **simplified surgery, fewer complications, shorter
rehabilitation, improved PIP extension, and in some cases avoided skin necrosis, avoided vascular loss, and
avoided amputation** — but **contracture consistently recurred once the device was removed.** In Messina's
own initial cohort (15 patients, 2 weeks of device use at 2mm/day elongation, followed by 20 hand-therapy
sessions plus ultrasound and night splinting): at roughly 3-month follow-up, **6 patients had no recurrence
and 9 had recurrence "to a lesser extent"** — and notably, **none of these patients ultimately chose
fasciectomy**, suggesting the pre-op device alone satisfied some patients.

## The value of a single pre-surgery therapy visit

`[02:05]`–`[03:08]` — Not always available or insurance-covered, but Skirven argues a single pre-op visit
helps set a baseline and manage expectations directly: **"many patients... will say, 'if I had known what
was involved, I may not have elected to have the procedure.'"** Framing this clearly in advance, she argues,
reduces post-operative anxiety even though it doesn't change the procedure itself.

## Post-operative splinting: two competing rationales

`[04:09]`–`[09:15]` — Two named approaches, both with stated evidence:

- **Gelberman's continuous dynamic splinting**: worn day and night for 4 weeks (removed only for
  exercises), then static night splinting begins while daytime dynamic splinting continues, then weaned to
  intervals at 8–12 weeks, with night splinting continuing **up to about six months.** Result: the 59%
  vs. 25% compliance-dependent PIP extension gain noted above.
- **Roz Evans's "no tension" approach**: a dorsal block splint holding the MCP joints flexed ~45 degrees
  while allowing the PIP and DIP joints to gently extend within the splint, applied **24 hours
  post-operatively.** The stated rationale: mechanical stress on digital vessels and nerves may cause local
  hypoxia and inflammation, and tension on healing tissue may drive hypertrophic scar formation — so
  removing tension from the equation should reduce flare-ups, scarring, and total therapy time. Evans's own
  retrospective-versus-prospective comparison (traditional maximal-extension approach versus her no-tension
  protocol) reported **improved motion, decreased flare rate, decreased scar rate, fewer therapy visits, and
  lower cost** with the no-tension approach. Patients are cautioned specifically **not to abruptly or
  aggressively stretch the finger** within the splint — extension is meant to be coaxed gradually.

## Continuous passive motion: genuinely mixed evidence

`[09:15]`–`[10:17]` — Sampson and colleagues (early 1990s) found **"really no clinical benefit"** from CPM
(8–10 hours/day for 4 weeks, combined with standard night splinting) compared to standard care without it.
Skirven's own Philadelphia series, by contrast, found CPM **well tolerated, without adverse effect on
swelling or wound healing**, and subjectively useful specifically for **multi-digit, open-palm procedure
patients**, facilitating earlier motion recovery than standard technique alone. She flags two practical
caveats regardless of benefit: **precise fitting must be done by the therapist, not the equipment vendor**,
and patients need to understand **"the machine wasn't going to do all the work."**

## The boutonnière-pattern problem after release

`[11:17]`–`[12:18]` — For patients with severe pre-operative PIP flexion contracture presenting a
boutonnière-like appearance, Skirven notes a specific post-op complication: **full passive extension may be
present, but an active extension lag persists** because of central slip attenuation/weakening from the
chronic contracture. Management requires **actively emphasizing PIP extension plus DIP flexion with the PIP
held straight**, specifically to stretch the oblique retinacular ligament and lateral bands — using a gutter
splint for a continuous 3-week period, or a **Capener splint** (spring-loaded, assists extension while
allowing flexion) to rebalance tendon power toward the weakened extensor side.

## General principles and modalities

`[12:18]`–`[16:21]` — **"No pain, no gain has no place in hand therapy"** — over-aggressive exercise is
described as capable of inciting an exaggerated, prolonged inflammatory response, a point Skirven says needs
active reinforcement with patients and family who tend to want to overdo it. Daily home exercise is
emphasized as necessary beyond clinic visits. Adjunct modalities named: heat, cold, ultrasound, electrical
stimulation; **iontophoresis with dexamethasone** for burning pain or painful scars; compression gloves,
Coban wrap, and galvanic stimulation for edema; **micropore paper tape along the incision** (Evans's
recommendation) to reduce scar widening and hypertrophy, plus massage, silicone gel sheeting, and elastomer
putty for scar softening. Night splinting typically continues for **six months** — Skirven's own patient
analogy: **"kind of like someone wears braces on their teeth."** Light strengthening begins cautiously later,
with a specific warning against **over-repetitious gripping (putty, balls, hand grips), which can
precipitate carpal tunnel syndrome or triggering** in the presence of residual post-operative swelling.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Recurrence of contracture once a pre-operative elongation device is removed** — described as consistent
  across studies, even when the device produced real interim straightening `[00:03]`.
- **Active extension lag despite full passive extension**, specifically in boutonnière-pattern presentations
  after release, from central slip attenuation `[11:17]`.
- **Post-operative flare, hypertrophic scarring, and prolonged inflammation**, linked to mechanical tension
  on healing tissue and to overly aggressive exercise `[06:11]`, `[12:18]`.
- **New-onset carpal tunnel syndrome or trigger finger symptoms provoked by over-repetitive gripping
  exercises during recovery** `[16:21]`.
- **Patient expectation mismatch about return to activity** — Skirven notes patients commonly assume they
  can resume activities like golf within days of surgery `[16:21]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **"No tension" splinting approach** — Evans's post-op protocol minimizing mechanical stress on healing
  tissue via a dorsal block splint, applied within 24 hours of surgery `[05:11]`.
- **Continuous elongation device (TEC)** — a surgically implanted pre-operative device that gradually
  stretches contracture before definitive surgery `[00:03]`.
- **Capener splint** — a spring-loaded splint assisting extension while permitting flexion, used to
  rebalance a weakened extensor mechanism `[12:18]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Long-term night splinting compared to wearing dental braces** — a patient-facing way to explain why
  splinting continues for months after visible correction `[15:20]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Skirven opens by stating plainly that **post-surgical hand therapy is accepted practice "notwithstanding
the fact that formal study and literature support is lacking."** Where evidence does exist, she cites it
specifically and sometimes conflictingly (CPM: one published study found no benefit, her own series found
subjective benefit) rather than picking one to present as settled. Pre-operative device benefits are
presented alongside the consistent finding that gains reverse once the device is removed, without
minimizing that limitation.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly relevant to `-jLfWXX3YUM`** (Eaton's Seven Questions video), which separately reports **"a half a
dozen well-conducted randomized, prospective, controlled" studies showing no long-term benefit of hand
therapy and splinting after open surgery, one even showing worse results with aggressive splinting"** —
apparently in tension with the Gelberman compliance data cited here, though the two may be reconcilable:
Eaton's cited studies concern long-term outcome differences between therapy and no therapy, while Gelberman's
data (as presented here) concerns differences *within* a therapy protocol by compliance level — a
distinction worth flagging explicitly rather than treating as a contradiction. **Relevant to `9LY5fyLw618`**
(Hurst's collagenase talk), which favors minimally invasive treatment partly for its **shorter recovery
window** — this talk's extensive post-operative therapy protocol (up to six months of night splinting) is
the recovery burden specifically associated with open surgical procedures that minimally invasive treatment
is positioned against.
