# John Agee MD, "Growing the PIP Straight with the Digit Widget" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**A PIP joint held in fixed flexion by Dupuytren's disease can be gradually "grown" straight by skeletal
traction rather than surgically stretched, and doing so via bone pins rather than skin avoids a specific,
demonstrable injury that skin-transmitted force causes.** Agee frames the device's underlying principle
directly: **"the right amount of force will cause something to grow, just like reversing your child's club
foot with serial plaster casts"** `[01:01]`.

## Why skeletal traction, not skin-transmitted force

`[10:13]`–`[11:14]` — Agee presents this as the talk's most important point, backed by a simple bench
experiment: hanging a 50-gram weight from a hinged glass slide representing a finger, held near the fingertip,
produces barely visible skin blanching; **shifting the same weight roughly 40% closer to the hinge raises
the effective pressure to about 100 grams per square centimeter and produces clear blanching** — and he
notes the problem is worse on the thinner dorsal PIP skin specifically. Under that much sustained pressure,
patients either remove the device from pain, or — in a neuropathic hand where they can't feel it — develop
**a red spot, blister, or ulcer**, the mechanism he explicitly compares to pressure injury in leprosy-related
neuropathy. **Skeletal traction, transmitting force through pins directly into bone, avoids this skin
pathway entirely.**

## How the device works

`[00:00]`–`[04:08]` — Two threaded pins are placed in the proximal end of the middle phalanx; a rubber-band
motor (dental orthodontic bands, selected for a torque range of roughly **100–250 gram-centimeters**)
transmits extension torque to the PIP joint via a band, with the counterforce taken by a cuff around the
hand — designed so the palmar side stays free and the patient can use the hand while the joint "grows"
straight, typically over **two to three months** in severe cases. Agee is explicit this is not a fixed
protocol: **"I thought you'd end up having a little chart... while I was completely wrong. In biologic
systems, mother nature decides how you do things"** — the right torque is titrated per patient by watching
for continued extension gain versus pain and swelling, which signal too much force.

## The torque-imbalance model, and why PIP and MP joints are linked

`[04:08]`–`[08:11]` — Agee frames virtually all PIP contracture as a torque imbalance: **"torque is the
product of the force on a tendon multiplied by its moment arm to the joint's axis of rotation."** In
Dupuytren's, a band/nodule on the flexor side has a large moment arm, while the thin extensor tendon has
little competing force. Two structural changes worsen this over time as the joint stays flexed: **the
profundus and superficialis tendons' suspension can shift palmarward, increasing their moment arm and
thus their flexion torque**, and **the lateral band migrates palmarward with the fixed contracture — unless
it can move back dorsal to the joint's axis once the finger is straightened, active extension cannot be
restored**, regardless of how well the joint itself is released.

Separately, **PIP flexion contracture increases tension in that finger's own MP extensor** (since the
tendon unit is pulled taut) while **decreasing tension in the MP flexor** — a mechanism Agee describes as
driving **MP hyperextension over time, evolving toward a claw deformity** in many patients unless the
Dupuytren's process itself prevents it. He illustrates the inverse with a case of ulnar nerve palsy causing
the same MP hyperextension by a related biomechanical pathway, and a diabetic neuropathy patient with
complete intrinsic muscle atrophy whose *absence* of clawing let him treat PIP contracture more simply — "
two diseases sort of work in synergy."

## Why surgical release alone can cause earlier recurrence

`[12:16]`–`[14:17]` — Agee argues cutting certain structures during release — the lateral retinacular
ligament (if it scars into the post-op wound, trapping the lateral band from ascending dorsally) or the
collateral ligaments/volar plate tails — can **displace the flexor tendons palmarward, increasing their
moment arm** even by "just a few millimeters," which he states produces **"a 20 or 30 percent increase in
the moment arm, and therefore the torque"** driving flexion — his proposed explanation for why some
surgical releases recur unusually quickly. His stated preference follows directly: **"why release these
joints if you can grow them straight?"** — reserving surgery for excising the disease tissue itself, with
the device handling joint extension.

## Protocol notes from case follow-up

`[14:17]`–`[15:17]` — In his final case example, torque was reapplied as soon as post-operative wounds
looked ready, and continued **"until the graph [of joint angle over time] tops out and flattens."** He
notes a specific correction from his hand therapist: removing the device the moment the angle plateaus
invites **elastic rebound**, so keeping traction on for **two to three additional weeks past plateau**
reduces the chance the joint droops back afterward.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **PIP joint fixed in flexion, resistant to passive straightening** `[00:00]`.
- **MP joint hyperextension developing secondary to a long-standing PIP contracture**, and progression
  toward a claw-hand posture `[07:11]`.
- **Skin blistering, ulceration, or pain under splint/cast pressure**, particularly in a neuropathic hand
  where pain wouldn't otherwise signal the injury `[11:14]`.
- **Loss of active extension after a technically successful release**, attributed to the lateral band
  failing to migrate back dorsal to the joint axis `[06:09]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Digit Widget** — a rubber-band-motor skeletal-traction device, pinned into bone, used to gradually
  extend a fixed PIP joint contracture `[00:00]`.
- **Torque imbalance** — Agee's organizing model for PIP contracture: flexor-side force and moment arm
  exceeding extensor-side force and moment arm `[00:00]`.
- **Moment arm displacement** — a structural shift (from surgical release or disease progression) that
  increases a tendon's effective leverage and thus its torque, without changing the tendon's own force
  `[12:16]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **Growing a contracted joint straight the way serial plaster casts correct a club foot** — gradual,
  biological correction rather than one-time mechanical stretching `[01:01]`.
- **The bench experiment with a weighted glass slide**, demonstrating skin pressure rising sharply as force
  moves closer to a joint's hinge — a concrete, reproducible illustration of why skeletal traction
  outperforms skin-transmitted splinting `[10:13]`–`[11:14]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Agee is candid that his device's dosing is empirical, not derived from a predictive model: **"I thought
you'd end up having a little chart... I was completely wrong."** He also explicitly labels the mechanism
behind why the extensor side becomes "impotent" during long-standing contracture as **"unproven, at least
in my hands"** `[09:12]`, and flags that outcomes "don't always hold up... at least initially" without
overstating durability `[12:16]`.
