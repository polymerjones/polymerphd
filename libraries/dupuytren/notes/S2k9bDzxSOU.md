# Dr. Mels van Driel: "Peyronie's Disease Treatment" 2015 Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**In a series of over 400 Peyronie's disease patients, 22% also had Dupuytren's disease — and van Driel walks
through a severity-stratified surgical algorithm while noting collagenase's Peyronie's results so far are more
modest than in Dupuytren's, with only recent (January 2015) European approval.** He opens with the comorbidity
figure directly: **"in a series of more than 400 patients presenting with Peyronie's disease, I found in 22
percent of them Dupuytren's disease"** `[00:00]`.

## Surgical history and a critique of the original technique

`[00:00]`–`[01:02]` — Surgical treatment traces to the 19th century; **Lowsley (US, 1946)** first reported
resecting fibrotic tunica tissue (the "plaque") combined with free fat grafting for hemostasis. **Nesbit
(1960s, US)** described correcting congenital penile curvature by shortening the convex side via small
elliptical tunica excisions, with many later variations. Van Driel is pointedly critical of Nesbit's original
technique, arguing its patients must have experienced **glans numbness from nerve damage**, since — contrary to
a commonly depicted textbook illustration — **the dorsal nerve is not a single bundle or single nerve.**

## Treatment algorithm and counseling

`[01:02]`–`[03:05]` — Per current European guidelines: patients are reassured Peyronie's is **not a form of
cancer**; plication (Nesbit or similar) or, in select cases, grafting surgery is considered **only once
disease is stable and no erectile dysfunction is present**. Van Driel stresses **counseling and the patient's
own expectations** as central to the final treatment decision, and recommends urologists proactively identify
patients at high risk of developing erectile dysfunction — for example via **penile duplex ultrasound**.
His own informed-consent discussion covers: overnight admission, a compression bandage, a bladder catheter,
possible decreased glans sensibility, circumcision, further shortening with Nesbit's technique (roughly **15
degrees of correction per 1cm of shortening** — he tells patients plainly **"the penis will never become a
ruler"**), six weeks of sexual abstinence, and pain during nocturnal erections. For non-Nesbit surgery, he
advises **tadalafil 5mg daily** (the same class of drug as sildenafil) to promote nocturnal erections and help
prevent graft contraction.

## Technique

`[03:05]`–`[05:11]` — Standard approach: circumcision, penile degloving, and excision of elliptical tunica
segments guided by **repeated intraoperative artificial erections induced with saline**, closed with Vicryl
sutures — producing a shortened but straightened penis, consistent with European guidelines. **Plaque incision
or excision is reserved for calcified/ossified plaques, hourglass deformity, or marked foreshortening with
preserved good rigidity**: a paraurethral incision, extended dissection of the neurovascular bundle, bilateral
relaxing plaque incisions, and grafting. For **"towbar" penis** (a severely ossified, sharp-edged plaque), the
plaque is removed specifically because of its painful sharp edges, followed by **bovine pericardium grafting**
and neurovascular bundle reconstruction. A ventral-curvature case example (van Driel notes roughly 50% of
Peyronie's patients eventually develop this curvature direction) is shown treated with dissection, plaque
grafting, and bundle reconstruction.

## Grafting materials and mechanical devices

`[05:11]`–`[06:12]` — Mechanical traction/vacuum devices exist for supportive use or early-stage disease, but
van Driel notes most patients find them too painful for practical use. On graft material: the ideal graft
would be **pliable, compliant, low-antigenicity, low-infection-risk, minimally inflammatory, available in
multiple sizes, and reasonably priced** — **no material meets every criterion**. Many materials have been
tried over 50 years; some (e.g. a synthetic graft material referred to in the transcript, likely Gore-Tex)
have been **abandoned due to high infection rates**; others required a second incision and more operative
time. Most current practice uses **bovine or porcine pericardium**, chosen because it withstands the high
intracavernosal pressure of erection and, in animal studies, showed reduced long-term fibrosis.

## Erectile dysfunction and prosthesis

`[07:13]` — For patients with coexisting erectile dysfunction unresponsive to PDE5 inhibitors (e.g.
sildenafil) or intracavernosal injection therapy, **penile prosthesis implantation** is discussed, with its
own risks (urethral injury, infection) — but van Driel notes contemporary inflatable implants are strong
enough to withstand the pressure needed to overcome Peyronie's-related curvature, and can double as a tissue
expander, with acceptable results generally achieved after a couple of remodeling sessions.

## Collagenase — early results

`[08:18]`–`[09:19]` — Conservative (non-surgical) treatment is currently limited to early-stage disease;
**collagenase injection is the only conservative option shown to significantly improve deviation and plaque
length**, though van Driel notes only a limited number of studies exist and **European approval had come only
a few months earlier (January 30)** at the time of this talk. Technique: a penile block, then injection of
collagenase into the plaque (deep to the neurovascular bundle), followed by manual remodeling pressure. **Phase
3 results: curvature decreased in 34% of collagenase-treated patients versus 18% of placebo** — a more modest
effect size than reported elsewhere in this library for Dupuytren's collagenase outcomes. Other trials
reported differing remodeling protocols and differing advice on traction/vacuum device use; van Driel flags
**fracture, bleeding, and cost** as practical considerations for injectable treatment.

## Closing framing

`[09:19]` — Van Driel closes with a deliberately provocative framing comparison: **"if one asked a man to
choose between losing the function of his fingers or his penis, we all know what decision he will make"** —
underscoring, without further elaboration, how differently patients may weigh treatment risk depending on
which body part is affected.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- **Glans numbness from dorsal nerve injury**, specifically flagged as an underappreciated consequence of the
  original Nesbit technique `[01:02]`.
- **Ventral penile curvature**, quantified as affecting roughly half of Peyronie's patients over time
  `[05:11]`.
- **Painful sharp edges from an ossified "towbar" plaque**, given as the specific indication for plaque
  removal in that presentation `[04:06]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Plaque** — the fibrotic lesion in the tunica albuginea characteristic of Peyronie's disease `[00:00]`.
- **"Towbar" penis** — severely ossified plaque with sharp, painful edges `[04:06]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"The penis will never become a ruler"** — van Driel's own phrase for setting patient expectations about
  residual curvature after Nesbit-type shortening `[03:05]`.
- **The closing fingers-versus-penis framing** — illustrating how risk tolerance for treatment complications
  can differ sharply by affected body part `[09:19]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Van Driel presents this largely as an overview of established surgical technique and current European
guidelines rather than his own primary research, and is explicit that collagenase evidence in Peyronie's is
still limited ("only a very limited number of studies") and much newer than the Dupuytren's collagenase
literature, with regulatory approval only months old at the time of the talk. He states his critique of the
original Nesbit technique as his own inference from the described method, not as directly documented in
Nesbit's own reported outcomes.

## Conflicts with other sources

**Directly relevant to `LVxj5lEVnz0`** (Wach's patient survey talk), which reports "relatively little" of a
condition abbreviated only as "IPP" in that transcript among Dupuytren's survey respondents — this talk's
subject, Peyronie's disease (sometimes termed "induratio penis plastica," consistent with that abbreviation),
is very plausibly the same condition, and this talk's 22% comorbidity figure is a directly relevant data point
for interpreting that survey's likely under-sampling of Peyronie's patients through Dupuytren's-focused
recruitment channels. **Relevant to `OwSS6grbMAc`** and **`0MBhLab1KUI`** (this library's Dupuytren's
collagenase trial notes) — this talk's Peyronie's collagenase results (34% vs. 18% placebo curvature
reduction) are visibly more modest than the Dupuytren's collagenase correction rates reported in those notes,
a useful comparison point for readers assessing collagenase's effect size across different fibrotic
conditions. **Relevant to any note in this library discussing Dupuytren's diathesis or associated ectopic
disease** — Peyronie's disease is a named associated condition in that broader diathesis pattern.
