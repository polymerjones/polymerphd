# Susan Emeigh Hart VMD PhD, "A Primer of Collagen Biology in Dupuytren's Disease" 2010 Miami Dupuytren Symposium

## Central claim

**Collagen itself is a valid treatment target in Dupuytren's, regardless of which of two competing
explanations for the disease you favor — because both explanations converge on the same actionable
conclusion: reduce the tension collagen is under.** Hart's closing framing: **"is collagen a valid target in
Dupuytren's disease? ... it doesn't matter whether you think of this disease as an imbalance in collagen
remodeling, or [as] wound healing gone bad — there's equal validity in either cutting the rope to relieve
the tension, or weakening the rope... so that it stops pulling on myofibroblasts"** `[18:21]`.

## What makes a protein a collagen

`[02:03]`–`[04:04]` — Two defining structural features: a **triple-helical segment made of three
polypeptide chains** (identical chains = homotrimer, different = heterotrimer), stabilized by a repeating
**Gly-X-Y amino acid motif**, where X and Y are usually proline or hydroxyproline. This structure makes
collagen simultaneously **very stable once formed, and very unstable during assembly** — Hart's summary:
**"hard to make and hard to break."** Of the ~28 collagen types (from 34 genes), the ones relevant to
Dupuytren's cords are the **fibril-forming collagens**: **type I is the primary structural component of the
cord** (and the most abundant collagen in the body generally), **type III is also present in the cord**, and
**type V partners with type I to help form and stabilize fibrils.** **Type IV**, by contrast, forms
basement membranes of blood vessels, nerve perineurium, and epithelial structures — explicitly **not a
collagen you'd want to target in Dupuytren's treatment**, since it plays a structural-support role elsewhere
in the same tissue.

## How collagen is built and taken apart

`[05:06]`–`[10:09]` — Synthesis happens in two phases: monomers assemble inside the cell, chaperoned by the
protein **HSP47** to prevent tangling before proline/lysine residues are hydroxylated (required for
cross-linking); the completed triple helix is then extruded through a specialized structure called a
**fibripositor**, which places fibrils where needed and keeps them grouped while still unstable. Once
propeptides are cleaved extracellularly, fibrils **self-assemble**, a process smoothed by **fibronectin,
specific cell-surface integrins (alpha-2-beta-1, alpha-5-beta-1), and lysyl oxidase cross-linking.**

Degradation requires an enzyme to do **three distinct things: bind the collagen, unwind it, and cleave
it** — Hart is explicit that **only enzymes proven to do all three qualify as a true "collagenase"**, and
that only two mammalian enzyme classes have this activity, primarily **matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)**,
with a more limited role for cathepsins L and K. The initial cleavage product is **gelatin** — itself
unstable enough to denature at body temperature — which is then further broken down by other proteases and
phagocytosed by fibroblasts and macrophages, the same cells capable of laying collagen down in the first
place.

## Two hypotheses for collagen's role in disease

`[12:13]`–`[16:21]` — Hart frames the field's explanations as two lenses, not necessarily exclusive:

1. **Remodeling imbalance**: collagen deposition and degradation are normally balanced processes; Dupuytren's
   reflects a skew toward deposition. Cited gene-expression evidence: **COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL3A2
   upregulated** (more synthesis), **alpha-5-beta-1 integrin, fibronectin, and lysyl oxidase 2 upregulated**
   (enhanced fibrillogenesis and stabilization), alongside **MMP3 downregulated and TIMP1 (an MMP inhibitor)
   upregulated** — a consistent pattern favoring net collagen accumulation.
2. **Wound healing gone bad / collagen as signal transducer**: collagen is the medium through which
   myofibroblasts sense and respond to mechanical tension, not merely a structural byproduct. Hart's own
   analogy: **"collagen is the rope"** in a game of tug-of-war — fibroblasts sensing tension respond by
   **recruiting more players** (differentiating into myofibroblasts), **pulling harder** (more actin
   filaments), **strengthening the rope** (more synthesis, thicker/less elastic fibers, more cross-linking),
   or **shortening/cutting the rope** — a **localized, focal upregulation of MMPs right next to the
   myofibroblast**, remodeling collagen even while under tension. She notes this specific idea dates to 1981
   in the literature, not new. This lens also explains an otherwise-confusing pattern in gene data: **MMP1,
   MMP13, and MMP14 are simultaneously upregulated in nodules alongside deposition genes** — MMP14 in
   particular is membrane-bound, keeping degradation localized to one spot near the cell rather than
   diffuse, reconciling the apparent contradiction of "more collagen made AND more collagen broken down at
   once."

## What this means for collagenase treatment

`[16:21]`–`[17:21]` — Hart connects the mechanism directly to injectable collagenase: literature on
clostridial collagenase suggests its **initial action on fibrils increases their elasticity**, making them
*more* susceptible to further degradation — meaning the drug's effect may not require overpowering active
myofibroblast tension so much as **giving MMP-style degradation a foothold once the cells briefly stop
pulling**, and this can happen efficiently even while some tension remains.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

None directly — this is molecular and biochemical background, not a discussion of patient-facing symptoms.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Gly-X-Y motif** — the repeating amino acid pattern (glycine plus two variable residues, often proline/
  hydroxyproline) that defines and stabilizes the collagen triple helix `[02:03]`.
- **Fibripositor** — the cellular structure through which newly formed collagen triple helices are secreted
  and positioned extracellularly `[07:07]`.
- **True collagenase activity** — defined strictly as requiring all three of binding, unwinding, and
  cleaving collagen; not every protease that touches collagen qualifies `[09:09]`.
- **Gelatin** — the unstable, heat-denaturable product of the first collagenase cleavage step `[10:09]`.
- **TIMP1** — named in the talk as "a protein that would inhibit MMPs," noted as upregulated in
  Dupuytren's tissue `[13:14]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Collagen is the rope" in a game of tug-of-war** — mechanical tension recruiting more myofibroblast
  "players," who pull harder, strengthen the rope, or selectively cut/shorten it — a single image spanning
  the entire disease-mechanism argument `[14:16]`–`[15:19]`.
- **"Hard to make and hard to break"** — collagen's structural stability once formed, contrasted with its
  instability during assembly `[02:03]`.
- **"You can't play tug of war with a bungee cord"** — why increased collagen cross-linking (reduced
  elasticity) matters mechanically for contracture `[15:19]`.

## Source-stated confidence

Hart is explicit about her financial conflict of interest upfront (full-time employee of a pharmaceutical
company developing a Dupuytren's treatment) and frames herself as a relative newcomer to matrix biology
specifically (five years in the field at the time). She presents the two disease hypotheses as **equally
valid framings rather than favoring one**, and flags the collagenase-elasticity mechanism as coming "from
the literature," not her own data.
