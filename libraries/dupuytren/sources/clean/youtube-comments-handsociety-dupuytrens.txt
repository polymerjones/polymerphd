TITLE: YouTube comments — "Dupuytren's Contracture: Causes, Symptoms, & Treatment" (TheHandSociety)
SOURCE URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V_EmTwz9iso
RETRIEVED: 2026-08-23
PARAGRAPH COUNT: 9
NOTE: This is a paraphrased summary of the video's comment section, not a verbatim capture of it —
commenters' own wording and usernames are not reproduced. It records what viewers (mostly patients
and family members) reported experiencing, not clinical evidence. Every claim here is anecdotal and
should be read as "a commenter said," never as an established fact — several of the remedies
described below have no supporting evidence beyond a single person's account, and that is noted
explicitly where it applies.
======================================================================

[¶1] Commenters frequently mentioned Northern European or Scandinavian ancestry (several referred to
it jokingly as a "Viking" trait), and one described a cluster within a Sicilian family (a
grandmother, her brother, the commenter, and a cousin all affected). Onset ages mentioned ranged
from the twenties into the sixties.

[¶2] Surgical outcomes reported in the comments were mixed and often negative. Several commenters
described recurrence after surgery — one reported four surgeries with recurrence each time,
describing physical therapy as making things "come back faster and often worse" in their
experience; another said surgery made both treated fingers worse and left ongoing pain months into
physiotherapy. One commenter's single-line claim of "basically a 15% success rate" was not
elaborated on or sourced.

[¶3] On collagenase injection (Xiaflex), one commenter reported it working well in one hand but,
after a repeat injection in the other hand, having no effect at all — describing this as having
"become immune" to it — and was scheduled for a minimally invasive procedure to remove a piece of
the cord through a small incision.

[¶4] Multiple commenters pushed back on the idea that Dupuytren's contracture is painless: one
described significant ongoing pain despite several injections and surgery, and asked what a "DC
flare" refers to; another commenter replied that it can indeed be very painful.

[¶5] A cluster of comments discussed a boron supplement. One commenter reported that their wife's
contracture returned after both surgery and injections, then resolved — hand-clawing gone, lumps
gone, fingers straightening — within a few months of starting a daily boron regimen (described as
dissolving a small amount of borax in water and taking a few tablespoons of the solution each
morning). This drew significant pushback in the replies: one commenter stated there is no direct
scientific evidence or clinical literature supporting boron as a Dupuytren's treatment, though
boron is used for inflammation in arthritis; another questioned whether the improvement actually
followed the earlier surgery rather than the boron; several asked for exact dosing without a
clear answer being given in the thread.

[¶6] Other unverified self-reported remedies mentioned, each by a single commenter and without
supporting evidence cited: hormone replacement therapy (one commenter reported their Dupuytren's
and general joint pain resolved after starting HRT at 59, with no recurrence four years later);
regularly and manually stretching/pulling the fingers (one commenter reported more range of motion
and a smaller nodule over time, while also noting skepticism that the video itself promotes an
injectable-enzyme treatment and suggesting viewers "follow the financial interests"); a combination
of intermittent fasting, daily olive oil, and NAC (N-acetylcysteine) supplementation (one commenter
described a reduction in a burning sensation and apparent improvement after starting this
combination, explicitly framed their own account as uncertain about which element helped, and
invited others to try it and report back); and holding the hand and arm in ice water for twenty
minutes (raised as an open question by one commenter, with no one in the thread reporting having
tried it).

[¶7] One commenter argued, as their own theory rather than a cited medical claim, that their
Dupuytren's traced back to a specific childhood hand injury (a jammed finger that healed poorly)
and that the body was essentially forming hardened "callus" tissue in response to repeated strain
on already-damaged tissue, extending the same explanation to a separate old toe injury. They
cautioned other commenters against overexerting the hands based on this personal theory.

[¶8] A different commenter argued the video was missing radiation therapy as a treatment option,
describing it as common outside the United States and increasingly available domestically.

[¶9] The comments also included general appreciation for the video's clarity and quality, with no
substantive disagreement about the video's core content — only about what it left out or how well
various treatments actually work in practice.
