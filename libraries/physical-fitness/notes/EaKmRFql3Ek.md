# TOP 5 ARM EXERCISES | WHITEBOARD WORKOUT

## Central claim

**Both lifters independently rank their five favorite bicep and tricep exercises and, in several
cases, explicitly separate "what I like" from "what works" — most pointedly on hammer curls,
which Seth calls his most-hated exercise for any body part while crediting a specific technique
change (copying Phil Heath's sub-90° range of motion) as the point his arms actually began to
grow.** `[06:05]` "This is my number one most hated exercise for every single body part. I hate
doing them... but guess what? They work." `[20:22]` "I watched a video of Phil Heath just do like
four or five on this arm... and he never broke that 90 degrees. Never. He never came up, and I was
like, what the hell is he doing? ... I started doing them that way just cuz I saw it... my arms
really started to shape [and] fill out."

## Building a personal exercise hierarchy over time (11:12–12:13)

Seth states directly that his list reflects years of accumulated testing, not a fixed ideal: `[11:12]`
"I have done over the past 27 years... so many different types of exercises, so many different
types of workouts, so many different intensifiers." He names the moment liking an exercise stopped
being the deciding factor: `[11:12]`–`[12:13]` "I hate this exercise, but I know it works for me...
I got this far by doing things that I just like to do. Now I have to start doing some things that I
don't like to do to get to the next level."

## Hammer curls: sub-90° range and grip position (05:03–07:06, 32:22–35:25)

The core technique, credited directly to observation rather than independent discovery: `[20:22]`
"he never broke that 90 degrees" — meaning the dumbbell never rises past the forearm-parallel
point. Grip position along the handle is restated as a distinct, separately testable variable:
`[32:22]`–`[33:22]` "hold my hands on the back side of the dumbbells and do a hammer curl and see
how it feels versus it being here... it's going to feel slightly different." Cross-body variation
is named for a different target: `[33:22]`–`[34:24]` "you'll feel a little bit more in the
brachialis when you do it across the body."

## Incline dumbbell curls: bench angle and injury-aware restraint (24:31–25:33)

Bench angle is tested live and pinned to a specific reference: `[24:31]` "I'd go one click above the
45[-degree]... whenever I go a little heavier, I would go one click up." Seth names a specific
compensation risk tied to the position: `[25:33]` "don't get out of control here because of that
hanging weight in the position... there's a lot of things that can go wrong if you start trying to
horse the weights too much"; and later, `[25:33]`–`[26:35]` "I can already feel it pulling... on my
front delt" as the signal to keep weight moderate.

## Tricep exercises: long-head focus, and a disclosed permanent injury (09:10–10:10, 26:35–27:37)

Seth states a specific self-diagnosed training bias: `[09:10]` "I am very long-head dominant in my
favorite tricep exercises... I'd say 75% of my training has been long-head focus." His top choice,
the overhead dumbbell extension, is named with an explicit risk disclosure: `[10:10]` "you're
probably going to whack yourself in the back of the head with the dumbbell, and it's a little rough
on the elbows." On floor-based skull crushers (common among powerlifters), he states a direct,
disclosed reason for avoiding them: `[26:35]` "the reason that I don't do these very often is
because of my weak right tricep, because of the tear — it just never healed 100%. I'd probably say
this arm is at like 75 to 80% of what it was from a tricep perspective."

## The JM press: an independently rediscovered variation (14:15–15:16)

Seth describes arriving at a named technique without initially knowing its name: `[14:15]` "I
started doing close-grip bench higher and higher... then I found out someone [was] saying, oh,
you're doing JM presses... it was a variation where I was bringing the close-grip bench down to my
teeth... I kind of thought I was groundbreaking when I started doing [it]. No — somebody else
already did it."

## Visual self-monitoring as a stated training tool (17:18–18:20)

Seth states directly why he trains in minimal clothing, distinct from simple vanity: `[17:18]` "I've
always been such a firm believer in making sure you're wearing like a cutoff... so that you can see
your arms in a workout... it wasn't for everyone else in the gym, it was for me to be excited about
what I'm building." He frames this as continuous technique feedback: `[18:20]` "the whole time I'm
working out, I'm watching myself going, all right, this is doing a good job... if I tried this, how's
it going to affect it?"

## Form cues and body signals addressed

- **A sub-90° range of motion on hammer curls is the specific mechanism credited for arm growth**,
  attributed to observing Phil Heath: `[20:22]`.
- **Grip position along the dumbbell is restated as testable and non-trivial**, on both hammer
  curls and incline curls: `[32:22]`, `[27:37]`.
- **A permanently reduced-capacity tricep (post-tear, ~75-80%) is a stated reason for avoiding a
  specific exercise variation (floor skull crushers)**, not a general caution against the movement
  category: `[26:35]`.
- **Feeling a lift shift into the front delt is the stated stop signal on incline curls**, not a
  rep count: `[25:33]`.
- **A 4-to-8-week window is named as the standard for judging whether a change is actually
  working**: `[42:30]` "I always said like four to eight weeks should give you a solid
  justification if something works or not."

## Glossary terms introduced

- **JM press** — a close-grip bench press variation brought down near the chin/teeth rather than
  the chest, discovered independently by Seth before he learned its established name `[14:15]`.
- **"Mandatory" skull-crusher/close-grip-bench pairing** — Seth and Tai's shared rule that
  cambered-bar skull crushers should always be immediately followed by a close-grip bench burnout,
  not performed alone `[12:13]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"A New York strip steak"** `[10:10]` — Seth's image for the thick, slab-like look of a
  well-developed long head, used as the visual goal for his favorite tricep exercise.

## Source-stated confidence

Both lifters are explicit that their rankings reflect personal experimentation rather than a
claimed universal best-exercise list, and Seth discloses a specific permanent physical limitation
rather than omitting it to keep his exercise endorsement clean:

- **The whole "top 5" format is framed as personal, not prescriptive**: `[04:03]` "that's the
  biggest difference for everybody — what works for you best."
- **The tricep-tear limitation is disclosed with a specific self-estimated capacity (75-80%)**,
  not glossed over: `[26:35]`.
- **The Phil Heath technique is credited by direct observation, not verified against outside
  study**: `[20:22]` "I started doing them that way just cuz I saw it."
