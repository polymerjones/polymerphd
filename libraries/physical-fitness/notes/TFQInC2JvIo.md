# LEG DAY & POSING W/ HANY RAMBOD

## Central claim

**Hany Rambod states that "fatigue" and "failure" are frequently conflated, and that bodybuilding
coaching requires explicitly defined vocabulary — "pushing," "full," "failure" — because without
shared definitions, a coach and athlete can't actually communicate about what a set felt like.**
`[28:50]`–`[29:51]` "fatigue and failure are not the same thing... something that needs to be defined
definitively within the bodybuilding world, because it's like, oh, I can go to failure at 15 or 16
reps, and it might be to a certain extent, but in a growing phase, building dense muscle, failure
[and] fatigue [are] two different things." He illustrates with a concrete example: `[29:51]` "I had
that conversation with Derek Lunsford... I said, do you feel like you're pushing? And he's like, I
don't know what that means... a pro, [who had] won pro shows."

## Time under tension and controlled eccentrics (10:20–13:23)

Hany repeatedly corrects a specific pattern — releasing tension too quickly at the top of a rep:
`[10:20]` "you're going through the motion... this isn't a pump rep... go up, come down twice as slow...
see how you're shaking? It's because you're used to just dropping and releasing tension." He
locates the specific point in the rep where this matters most: `[13:23]` "it's not the area at the
very top where you lock your knee out, it's that area about 20% further down that you want to kind
of release the tension... slow that area down." Later he attributes an entire training gap to
under-loading: `[24:43]` "I think that's one of the reasons I probably haven't been keeping as much
time under tension... the weight might have been too light... got a little too easy to move, and
therefore just kind of going through the motions."

## Toe position on leg extensions (10:20–11:21)

A specific, testable variable: `[11:21]` "point your toe as much as you can... you're going to feel
a little bit more in your upper thigh" — contrasted with the neutral/turned-in foot position used
earlier in the set.

## Training frequency has changed with age (13:23–15:26)

Hany states a direct, career-spanning change in his programming philosophy: `[13:23]`–`[14:25]`
"one thing that has transformed in the last 15 years is a bit more rest... before, we would be
banging six days a week, one day off, six days on. Now it's much more... three to four [days] with
one off... not always having the same body part on the same days of the week... but still making
sure that... the weaker body parts that need to grow the most [are trained] in the beginning of the
week... after a rest day." He explicitly ties this to age-based recovery differences: `[15:26]`
"it really is also going to depend on your recovery, especially when you're 40 versus when you're
25."

## Occlusion/"blood-starving" training: an explicit limiting caution (38:09–39:09)

Building on the occlusion concept documented elsewhere in this library, Hany adds a caution not
stated in that earlier note: `[38:09]`–`[39:09]` "we were doing blood-starving sets back in the
day... that's when we would drive extra volume... releases a lot of metabolites that really help
with growth... [but] I don't utilize those as often, because it's one of those things you really
want to be able to get [ready] for... he would be dead, and then he would be messed up for the next
3 or 4 days, and it wouldn't be really fruitful for the rest of the week." He states it was reserved
specifically for lagging body parts: `[39:09]` "that's why we did it for the weaker body parts... we
would use that technique... and it works really, really well" — but not as continuous, everyday
training.

## The "old muscle car" analogy for training with age (11:21–12:22)

Seth's own framing, endorsed by Hany as widely applicable: `[11:21]`–`[12:22]` "you're like an old
muscle car... you can still bang, you can still rip, you still go to the track, but after every
lap... you got to pull over, check the dipstick... because out of the blue you can blow a head
gasket... even if you do do those things, they can still happen, so you got to be mindful of it
all."

## Form cues and body signals addressed

- **Shaking during a controlled-tempo rep is named as evidence the muscle, not momentum, is doing
  the work** — the intended response, not a warning sign: `[10:20]`.
- **Toe position on leg extensions is a stated, testable lever for upper-thigh emphasis**:
  `[11:21]`.
- **Training frequency is explicitly reduced with age**, restructured to protect recovery capacity
  rather than maximize volume: `[13:23]`–`[15:26]`.
- **Occlusion/blood-starving training is explicitly time-limited and body-part-specific**, not a
  general everyday technique, due to its multi-day recovery cost: `[38:09]`–`[39:09]`.
- **A high-fat/dairy-heavy cheat meal is named as carrying a multi-day digestive and performance
  cost**, similar to the hangover-and-dehydration effect of alcohol: `[34:56]` "you're not only going
  to pay the price... in bloating... but you're going to end up causing yourself a lot of harm in
  your digestive tract for a day or two."

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Pushing** — Hany's term for genuine effort intensity during a set, distinct from simply
  completing reps; explicitly flagged as a concept some athletes (even pros) don't initially
  understand `[29:51]`.
- **Fatigue vs. failure** — restated and sharpened from `w41YZ6czsZY`: fatigue is just tiredness
  from continuing reps; failure/true effort is the actual growth-producing state `[28:50]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Old muscle car"** `[11:21]`–`[12:22]` — Seth's own analogy, explicitly praised by Hany, for
  training hard at an older age while building in regular maintenance (soft tissue work, check-ins)
  rather than training as if nothing has changed.

## Source-stated confidence

Hany is explicit that his own coaching philosophy has changed over 15+ years of practice, and
frames current recommendations as evolved rather than always having been correct:

- **The training-frequency change is stated directly as a revision to his own past methods**, not
  a claim that the old approach was always wrong for its time: `[13:23]` "one thing that has
  transformed in the last 15 years is a bit more rest."
- **The fatigue/failure vocabulary point is stated as something "needs to be defined"** within the
  sport generally, an ongoing communication gap rather than a solved problem: `[28:50]`.

## Conflicts with other sources

Against `w41YZ6czsZY`'s fatigue-vs-failure framework (Hany's own "pencil vs. hammer" analogy), this
note reinforces and sharpens the same distinction with a concrete coaching anecdote (Derek
