# Tips for Growing Bigger Arms | Adding Intensity

## Central claim

**Seth structures arm training around getting pumped before anything "counts," then layering
named intensifiers (drop sets, giant sets, strip sets) onto a normal set rather than chasing raw
volume.** `[03:01]` "I like to be pumped and ready like that way whenever I'm doing an exercise
like the very first exercise it counts... so that way the the warmup might be 10 minutes or 15
minutes but then all of a sudden once I get started every single rep counts." He frames this as
the reason "top set" language makes sense to him at all: `[04:02]` "there's no more warm-ups
there might be like a warm-up set that let your body acclimate to like an exercise or a feeling
but then after that it counts."

## Warm-up: cables over dumbbells for constant tension (05:03–06:03)

He states a specific, named reason for choosing cables to warm up biceps rather than dumbbells:
`[05:03]` "I like doing the cables as a warm-up simply because it's constant tension just to
move blood... I prefer these over dumbbells just because of the constant tension and lightweight
doesn't beat [dumbbells]." Restated as a direct comparison: `[06:03]` "I don't like doing
dumbbells as your warmup just because I've noticed that I push way more blood by doing the
cable... I can do three sets here and like I'm like ooh there's the blood, there's the blood, I
already feel it flowing."

## Push vs. press: elbow placement for tricep head emphasis (07:04–09:09)

Seth gives his own working vocabulary for two tricep push-down variants, distinguished by elbow
position rather than by machine: `[07:04]` "I call this a push down and this a press down... in
my head and in my notebooks and stuff and all documentation, push press." Flaring the elbows out
shifts emphasis toward the long head: `[08:06]` "if you wanted to, you could flare your elbows
out even more this way... and then push your elbows out even more, there you go, get down and
hit the... long head." Keeping the elbows tucked and leaning in shifts toward a press: `[08:06]`
"on this I would focus on flaring everything out so your long head[,] medial head would get hit
more, and then to do the press you can just stay this way." He ties the same elbow-flare logic to
visible physique outcomes: `[09:09]` "well yeah that's that's like that's what I was saying, some
of the pictures and images of you, a lot of times your shoulders are like cannonballs on bowling
balls."

## Intensifiers: what the word means to him (16:35–17:37)

He defines "intensifier" narrowly, distinct from a separate named program: `[16:35]` "whenever I
talk about intensifiers in workout videos... it's an intensifier, I like to add, that's all that
was, we weren't doing anything different other than saying the last four five six reps or last
set let's do a drop set." The category is a toolbox, not one technique: `[17:37]` "everybody's
like oh dude I did four sets of 10... it's like I was a volume guy but like within that volume I
used a lot of intensifiers, drop sets, strip sets, giant sets, anything to add [intensity] to
it." He is explicit that this is preference, not a claimed study result: `[17:37]` "it might not
be science based but it's fun."

## Blood-flow technique: the "spin-out" stretch, and an occlusion-training aside (21:46–23:48)

Between sets he spins the dumbbells out to stretch the biceps, crediting the technique to
bodybuilding history rather than claiming it as his own invention: `[21:46]` "that's an old
Arnold Pumping Iron thing... all it was is like you'll see me stretch out my biceps, [take] a
break between a drop set — it's all it's doing is allowing more blood to flow." He then contrasts
this with the opposite approach, occlusion/"blood starving," and is careful to frame the
mechanism as something he's repeating rather than something he measured: `[22:47]` "in the
opposite regard of that would be like a blood starving set or the occlusion training, so you're
not allowing the blood to flow to that area... that's what [people were] doing years and years
ago before occlusion training was even like a thing," describing feet held elevated between leg
press sets as the analogous move. The physiological claim is stated but explicitly hedged as
received wisdom, not his own finding: `[23:48]` "that occlusion and blood starving stuff is
supposed to help release more... igf-1 throughout the body, more growth hormone... there's
science behind it, but what also — it's not almost that, it's like a wild look occurs."

## Mind-muscle connection and the post-set squeeze (29:04–31:07)

Squeezing at the end of a set is framed as functional, not cosmetic: `[29:04]` "whenever you're
doing these at the end or at any time, it's always good to get a couple squeezes in there, it's
just going to help with that mind and muscle connection." On biceps specifically he calls the
connection mandatory rather than optional: `[30:06]` "on biceps you have to make that connection,
have to, in my opinion... you can actually, in an exercise, put your hand on it and feel it
working."

## Deliberately reordering exercises: preacher curls first (31:07–32:08)

He describes trying preacher curls as the first exercise — against the usual convention — as a
specific, dated experiment rather than a rule he started with: `[31:07]` "whenever you're
building a workout... people always put preachers, you rarely see them at the beginning of a
workout. I was always like, I don't know why... and then one day I was like, I'm going to just do
it a little heavier, and I was like, holy, these feel great." On hammer curls, the stated focus
narrows to two structures rather than "biceps" broadly: `[32:08]` "whenever you're doing hammers,
I don't focus on anything else but overall bicep thickness and brachialis."

## Three tricep heads, and why the medial head gets singled out (34:09–35:10)

He names all three heads of the triceps as targets of any pressing/extension movement, then picks
out one as the visually decisive head on an overhead extension: `[34:09]` "there's three heads to
my tricep, and of course I'm working all three when I'm doing a tricep exercise, but what's the
focus... whenever you're doing overhead extensions, bro, one thing that pops out like crazy is
your medial head... if you ever see a dude that has like a crazy thick medial head, that's like
when he poses you're like, oh bro, that thing would just pop out."

## Form cues and body signals addressed

- **Pump as a gate before a rep "counts"**: `[03:01]`–`[04:02]` warm-up sets are explicitly not
  counted until a pump is established; after that point, "it counts, it counts, it counts."
- **Cable constant-tension over dumbbell warm-up**: `[05:03]`–`[06:03]` chosen specifically for
  perceived blood flow, not load.
- **Elbow position as the checked variable on push-downs**: `[08:06]` flared elbows for long
  head, tucked/leaned-in for a more press-like emphasis — the same cable stack, two different
  named movements.
- **Stability/isolation on hammer curls narrowed to two structures**: `[32:08]` "overall bicep
  thickness and brachialis," nothing else.
- **Post-set squeeze as a checked connection, not a flourish**: `[29:04]`–`[30:06]` a hand placed
  on the muscle to confirm it is "working."

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Push down / press down** — Seth's own working vocabulary (his words: "in my head and in my
  notebooks") for two elbow-flare variants of a cable tricep extension, distinguished by which
  head gets emphasized `[07:04]`.
- **Intensifier** — his umbrella term for drop sets, giant sets, and strip sets added within a
  volume-focused set rather than a separate training system `[16:35]`.
- **Blood starving** — his term for the pre-occlusion-training practice of keeping blood from
  flowing to a muscle between sets (e.g., feet elevated on a leg press) `[22:47]`.

## Analogies worth reusing

- **"Cannonballs on bowling balls"** `[09:09]` — describing the rounded shoulder look that
  results from the flared-elbow press variant.
- **"Old Arnold Pumping Iron thing"** `[21:46]` — crediting the between-set dumbbell spin-out
  stretch to bodybuilding history rather than presenting it as original technique.

## Source-stated confidence

This is an informal, conversational training video, not a studied protocol, and Seth repeatedly
marks his own claims as preference or received wisdom rather than tested fact:

- **Intensifiers are explicitly not claimed as evidence-based**: `[17:37]` "it might not be
  science based, but it's fun."
- **The occlusion/"blood starving" growth-hormone/IGF-1 claim is hedged as something he's
  repeating, not something he measured**: `[23:48]` "there's science behind it, but..." — the
  claim itself (more IGF-1, more growth hormone) is stated, but its evidentiary basis is
  attributed elsewhere, not to this video.
- **The reordered-exercise findings (preacher curls first) are framed as one lifter's personal
  experiment on one occasion**: `[31:07]` "one day I was like... these feel great" — not
  generalized as a rule for other lifters.

## Conflicts with other sources

Against Body Beast's `bulk-arms`: both sources use drop sets on arm exercises, but the framing
differs. `bulk-arms`[16:10] treats the drop set as one fixed step inside Sagi Kalev's named
"dynamic set training" system (progressive set, force set, single set with drop set — each
exercise assigned its set type in advance). This video instead treats drop sets, giant sets, and
strip sets as an interchangeable "intensifier" toolbox added by feel within a given set,
explicitly not tied to a fixed program (`[17:37]`). Both agree drop sets work well on isolation
arm movements — no disagreement there, just a systematic-program framing versus an ad hoc one.

On hammer curls, `bulk-arms`[22:10] runs them one arm at a time as a "concentrated" progressive
set; this video runs them bilaterally as a heavier, blood-flow-first exercise placed early in the
session `[06:03]`. Both converge on hammer curls as a thickness/brachialis-focused movement,
differing only in unilateral-vs-bilateral execution and where in the session it's placed.

No existing physical-fitness note addresses occlusion training or a "blood starving" technique —
this is a new topic for the library, and its confidence framing (hedged, "there's science behind
it but...") should travel with it if referenced elsewhere.
