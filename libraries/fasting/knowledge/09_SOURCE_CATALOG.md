## 1. Fasting Can Save Your Life By Herbert M. Shelton

- **Source ID:** `fasting-can-save-your-life-by-herbert-m-shelton`
- **Author:** Herbert M. Shelton
- **Source file:** `sources/clean/fasting-can-save-your-life-by-herbert-m-shelton.txt`
- **Main subjects:** a book-length argument for supervised, water-only therapeutic fasting as the central practice of "Natural Hygiene," a health movement Shelton helped organize through the American Natural Hygiene Society (founded 1948, later renamed the National Health Association), a sharp terminological distinction between "fasting" (abstinence while the body still has adequate nutritive reserves) and "starving" (abstinence after those reserves are exhausted), used throughout to argue fasting itself cannot cause death, a chapter-by-chapter survey of specific diseases and conditions Shelton claims to have observed improve or resolve under fasting, based on his own clinical experience supervising what the book's introduction states as over 40,000 fasts, an explicit rejection of the word "cure" — Shelton argues fasting does not cure anything; it is physiological rest that removes obstruction and lets the body's own self-healing processes work, repeated, emphatic insistence that fasting (beyond a very short duration) must be supervised by an experienced practitioner, not self-directed, a shared causal framework ("toxemia" and "enervation") applied across roughly 20 named diseases and conditions — the common-cold, multiple sclerosis, asthma, arthritis, peptic ulcer, migraine, hay fever, hypertension, heart disease, colitis, psoriasis/eczema, prostatic enlargement, gonorrhea, Parkinson's disease, nephritis, gallstones, breast tumors, infertility, and pregnancy nausea are each explained as local expressions of the same underlying systemic cause, not as biologically distinct diseases, an explicit and important distinction, in the breast-tumor chapter, between benign lumps Shelton believes were misdiagnosed as cancer and "undoubted cancer," which he states plainly he has never seen recover under fasting or any other care `[¶1512]`
- **Fasting protocols:** water-only fasting, explicitly distinguished from "juice fasting" or "fruit fasting," which the book calls a "feast" rather than a fast in the relevant physiological sense `[¶358]`, duration set by observed day-to-day developments in the faster rather than a fixed schedule; the book states Shelton personally supervised fasts up to 90 days, with several patients passing 70 days `[¶660]`–`[¶661]`, institutional/supervised fasting recommended over home fasting, citing family interference and lack of controlled conditions as the main practical obstacles to fasting at home `[¶646]`–`[¶654]`, weight loss under fasting cited at roughly 2.5–2.7 lbs/day on average, based on Shelton's own experience and two named physician-run hospital studies (Lyon Bloom, M.D., Piedmont Hospital, Atlanta; Garfield Duncan, M.D., University of Pennsylvania) `[¶444]`–`[¶448]`, {'a specific whole-fruit refeeding protocol for breaking a 14+ day fast': '4 oz whole orange every 2 hours on day one, doubling on day two, gradually introducing salads and small amounts of protein by day five or six `[¶758]`–`[¶764]`'}, multiple sequential fasts (Shelton describes cases using two, three, or as many as five) recommended over one very long fast for long-developed chronic conditions like arthritis and Parkinson's disease `[¶947]`, `[¶1451]`
- **Concerns & contraindications:** {'explicit contraindications named in this section of the book': 'advanced heart disease, cancer, diabetes, advanced tuberculosis, cancer of the liver or pancreas, and states of great emaciation `[¶642]`'}, {'pregnancy and nursing': 'fasting advised against except for urgent necessity, though a short fast for morning sickness is called beneficial `[¶643]`–`[¶644]`'}, self-directed (unsupervised) fasting repeatedly discouraged as dangerous not because fasting itself is unsafe, but because an untrained person cannot recognize when the fasting stage has ended and the starvation stage has begun `[¶656]`–`[¶662]`, one named fatality (Gloria Lee Byrd, 1962) is discussed at length as a starvation death, with Shelton arguing it does not demonstrate fasting itself is dangerous `[¶585]`–`[¶592]`
- **Important concepts:** autolysis — the enzymatic self-digestion of the body's own reserve and "less essential" tissues to nourish vital tissues during a fast, offered as the physiological mechanism sustaining a faster `[¶479]`, `[¶531]`, the "hunger versus false appetite" distinction — genuine hunger described as a mild, pleasant, intermittent sensation, contrasted with the gnawing, painful "false appetite" the book attributes to habit and withdrawal from stimulating foods `[¶497]`–`[¶515]`, the term "physiological compensation" — energy conserved by suspending digestion becomes available to other body systems, illustrated with a bathtub-faucet analogy `[¶521]`–`[¶523]`, the claim that tissue breakdown during a fast proceeds in "inverse order of importance," sacrificing fat and non-essential tissue before vital organs `[¶482]`–`[¶483]`, {'the paired terms "toxemia" and "enervation" — the book\'s central, catch-all disease mechanism': 'nerve-energy depletion from habitual overeating/stimulants ("enervation") checks the body\'s elimination of waste, which accumulates in blood and tissue ("toxemia") and is offered as the shared root cause of roughly 20 named diseases covered chapter by chapter `[¶776]`, `[¶1054]`'}, a proposed universal disease-progression sequence — irritation, inflammation, induration (hardening), ulceration, and, in the worst case, cancer — applied across peptic ulcer, colitis, and other chronic inflammatory conditions as one continuous process rather than separate diseases `[¶1130]`–`[¶1139]`
