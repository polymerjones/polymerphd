# Combined Glossary

## Nutrition

# 07 — Plain English Glossary

Every technical term introduced across this knowledge package, defined the way the source
material defines it. Terms are alphabetical. Where a source hedges its own claim about a term
("maybe," "said to," "described as"), that hedge is kept rather than smoothed away.

If a term appears in another file and you want the fuller treatment, `03_CONTAMINANTS_AND_MECHANISMS.md`
carries the extended explanations.

## A

**A1/A2 casein** — two variants of a milk protein found in cow's milk. A1 is described as more
likely to cause immune and digestive problems than A2.

**Acetaldehyde** — the toxic byproduct the body makes when it breaks down alcohol; a driver of
hangover inflammation.

**Acrolein** — an irritating compound formed when cooking oil is heated to high temperatures. The
amount produced by a single large fast-food fried serving has been compared, for that one
compound, to the amount in a pack of cigarettes.

**Adjuvant** — an additive mixed into a pesticide or herbicide product alongside its declared
active ingredient (for example, in Roundup). These additives can make the full formulation test as
more toxic than the active ingredient does on its own.

**Advanced glycation end products (AGEs)** — compounds formed when sugar molecules bind
permanently to proteins in the body, generating free radicals in the process. Considered one
contributor to tissue aging.

**Aflatoxin** — a toxin produced by mold that can contaminate grain-based animal feed, such as
peanut meal, and pass into the meat of animals raised on it.

**Aggregate nutrient density index (ANDI)** — a scoring system ranking foods by nutrients per
calorie, criticized for ignoring how much of a food a person would realistically eat, its water
content, and how well the body actually absorbs its nutrients.

**Air drying** (as a meat-preservation method) — preserving meat using controlled airflow alone,
distinct from curing (which relies on salt) or cooking. Said to retain enzymes that stabilize the
meat without the heavy salt loads or chemical preservatives curing requires.

**Alberger process** — an evaporative salt-making process that produces the distinctively shaped,
flaky grains of Diamond Crystal salt.

**Alpha-gal (galactose alpha-1,3-galactose)** — a sugar molecule present in the tissue of most
mammals but not in humans. A tick bite can introduce it into the human bloodstream, triggering the
allergic sensitization to red meat at the center of this syndrome.

**Anhedonia** — a reduced ability to feel pleasure; one of the symptoms of nicotine withdrawal.

**Anticuchos** — a cumin, chili, garlic, and oregano marinated preparation of grilled or pan-fried
beef heart.

**Antimony** — a heavy metal that can migrate out of polyethylene plastic containers into the
vegetable oil stored inside them.

**ApoB-containing lipoproteins** — the broader family of cholesterol-carrying particles in the
blood, including LDL, VLDL, and chylomicrons; argued not to be, by itself, the cause of
atherosclerosis.

**Astaxanthin** — the carotenoid pigment that wild salmon accumulate from eating krill, giving
their flesh its color. Farmed salmon are typically fed a synthetic version of the same pigment
instead.

**Autolyzed yeast extract** — a food ingredient that can supply a form of the flavor-enhancing
compound found in MSG without disclosing it as MSG.

## B

**Benthic organisms** — bottom-dwelling shellfish, such as mussels, clams, and oysters, that can
accumulate heavy metals like cadmium from their environment.

**Bioaccumulation** — the buildup of a substance, such as a pesticide, as it moves through a food
chain — for example, concentrating from a chicken's feed into the eggs she produces.

**BMAA (beta methyl amino alanine)** — a non-protein amino acid; one of several cyanotoxins tested
for in a cited study of algae-based supplements.

**BZRP (benzodiazepine receptor protein) and CYP19** — two gene-expression targets found, in one
cited study, to be altered by exposure to fluorochemicals, linked to lower androgen and higher
estrogen production.

---

## C

**Canola** — the acronym "Canadian Oil, Low Acid," naming a 1980 genetically modified version of
rapeseed bred to contain much less erucic acid than the unmodified plant.

**Carbon-negative** — describing a farm that stores more carbon in its soil than its production
emits; used to distinguish regeneratively raised beef from both conventional beef and plant-based
meat alternatives.

**Carrageenan** — a compound extracted from algae, added as a thickener to some oat and almond
milks, and linked to gut irritation.

**Caseros** (chickens) — a farmers-market term for a chicken breed that tolerates heat well and
stays out of the coop foraging more than typical commercial breeds.

**Certificate of analysis** — a laboratory document reporting the measured composition and
contaminant levels of a food or water sample.

**Chlor Alkali procedure** — a step once used in refining high-fructose corn syrup, tied
historically to mercury contamination; industry sources say it is no longer in use.

**Choline** — a nutrient concentrated in egg yolk, described as necessary for brain development in
both infants and adults; most people are said to fall short of getting enough.

**Citrate** (as a mineral supplement form) — potassium, magnesium, or calcium bound specifically to
citrate rather than another compound, chosen because citrate binds onto oxalate crystals and
weakens the calcium bonds holding them together.

**Citrate synthase** — an enzyme said to be overproduced by cancer cells alongside another enzyme,
fatty acid synthase; cited as a reason dietary citric acid has been framed as potentially feeding
tumor growth.

**CLA (conjugated linoleic acid)** — a compound in butter associated with leanness and improved
weight loss.

**Congeners** — fermentation byproducts found in alcoholic drinks, lower in clear spirits than in
dark ones. Not the cause of a hangover, but a factor that can make one worse.

**Conservation Reserve Program (CRP)** — a US government program that pays farmers to leave land
fallow, described as an untapped resource for scaling up regenerative grazing.

---

## D

**Dacthal (DCPA)** — a weed-killer whose residue can remain on kale and other leafy greens,
acknowledged by the EPA to disrupt thyroid function.

**DATEM** — a common additive found across several packaged and fast foods.

**Delta K** — a figure on an olive oil's certificate of analysis; an elevated value is said to
suggest possible adulteration, such as cutting the oil with a cheaper seed oil.

**Dimethyl polysiloxane** — a silicone compound also used in Silly Putty, added to deep-fryer oil
as an anti-foaming agent and found in some breaded and fried fast foods.

**Dry-aging** — a temperature-, airflow-, and humidity-controlled process that concentrates flavor
and tenderizes meat through natural enzyme activity, distinguished from uncontrolled spoilage.

**Dysbiosis** — an unbalanced population of bacteria in the gut.

---

## E

**Eicosanoids** — signaling molecules the body makes from omega-3 and omega-6 fats. The two fat
types produce different eicosanoids, which is the conventional basis for treating omega-3s as
anti-inflammatory and omega-6s as inflammatory.

**Endocannabinoids (AEA, 2-AG)** — the body's own molecules that activate the CB1 receptor (the
same receptor cannabis acts on), made from arachidonic acid, which is itself derived from linoleic
acid. Linked to appetite stimulation.

**Erucic acid** — a monounsaturated fat present in unmodified rapeseed oil, described as toxic and
damaging to the heart.

**Estrogen receptor alpha/beta expression ratio** — the balance between two types of estrogen
receptor in the body; glyphosate exposure is said to shift this ratio.

**Eutrophication** — pollution of ocean water caused by the concentrated waste output of open-net
salmon farming.

**Excipient** — an "inactive" ingredient — such as silicon dioxide, titanium dioxide, talc, or
citric acid — added to a supplement or medication beyond its active ingredient.

**Exclusion zone** (or "fourth phase" water) — a more ordered, gel-like, hexagonally structured
layer of water that researcher Gerald Pollack found forms near a gel membrane interface, distinct
from the structuring claimed by commercial "structured water" devices.

---

## F

**Ferroptosis** — a form of programmed cell death driven by lipid-peroxide-induced oxidative
stress, distinguished from the more familiar process of apoptosis.

**Fluorosilicic acid** — an industrial byproduct of phosphate fertilizer and metal production that
is added to municipal drinking water as its fluoride source.

**Fluorosis** — white-to-brown dental staining or pitting caused by excess fluoride exposure,
distinguished from a cavity.

**Freebase nicotine** — nicotine chemically converted, using compounds like diammonium phosphate,
into a form that crosses into the brain faster than the nicotine salts naturally present in
tobacco.

**Fructosamine assay** — a blood test measuring glucose, galactose, or fructose bound to the
protein albumin, contrasted with the hemoglobin A1c test, which measures the same sugars bound to
hemoglobin instead.

**Full-animal-trim ground beef** — ground beef made from the whole carcass rather than from
leftover trim after premium cuts are removed, offered as more representative of the flavor of the
whole animal.

---

## G

**Galpin equation** — a rule of thumb for exercise hydration: body weight in pounds divided by 30
equals the ounces of fluid to drink per 15–20 minutes of exercise.

**Generational education** — the passing down of uncritically inherited textbook beliefs from
teacher to student across generations, even when the underlying claim was never re-examined.

**Genistein** — a polyphenol (also described as an isoflavonoid) found in soy, beans, and other
plant foods, whose effects on fertility and reproduction are described as compounding with the
estrogenic effects of glyphosate.

**Glucosinolates** — plant-made defense chemicals found in cruciferous vegetables like cabbage, one
class among dozens of natural pesticide-like compounds these plants produce.

**Goal body weight** — a person's target or ideal body weight, as distinct from their current
weight; used as the basis for protein-intake rules of thumb such as one gram of protein per pound
of goal weight.

**Goitrin** — a specific isothiocyanate compound found in Brussels sprouts and chard, tied to
reduced iodine uptake at the thyroid.

**GPL-TOX** — a broader laboratory panel (from Great Plains Laboratory) that tests for multiple
pesticides and environmental contaminants beyond glyphosate alone.

**Greenwashing** — marketing language on an industrially produced food product that makes it sound
as if it was raised or grown using genuine regenerative or pasture-based practices when it was
not — for example, labeling grain-pellet-fed cattle as "grass-fed."

**Gross alpha / gross beta** — two radioactivity measures reported for mineral waters; gross beta
radiation is described as essentially electron emission.

---

## H

**Hatterism** — a term for the mental disturbance caused by mercury poisoning, referencing the
historical phrase "mad as a hatter."

**Healthy-user bias** — a confound in observational nutrition studies: people who adopt one
health-associated behavior (choosing organic food, using seed oils, and so on) also tend to do
many other healthy things at the same time, which makes it hard to isolate the effect of any single
factor.

**HFCS 42 / HFCS 55** — grades of high-fructose corn syrup named for their approximate fructose
percentage, both blended down from a much higher-fructose base stock (HFCS 90).

**HNE (4-hydroxynonenal)** — a toxic oxidation product formed specifically from linoleic acid as it
breaks down, shown in cell and animal studies to promote weight gain.

**Holistic management** — an approach to farming aimed at restarting the cycles of nature — water,
energy, microbial, grazing, and carbon — that industrial monocropping breaks.

**Hydroxyapatite** — the mineral that either calcium or fluoride can form within tooth enamel,
cited as the shared mechanism behind how each one helps resist cavities.

**Hydroxytyrosol** — a polyphenol found in olive oil, credited with gut-focused effects that its
own proponents repeatedly hedge as "maybe."

**Hypernatremia / hyponatremia** — the acute medical conditions of blood sodium being too high or
too low, respectively. Hyponatremia in particular can result from drinking large amounts of plain
water without matching electrolyte intake, especially during long endurance exercise.

---

## I

**"Ioso"** — a garbled term (likely a captioning error) used in one source for the differing
signaling byproducts that omega-3 and omega-6 fats each produce — the same idea more commonly
described by the term *eicosanoids*.

**Isothiocyanates** — a family of compounds found in cruciferous vegetables (kale, cauliflower,
Brussels sprouts, mustard, chard) that can interfere with the thyroid's ability to take up iodine.
One source referred to them by the shorthand "grogens," apparently meaning "goitrogens."

---

## K

**K232 / K270** — light-absorption measurements reported on an olive oil's certificate of analysis,
used to indicate the oil's degree of rancidity.

---

## L

**LDL oxidation susceptibility** — how readily a person's LDL cholesterol particles oxidize; used
as the outcome measure in a study linking the linoleic-acid content of eggs to cardiovascular risk.

**Life cycle assessment (LCA)** — a methodology for calculating the net carbon emissions produced
per pound of a food, such as beef, across its entire production process.

**Linoleic acid** — an 18-carbon polyunsaturated fat that makes up a small share of the fat in meat
(roughly 2%) but a much larger share of the fat in seed oils (25–65%). It is used repeatedly as a
chemical marker of what an animal was actually fed — for example, rising from around 2% in
traditionally raised pork to 15–20%+ in today's corn-and-soy-fed pork — and its level in human body
fat has climbed in step, from roughly 2–5% a few generations ago to over 20% today, tracking the
broader rise of seed oils in the diet.

**Lipopolysaccharide (endotoxin)** — a component of the outer wall of certain gut bacteria; when
gut bacteria overgrow, it can enter the bloodstream and trigger an inflammatory response.

---

## M

**Membrane pacemaker theory** (of aging) — the idea that the amount of polyunsaturated fat built
into a tissue's cell membranes influences how fast that tissue oxidizes and ages, and by extension
how long an organism lives.

**Mercury ball effect** — the visual cue of water beading and dancing across a stainless steel pan,
signaling the pan is hot enough to cook on without food sticking.

**Metabolic alkalosis / respiratory alkalosis** — two medical conditions caused by the blood
becoming too alkaline, named as the real risk of over-alkalinizing the body with very high-pH
water.

**Microcystins** — a class of toxins produced by algae ("plant toxins"), tested for in a study of
algae-based supplements.

**Microplastics as a "flux"** — the idea that the body doesn't simply accumulate microplastics
indefinitely, but continually absorbs some and clears others, so the total stays in a rough
balance rather than building up one-way.

**Millisievert (mSv)** — the unit used to measure radiation dose, allowing comparison between
bottled water, medical imaging, and everyday background exposure.

**Mitochondrial proton leak** — a proposed mechanism by which excess polyunsaturated fat
(particularly linoleic acid) built into cell membranes impairs the mitochondria's ability to
produce cellular energy efficiently.

**Monogastric** — having a single stomach, as pigs, chickens, and humans do, unlike ruminants such
as cattle. A monogastric animal cannot process dietary polyunsaturated fat the way a ruminant's
multi-chambered stomach can, so the fat accumulates directly in its body tissue instead.

**Mycorrhizal networks** — fungal networks in soil through which nutrients from grazing-animal
waste are taken up by regrowing pasture grass.

---

## N

**Nanoplastics versus microplastics** — nanoplastics are defined as roughly 50–500 nanometers
across, about 20 times smaller than a micrometer-sized microplastic.

**National Toxicology Program (NTP) report** — a federal report examining a possible link between
fluoride exposure and IQ, said to have been released only after a Freedom of Information Act
lawsuit.

**Natural flavors** (as a labeling loophole) — a catch-all ingredient category on food labels that
can legally include substances such as castoreum, ambergris, civet secretion, shellac, or
cochineal without disclosing them individually.

**Neonicotinoids ("neonics")** — systemic insecticides absorbed into a plant's own tissue from a
coated seed, which bees then pick up through pollen and nectar. Described as impairing bee gut
health and shortening bee lifespan even at low doses.

**Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor** — the brain receptor that nicotine binds to, named for the
historical use of nicotine in research even though its natural (endogenous) trigger is the
neurotransmitter acetylcholine, not nicotine itself.

---

## O

**O-ring test** — a muscle-strength test used to identify which foods or substances weaken a
person's grip strength when held near the body.

**Odd-chain fatty acids (C15, C17)** — pentadecanoic acid (C15) and heptadecanoic acid (C17), fats
found in full-fat dairy and tallow that serve as blood biomarkers associated with better health
outcomes in observational research. C15 in particular has also been described as protective
against ferroptosis.

**Odontoblast** — living cells within a tooth that help resist bacterial damage to the enamel when
adequately supplied with fat-soluble vitamins.

**Organic barcode prefix** — the leading digit "9" on a produce sticker's price-lookup code, which
distinguishes an organically grown item from its conventionally grown counterpart.

**Organic grass-fed** — a narrower label than "grass-fed" alone, additionally excluding GMO feed
and any grass or hay that was sprayed with glyphosate.

**Outside skirt** (diaphragm) — a cut of beef that is, anatomically, the diaphragm muscle,
distinguished from the "inside skirt" cut; a flavor difference between the two is attributed to
the outside skirt's greater oxygen exposure.

**Oxalate dumping** — a process, credited to researcher Susan Owens, in which the body actively
expels stored oxalate deposits once dietary oxalate intake drops, producing a temporary flare of
symptoms.

---

## P

**Pasture-raised** — a label meant to indicate an animal was raised on open pasture with meaningful
space per animal — for example, hundreds of square feet per chicken, versus roughly two square
feet under a "free-range" label, or warehouse confinement under "cage-free." For chickens and pigs
specifically, the label describes a management practice rather than a return to how those animals
actually evolved to live.

**Pepsin** — a digestive enzyme in the stomach, said to be denatured (deactivated) by very
high-pH water, such as some "alkaline" waters.

**Peroxidation index** — a measure of how readily a fat oxidizes when heated, offered as a more
relevant safety criterion for choosing a cooking oil than the oil's smoke point.

**Peroxide value** — a laboratory marker of oxidation, invoked to explain why oils lower in
saturated fat degrade faster under heat.

**PFAS ("forever chemicals")** — an umbrella term for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances
(including PFOA, PFOS, and PFHxS), found in products such as nonstick cookware, food packaging,
and dental floss. These compounds are persistent in both the environment and the body and are
considered hormone (endocrine) disruptors.

**Phthalates** — plasticizer chemicals, also found in some fragranced products like air fresheners,
that are drawn to fat ("lipophilic"). This is why fatty foods like cooking oil pick up more of them
from contact with plastic than water does. Considered endocrine disruptors.

**Phytic acid** — a compound in almonds and other seeds that inhibits digestive enzymes and also
binds ("chelates") minerals such as magnesium, reducing how much of them the body can absorb.

**Pineal gland calcification** — a bone-like mineral buildup that accumulates in the brain's pineal
gland with age, of undetermined pathological versus physiological significance.

**POEA** — an additive in Roundup herbicide formulations, found in several cited animal studies to
be more toxic in combination with glyphosate than glyphosate is on its own.

**Polyol pathway** — the body's own conversion of excess blood glucose into fructose, said to rise
sharply — from roughly 3% to roughly 30% — under insulin resistance.

**Propolis** — a resin bees collect from plants and trees and mix with honey and pollen to seal and
bind the hive. It can also carry whatever contaminants were present on the plants it was collected
from.

**Protein leverage hypothesis** — the idea that daily protein intake is one driver, though not the
only one, of satiety and hunger regulation.

**Protoporphyrin IX / oocyanin** — the two pigments responsible for brown and blue chicken eggshell
color respectively, offered to show that shell color is a genetic trait rather than a marker of egg
quality.

---

## R

**Raphide** — a needle-shaped calcium oxalate crystal that some plants deploy in bundles as a
chemical and physical defense against being eaten.

**Red Bro** (chicken breed) — a slower-growing chicken breed, typically harvested at 7–8 weeks,
contrasted with the fast-growing, leg-breaking Cornish Cross breed.

**Regenerative** (as an egg-carton label) — a term with no legal teeth, borrowed from
cattle-grazing language, that a producer can print without meeting any defined standard.

**"Replicatable," not "linear scalable"** — a distinction for how regenerative agriculture should
grow: by adding many smaller farms run the same way, rather than by scaling one operation up to
industrial size.

**Resilience versus efficiency** — a described tradeoff in farming systems, where industrial
agriculture maximizes efficiency at the cost of a system's ability to handle adversity and keep
going, while regenerative approaches favor resilience instead.

**Retained water** — a chicken-labeling term indicating the bird was chilled in a chlorinated water
bath during processing, leaving some of that water absorbed into the meat; air-chilling avoids the
water bath entirely.

**Rimonabant** — a drug that blocked the CB1 receptor, reducing obesity and improving
cardiometabolic markers in animal and human studies, but was withdrawn after being linked to
increased suicidal ideation.

**Royal jelly** — a secretion nurse bees feed briefly to all larvae, and continuously to future
queens.

---

## S

**Shikimate pathway** — described as a folate pathway present in plants and some gut bacteria that
the herbicide glyphosate is known to inhibit; proposed as a mechanism for gut-microbiome
disruption.

**Sodium silico aluminate** — an aluminum-derived compound identified as a component of a
fast-food restaurant's salt blend, distinct from plain sodium chloride (table salt).

**Specific gravity** (as a urine hydration marker) — a test-strip measurement of urine
concentration used to estimate hydration status; the exact threshold for "dehydrated" is disputed
across sources.

**Sweetbreads** — a culinary term specifically for the thymus gland, though some traditional
preparations combine it with other glands.

**Swill** (milk) — a historical term for the spent grain left over from alcohol fermentation, once
fed to unsanitarily kept urban dairy cows and cited as a cause of raw-milk-related sickness in the
early 1900s.

---

## T

**TBHQ** — a synthetic preservative that some retailers, such as Whole Foods, have declined to
stock products containing.

**Thallium** — a toxic heavy metal that leafy greens, kale in particular, can take up from
naturally contaminated soil.

**"The cholesterol hypothesis"** — shorthand for the mid-20th-century medical consensus that
blamed LDL cholesterol, and by extension saturated animal fat, for causing heart disease.

**"The Garden of Eden diet"** — a diet of fruit, nuts, and seeds that Seventh-day Adventist
doctrine frames as the diet for salvation, not as a health claim in itself.

**"The temple as the filter"** — a phrase contrasting two approaches to contaminated water:
filtering it before drinking, versus relying on the body to filter out contaminants after the
fact.

**Tolerable upper limit** — the maximum daily intake of a substance, such as lead, considered safe.
Different regulatory bodies can set very different numbers for the same substance — for example,
California's Prop 65 standard of 0.5 mcg/day for lead versus the FDA's 12 mcg/day.

**Torine (taurine)** — an amino acid associated with beneficial cardiovascular outcomes, found
concentrated roughly five-fold in beef cheek.

**Trans-vaccenic acid (TVA)** — a fatty acid found only in animal foods, cited to a study showing
potential anti-tumor immune reprogramming — a finding preserved here with its own source's hedge
that "we need to study this more."

**True Source certification** — a honey-authenticity label that tests beyond simple
point-of-origin claims, meant to verify that the honey inside a product is genuinely honey.

---

## V

**Vascular autonomic signal (VAS)** — a term for a detectable pulse change used in auricular (ear)
diagnosis, attributed to research from the 1960s.

**VLCFA (very long-chain fatty acids)** — saturated fats with unusually long carbon chains that
don't fit properly into a cell membrane, found overrepresented in brain tissue affected by
Alzheimer's disease.

---

## W

**Water-chilled versus air-chilled** (chicken) — two chicken-processing methods. Water-chilling
leaves the meat holding some retained chlorinated water, disclosed on the label as a percentage;
air-chilling skips the water bath entirely.

---

## X

**"X factor"** — a term for vitamin K2 as the key fat-soluble nutrient for dental health.

---

## Y

**Yerkes-Dodson law** — the principle that there is an optimal level of mental arousal for task
performance — too little or too much both hurt it — used to explain why nicotine might help
someone under-aroused (tired or withdrawing) but not someone already well-rested.

## Restorative Physiology

If a term appears in another file and you want the fuller treatment, `03_BIOLOGICAL_MECHANISMS.md` carries the extended explanations.

## A

**Acetylcholine** — the neurotransmitter that governs the brainstem circuits producing yawning, and that supports REM sleep and memory consolidation. Its decline across the lifespan is one mechanism behind reduced yawn frequency, lighter REM sleep and the autonomic changes of ageing. Anticholinergic medications block its receptors.

**Actin and myosin** — the two protein filaments that slide past each other to produce muscle contraction. Myosin performs the mechanical stroke; every stroke consumes ATP and releases heat.

**Action potential** — the electrical spike a nerve cell fires once its membrane voltage crosses about −55 mV. It is all-or-nothing: the signal either fires at full strength or not at all.

**Activation energy** — the energy barrier a chemical reaction must clear before it can proceed. Think of it as a hill every reaction has to climb. Raising temperature gives more molecules enough energy to clear it, which is why warmth accelerates chemistry.

**Adenosine** — the waste product left behind every time a brain cell burns ATP. It accumulates through the day, binds receptors that progressively slow neural activity, and produces what is felt as sleep pressure. It is cleared during deep sleep. Caffeine does not remove adenosine — it blocks the receptors so the accumulated adenosine cannot be read.

**Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)** — the universal energy currency of every cell. Breaking one of its phosphate bonds releases the energy that powers muscle contraction, nerve firing, protein synthesis and every other cellular process. The reaction is roughly 40% efficient; the remaining 60% becomes heat.

**Adiponectin** — a protective hormone released by fat cells that keeps muscle and liver sensitive to insulin. Small fat cells release it generously; as fat cells enlarge past a certain size they outgrow their own blood supply and produce steadily less of it, which is part of why weight gain itself makes insulin resistance worse over time.

**Adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine)** — the catecholamines released by the adrenal glands during acute stress. They raise heart rate, blood pressure and alertness. At extreme concentrations they become directly toxic to cardiac muscle.

**Advanced glycation end products (AGEs)** — permanent cross-links formed when glucose binds non-enzymatically to proteins. They stiffen ageing collagen, making arteries rigid, joints less pliable and the lens of the eye less clear. Once formed they are not easily removed.

**Afferent and efferent fibres** — afferent nerves carry information *from* the body *to* the brain; efferent nerves carry instructions *from* the brain *to* the body. Roughly 80% of the vagus nerve's fibres are afferent, which makes it primarily an information highway reporting on internal state.

**Allodynia** — pain produced by a stimulus that causes no tissue damage at all: a bedsheet's weight, a light touch, a breeze. It occurs when the pain-processing system's gain is turned up and stays up.

**Alpha waves** — brain oscillations at 8–13 Hz associated with calm alertness — aware but not anxious, attentive without effort. They increase near moving water, under dappled canopy light, and during repetitive prayer or chant.

**Alveoli** — the tiny air sacs where the lungs exchange gases with the blood, offering roughly 70 square metres of membrane.

**AMPK** — the cellular energy sensor that monitors the ratio of AMP to ATP. When energy is scarce it inhibits mTOR and initiates repair processes including autophagy. It also drives glucose transporters to the muscle cell surface independently of insulin.

**Amygdala** — the brain's threat-detection centre. It classifies incoming signals as safe or dangerous below conscious awareness and triggers the stress cascade accordingly. It is enlarged and more reactive in people with chronic trauma histories, and it does not distinguish between a real threat and a remembered or televised one.

**Anabolic resistance** — the age-related stiffening of the muscle growth switch, on both of its inputs at once. A serving of protein that turned the switch easily in a younger body produces a fraction of the response later in life, and an identical bout of resistance exercise yields a smaller building response in older muscle. Both keys still fit; each simply has to turn further before anything moves.

**Anterior cingulate cortex** — a region processing the emotional component of pain. It activates for physical injury and for social loss alike.

**Arrhenius equation** — describes how reaction rates and fluid properties change with temperature, exponentially rather than proportionally. It explains why synovial fluid thickens sharply in cold, why chemistry runs faster when warm, and why hypothermia both stops shivering and protects the brain.

**Articular cartilage** — the smooth resilient tissue capping bone ends inside joints. It has no blood supply and no nerve endings. It is fed entirely by fluid moving in and out under alternating compression, and it acts as insulation between mechanical events and the pain-sensitive bone beneath.

**Auricular branch (of the vagus)** — the only place the vagus nerve reaches the skin's surface, at a small shelf of ear cartilage called the cymba concha, just above the ear canal entrance. Gentle pressure there is a genuine, measurable route into the same brainstem circuit reached by breath and touch elsewhere in the body.

**Autophagy** — the cell's recycling programme. It identifies damaged components, engulfs them in a membrane sac, delivers them for breakdown and reuses the parts. It is suppressed while nutrients are abundant and released when they are not.

## B

**Baroreceptors** — pressure sensors embedded in the walls of the aortic arch and carotid arteries. They detect changes of less than 1% and adjust heart rate beat-by-beat. They cannot distinguish an internal pressure change from an external one, which is why atmospheric pressure shifts affect how you feel.

**Baroreflex** — the automatic circuit by which baroreceptor input slows the heart and lowers blood pressure. It is one of the fastest autonomic responses in the body, operating within a single cardiac cycle, and it cannot be overridden by conscious effort.

**BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor)** — the primary molecular driver of synaptic plasticity and the growth of new neurons in the hippocampus. Walking raises it. Ketones raise it. Injury raises it locally.

**Beau's line** — a transverse trench running across the whole width of the nail, stamped when a week of serious illness slows every production column at once. Because the nail grows out in strict order, the line rides slowly toward the tip over the following months, functioning as a dated marker of a bad week.

**Beta-hydroxybutyrate** — the main ketone body, produced by the liver once glycogen is depleted. It fuels the brain and simultaneously acts as a signalling molecule that opens chromatin and increases BDNF expression.

**Beta waves** — brain oscillations at 13–30 Hz, dominant during focused analytical work, worry and threat assessment.

**Bohr effect** — the finding that rising carbon dioxide makes haemoglobin release its oxygen more readily. Described by Christian Bohr in 1904, it is the continuous operating principle of oxygen delivery.

**Brown adipose tissue (brown fat)** — a specialised fat that burns fuel to produce heat rather than to produce usable energy. Packed with mitochondria, concentrated around the major vessels serving brain, heart and kidneys.

**Butyrate** — a short molecule produced when gut bacteria ferment dietary fibre. The cells lining the colon burn it for the majority of their energy — on the order of 70%. Those are the same cells that hold the intestinal barrier sealed, so the wall keeping bacteria out is fuelled by the bacteria it is keeping out.

---

## C

**Calcaneal fat pad** — chambers of fat separated by fibrous walls beneath the heel bone, distributing impact across the whole heel rather than letting it concentrate at a point. It thins with age and with insufficient loading.

**Calf muscle pump** — the calf muscles compressing the deep leg veins with each contraction, driving blood upward past one-way valves. Sometimes called the second heart. It is inactive when sitting or standing still.

**Cambium** — the thin ring of living tissue beneath tree bark that produces new wood and bark each year, indefinitely and without degradation.

**Capillary recruitment** — the opening of capillary beds that were closed at rest, increasing the surface area available for oxygen and nutrient exchange. It is why energy shifts a few minutes into walking.

**Carbonic anhydrase** — the enzyme inside red blood cells that converts carbon dioxide and water into carbonic acid, the first step in the chain that releases oxygen at the tissues.

**Catecholamines** — see *adrenaline and noradrenaline*.

**Cavitation limit** — the point at which water under tension tears apart and gas bubbles break the column. It caps the maximum height of trees at around 380 feet.

**Central sensitisation** — the state in which pain-processing neurons lower their activation thresholds, amplify incoming signals, and recruit neighbouring neurons that normally handle touch. The result is pain from harmless stimuli, with no remaining tissue injury.

**Cephalic phase** — the digestive preparation that begins before food arrives, triggered by the sight and smell of cooking. It releases 20–30% of gastric acid, primes insulin, and pre-releases bile.

**Cerebellum** — the brain region that integrates balance and position data and generates postural corrections. It calibrates on the complexity of the input it receives, which is why varied ground maintains it and flat ground degrades it.

**Cholecystokinin (CCK)** — a gut hormone released when fat arrives. It slows stomach emptying and suppresses the hormone that drives the fasting cleaning wave.

**Cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway** — a dedicated neural circuit, described by Kevin Tracey in 2002, through which the vagus nerve directly suppresses inflammatory cytokine production. It weakens when vagal tone is low, which is why chronic stress raises inflammation.

**Circadian rhythm** — the roughly 24-hour internal cycle governing alertness, hormone release, body temperature and repair. It is set by light and by the regularity of daily behaviour.

**Cones and rods** — the two photoreceptor types. Cones handle colour and detail in bright light and are largely preserved into the 70s and 80s. Rods handle dim light and are lost at roughly 0.2–0.4% per year after 40, mostly in the periphery.

**Cortisol** — the primary glucocorticoid stress hormone. In acute doses it mobilises energy and suppresses inflammation. Chronically elevated, it suppresses immune surveillance, accelerates collagen cross-linking, shortens telomeres, degrades muscle protein, impairs glucose uptake, damages the hippocampus and disrupts sleep.

**Cortisol awakening response** — the 50–100% surge in cortisol during the 30–45 minutes after waking. It is a normal and necessary signal, not a stress response.

**C-tactile afferents** — a second, slow, unmyelinated touch system discovered in 2002. It does not detect pressure or shape. It responds only to gentle, slow-moving contact at skin temperature, and it reports to the emotional brain rather than the sensory map. It is dense on the back, shoulders, arms and scalp, and sparse on the palms.

**Cytochrome c oxidase** — the final enzyme of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. It absorbs red and near-infrared light at 660 and 830 nm, and runs faster when that light reaches it.

**Cytokines** — signalling molecules of the immune system. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor alpha rise with chronic stress, grief, poor sleep and inactivity.

---

## D

**Default mode network** — the brain network that activates when no external task is running: replaying conversations, rehearsing tomorrow, revisiting old regrets. It consumes roughly 20% of the brain's energy budget, and it is displaced not by willpower but by giving attention something else to do.

**Denaturation** — the irreversible unfolding of a protein that destroys its function. In humans it begins between 40 and 45 °C.

**De novo mutations** — the 60–70 new genetic changes present in you and in neither parent, produced by copying errors when the egg and sperm were made.

**DNA methylation** — chemical tags attached to DNA that silence or quieten genes without altering the sequence. They are laid down by experience, including experiences before birth, and unlike mutations they can change again.

**Dorsal raphe nucleus** — the brainstem nucleus that produces serotonin for the forebrain. It is the target of SSRI medication, and it is also reached by vestibular input from rocking and by immune signalling from soil bacteria.

**Duchenne laughter** — genuine laughter that engages the muscles around the eyes and the full diaphragmatic contraction. Only this form produces the physiological reset; polite laughter does not.

---

## E

**Electron transport chain** — the sequence of four protein complexes in the mitochondrial membrane that pumps protons to create the gradient driving ATP production.

**Endothelium** — the single layer of cells lining every blood vessel. It produces nitric oxide in response to the friction of flowing blood, keeping vessels dilated and anti-inflammatory. Without flow, it switches to an inflammatory state within about 45 minutes.

**Endotoxin translocation** — the passage of bacterial outer-coat fragments from the inside of the gut into the bloodstream through a barrier that has become slightly permeable. Inside the gut the fragment is harmless and expected; in the blood it is read as proof of invasion, triggering inflammation with no live infection present.

**Entrainment** — the tendency of two oscillating systems sharing a connection to fall into the same rhythm. It applies to pendulum clocks on a shared beam, to two hearts in contact, to a choir breathing together, and to the brain locking onto a repeated rhythm.

**Epigenetics** — the layer of chemical marks sitting on top of DNA that determines which genes are readable. It is how identical genomes produce different outcomes, and how experience leaves a durable biological trace.

**Excitotoxicity** — the process by which glutamate, the brain's main excitatory signal, kills neurons when it accumulates because there is no energy left to clear it. Named by John Olney in 1969.

---

## F

**Fascia** — the connective sheets wrapping every muscle and organ. It requires mechanical pumping to stay hydrated and pliable; overnight stillness dehydrates it, which is part of morning stiffness.

**Fick's law** — diffusion proceeds down a concentration gradient at a rate proportional to the gradient, the surface area and the contact time.

**Focal adhesion kinase** — the enzyme docked at the inner end of each integrin. When the integrin physically bends under near-limit tension, this enzyme changes shape with it, switches on, and relays a chemical signal inward to the growth switch. It is the point where mechanical strain becomes a molecular instruction.

**Functional residual capacity** — the air remaining in the lungs at the end of a normal passive breath out.

---

## G

**Gait speed** — walking speed measured over four metres. It predicts survival more accurately than blood pressure, cholesterol or smoking history, because it summarises the integrated function of every system at once.

**Gate control theory** — Melzack and Wall, 1965. Pain signals pass through a checkpoint in the spinal cord where touch and pressure signals can compete with them and reduce what reaches the brain. It is why rubbing an injury helps.

**Geosmin** — the molecule that gives soil and rain their earthy smell, released when *Streptomyces* bacteria die. Humans detect it at five parts per trillion.

**Ghrelin and leptin** — the hormones signalling hunger and fullness. Sleep loss raises ghrelin and lowers leptin. Leptin is made by fat cells in proportion to their size and is read by the hypothalamus as a report of how full the body's fat stores are; years of elevated weight cause the hypothalamic receptors to grow less responsive to it (leptin resistance), so hunger can persist even when fat stores are objectively full.

**Glucocorticoid receptors** — the binding sites for cortisol, present in nearly every tissue. Under chronic elevation they downregulate, which is why long-term stress produces both low resting cortisol and exaggerated reactivity.

**Gluconeogenesis** — the manufacture of glucose from amino acids, drawing on muscle protein. Cortisol drives it.

**GLUT4** — the transporter that carries glucose from blood into muscle cells. Insulin normally summons it to the cell surface; muscle contraction and nitric oxide can do the same thing without insulin.

**Glymphatic system** — the brain's waste clearance network. During deep sleep the spaces between brain cells expand by roughly 60%, allowing fluid to flush out metabolic debris including amyloid beta.

**Golgi tendon organs** — sensors at the muscle-tendon junction reporting force and tension.

---

## H

**Haemoglobin** — the four-part protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. Loading oxygen and delivering it are separate problems; a full load means nothing if it is not released.

**Hallux valgus** — the medical name for the bump beside the big toe, from the Latin for "the big toe turning away from the midline." It describes a joint dislocated in angle, not new bone growth: the toe drifts toward its neighbours while the long bone behind it drifts the opposite way, exposing the joint head under the skin. Once the tendons' line of pull shifts with the joint, they begin actively pulling it further off course with every step.

**Heart rate variability (HRV)** — the beat-to-beat variation in the intervals between heartbeats. High variability indicates a flexible, well-regulated system; low variability indicates sympathetic dominance and predicts cardiovascular events.

**Heat shock proteins** — molecular chaperones that grab unfolding proteins and hold them in shape. They are triggered by fever, sauna and vigorous exercise, and they repair damage that had already accumulated.

**Henry's law** — the amount of gas dissolved in a liquid is proportional to the pressure above it. When pressure drops, dissolved gas comes out of solution — in a bottle of sparkling water, and in the fluid inside your joints.

**Hering-Breuer reflex** — lung inflation signalling the brainstem to delay the next breath, slowing the breathing rate.

**Hippocampus** — the structure that consolidates memory and tags experience with time and place. It carries the highest density of cortisol receptors in the brain and shrinks under chronic stress. Walking regrows it.

**Hormesis** — the principle that a small, controlled, recovered-from stress triggers a repair response that overshoots, leaving the system better off than before.

**HPA axis** — the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal cascade: the hypothalamus releases CRH, the pituitary releases ACTH, the adrenals release cortisol. Each step amplifies the last.

**Hypothalamus** — the brain region holding the body's set points for temperature, hunger, thirst, stress and circadian timing.

---

## I

**Imbibition** — the compression-and-release fluid pump that is the sole nutrition mechanism for cartilage and spinal discs. Both are avascular; movement feeds them and stillness starves them.

**Inflammaging** — the chronic low-grade inflammation that accumulates with age, characterised by a shift away from regulatory immune function.

**Insulin resistance** — the state in which cells respond less to insulin's instruction to take up glucose, so blood sugar stays elevated and insulin climbs to compensate.

**Integrins** — protein bolts threaded through the outer membrane of a muscle fibre, gripping the collagen scaffolding outside and the cell's internal skeleton inside. They cluster in bands repeating along the fibre's whole length, so the entire cell reads load as one distributed strain gauge. Gentle tension leaves them unchanged; tension near what the fibre can bear physically bends them, and the bend is the signal.

**Interstitial fluid** — the fluid in the spaces between cells, through which nutrients and waste move.

**Intrathoracic pressure** — the pressure inside the chest cavity. It rises with a weight on the chest, with a long exhale, and with the closed glottis of humming, and each rise is detected by the baroreceptors.

**Iron-dependent bottleneck** — the single cellular machine every new strand of genetic blueprint must pass through before a cell can divide, carrying an iron atom at its working heart. Because every division in a tissue squeezes through this one gate, a drop in iron delivery is the first thing in the chain to slow the tissue down.

---

## K

**Keratin** — the hard structural protein that dividing cells fill themselves with before they die, forming hair and nail. The visible nail is entirely dead keratin-filled cells, pushed forward by living cells dividing behind them.

---

## L

**Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)** — the sugar-and-fat molecule forming the outer skin of certain gut bacteria, shed constantly as they die. Immune cells carry a sensor built specifically to recognise it, because over evolutionary time it proved one of the most reliable fingerprints of bacterial presence. That specificity is also the problem: a fragment leaking through a stressed intestinal wall sets off the identical alarm a real invasion would.

**Lipoprotein lipase** — the enzyme that clears fats from the blood and enables muscle glucose uptake. Its activity in leg muscle falls to near zero within hours of sitting and returns within minutes of walking.

**Low-grade systemic inflammation** — a faint, continuous inflammatory signal circulating in the blood, producing fatigue, mental fog and a sense of being unwell without a nameable illness. Distinct from inflammaging in cause rather than character: this is driven by an ongoing source such as a permeable gut wall rather than by age alone.

**Lux** — a measure of light intensity. Outdoor daylight is 10,000–100,000 lux; indoor lighting is typically 300–500. The difference is the reason indoor light does not set the body clock.

**Lymphatic system** — the network draining fluid from tissue, transporting immune cells and filtering waste. It has no pump. It moves only when muscles compress it.

---

## M

**Macular pigment** — a yellow filter of lutein, zeaxanthin and meso-zeaxanthin in front of the central retina, absorbing blue light before it reaches the photoreceptors. Lutein and zeaxanthin come entirely from diet (dark leafy greens, egg yolk, orange produce); meso-zeaxanthin has almost no dietary source and is instead made on site by an eye enzyme that also regenerates visual pigment, converting a small amount of the lutein already delivered by food. Denser pigment correlates with faster recovery from glare — how quickly vision returns after an oncoming headlight — and the filter is built molecule by molecule over months, not days.

**Major histocompatibility complex (MHC/HLA)** — the gene cluster determining immune self-recognition and transplant compatibility. It also produces a volatile scent signature nearly unique to each person, which the nose reads below awareness.

**Mechanoreceptors** — sensors that detect physical force. The main classes are Meissner corpuscles (flutter and texture), Merkel cells (sustained pressure), Pacinian corpuscles (vibration) and Ruffini endings (stretch).

**Mechanotransduction** — the conversion of mechanical force into a biological signal. It is how bone knows to build, how cartilage knows to maintain itself, how the endothelium knows blood is flowing, and — through integrins bending in the muscle fibre membrane — how muscle knows it was worked close to its limit.

**Melanopsin / ipRGCs** — light-sensitive retinal cells that do not contribute to vision. They report light intensity directly to the master clock and are most sensitive to blue wavelengths.

**Melatonin** — known as the sleep hormone, produced by the pineal gland in darkness. Separately, mitochondria produce their own melatonin locally in response to red and near-infrared light, where it acts as a targeted antioxidant rather than a sleep signal.

**Microarousal** — a brief lightening of sleep without conscious waking. Enough of them fragment sleep architecture without the person knowing their sleep was disturbed.

**Microchimerism** — the persistence of another person's living cells in your body. Cells cross the placenta in both directions during pregnancy and can remain for decades.

**Migrating motor complex** — the four-phase cleaning wave that sweeps the small intestine between meals. It runs only in the fasting state and is switched off by eating — and also by stress hormones, which removes the mechanism keeping the small intestine's bacterial population from creeping upward and settling where it should not.

**Mirror neuron system** — circuitry that fires both when you perform an action and when you watch someone else perform it. It underlies contagious yawning and laughter, and it is the basis of mirror therapy for phantom limb pain.

**Mitochondria** — the cell's power plants, and originally free-living bacteria incorporated about 1.5 billion years ago. They retain their own DNA and their own membranes. Population falls with age and with disuse, but new ones can still be built (mitochondrial biogenesis) — a separate process from maintaining existing ones, gated by PGC-1 alpha and triggered only within a narrow window of sustained effort.

**mTOR** — the cell's nutrient sensor and the master switch for building new muscle protein. When active it prioritises growth and suppresses repair; when it deactivates, autophagy is released. For muscle it behaves as a lock taking two keys at once — one chemical, cut from dietary protein, and one structural, cut only by tension near the fibre's limit. Either key turned alone leaves the lock unmoved.

**Muscle spindles** — sensors woven into muscle reporting length and stretch. They habituate to sustained positions and are reset by the stretch that accompanies a yawn.

**Myelin** — the fatty insulation around fast nerve fibres. It allows the signal to jump between gaps rather than travel continuously, raising conduction speed roughly twentyfold.

**Myonuclear domain** — the stretch of a muscle fibre that a single nucleus can supply with blueprints. A fibre is far too large for one command post, so it carries hundreds of nuclei, each governing its own territory. The protein a fibre can build and maintain is capped by how many nuclei it holds, which is why growth beyond a modest range waits on recruiting more.

---

## N

**Nail fold capillary loops** — the blood supply to the nail matrix, arriving not as one broad river but as a dense row of hairpin vessels packed side by side like a comb of fine wire. Each loop serves the strip of dividing tissue directly above it and very little else, so a single loop can fail while its neighbour stays whole. With age the comb also thins, loops lost and not replaced, leaving survivors farther apart with no neighbour to lean on.

**Nail matrix** — the crescent of living, dividing tissue tucked behind the cuticle where all nail is manufactured. It is divided lengthwise into narrow strips, each running as an independent production line fed by its own vessel. Where one strip slows while its neighbour keeps pace, the mismatch surfaces months later as a vertical ridge.

**Natural killer cells** — immune cells that identify and destroy virus-infected and early cancerous cells without needing prior exposure. Their activity rises with forest air and falls with cortisol.

**Nitric oxide** — a gas that relaxes smooth muscle, widening airways and blood vessels. It is produced by the endothelium in response to blood flow, released from the sinuses by humming and singing, released from skin by ultraviolet light, and freed inside mitochondria by near-infrared light.

**Nociceptors** — bare nerve endings that convert tissue damage into an electrical signal. They are dense in the fingertips and nearly absent in internal organs.

**Nodes of Ranvier** — the gaps in myelin, about a millimetre apart, where the nerve signal regenerates at full strength as it jumps along.

**Nucleus tractus solitarius** — the brainstem's autonomic switchboard, receiving input from heart, lungs, gut, baroreceptors and skin, and integrating it into the vagal response.

---

## O

**Olfactory-limbic pathway** — the route from the nose directly to the memory and emotion centres, bypassing the relay station every other sense passes through. It is why a smell produces a feeling before you have identified the smell.

**Osteoblasts and osteoclasts** — the cells that build bone and the cells that dismantle it. Osteoblasts need a mechanical signal to work; osteoclasts run on metabolic demand regardless. Remove the loading and the balance tips to loss.

**Osteocytes** — the sensing cells embedded throughout bone, roughly 25,000 per cubic millimetre, connected by a network of fine processes. They detect loading and instruct the building cells.

**Oxytocin** — the bonding hormone released by sustained touch, by singing, and by trusted presence. It quietens the amygdala, suppresses cortisol, and accelerates wound healing.

---

## P

**Pandiculation** — the stretch that accompanies a yawn. It resets muscle spindle sensitivity after sustained stillness.

**Paranasal sinuses** — four pairs of air-filled chambers in the skull. They continuously produce nitric oxide and act as acoustic resonance chambers, and their narrow drainage openings are why pressure changes cause headache.

**Parasympathetic nervous system** — the recovery branch of the autonomic nervous system: digestion, repair, immune surveillance, slowed heart rate. Its main pathway is the vagus nerve.

**Peristalsis** — the muscular waves that move food through the gut. Walking stimulates them.

**Petrichor** — the smell of rain on dry earth, named in 1964 from the Greek for stone and the blood of the gods.

**PGC-1 alpha** — the master switch that triggers a cell to build new mitochondria. It activates only when existing mitochondria can no longer keep pace with sustained energy demand — an effort level held too gently coasts under the threshold, and effort pushed too hard overshoots into a faster, much less efficient fuel route that bypasses mitochondria entirely rather than straining them.

**Photobiomodulation** — the use of red and near-infrared light to increase cellular energy output and blood flow by acting on cytochrome c oxidase.

**Phytoncides** — antimicrobial compounds released by trees, principally alpha-pinene and beta-pinene. Inhaled, they raise natural killer cell activity for days.

**Piezoelectricity** — the generation of an electrical charge by mechanical pressure. Bone crystals do this, and the charge is the signal that directs bone building.

**Plantar venous plexus** — the dense vein network in the sole of the foot, compressed by every footstrike. Sixty to seventy pump strokes per minute per foot while walking; none while standing or sitting.

**Postprandial hypotension** — a blood pressure drop of 20–30 mmHg in the 30–75 minutes after a meal, affecting up to a third of older adults as the baroreceptor compensation weakens with age.

**Proprioception** — the sense of where your body is in space, built from muscle, tendon and joint sensors. It calibrates on use and degrades without varied input.

**Purkinje cells** — the output neurons of the cerebellum, each integrating input from up to 200,000 parallel fibres.

**Purkinje shift** — the migration of peak visual sensitivity toward blue-green as light fades and rods take over. A yellowed lens suppresses it, holding warm colour perception longer.

---

## R

**Reactive hyperemia** — the surge of blood into a region after a period of restricted flow.

**Resistin** — a hormone released by fat cells that directly blunts insulin sensitivity, rising as fat cells enlarge just as adiponectin (its protective opposite) falls. The two together are part of why weight gain compounds its own effect on blood sugar control.

**Rayleigh scattering** — the scattering of light by air molecules, proportional to the inverse fourth power of wavelength. Blue scatters about nine times more than red, which is why the sky is blue and sunsets are not.

**Respiratory sinus arrhythmia** — the natural speeding of the heart on the in-breath and slowing on the out-breath. It is what heart rate variability measures.

---

## S

**Saltatory conduction** — the jumping of a nerve signal from one myelin gap to the next, regenerating at full strength each time.

**Sarcopenia** — the loss of muscle mass and strength that follows the removal of contractile load. After 50 it is compounded by anabolic resistance and by a thinning satellite cell pool, so the same habits that maintained muscle earlier slide under a threshold that keeps rising.

**Satellite cells** — stem cells parked in shallow dormancy on the outer surface of a muscle fibre, between the membrane and the collagen wrap — stationed among the very bolts that read mechanical strain. The narrow space one sleeps in changes shape when the fibre beneath it deforms, so they are woken mechanically. Roused repeatedly across weeks they divide and fuse into the fibre, donating nuclei permanently.

**Second brain** — the roughly 400 million neurons embedded in the gut wall — more than the spinal cord carries. They run digestion on their own timing, coordinating muscular waves and secretions without instruction from the brain. A gut with its vagus nerve cut still digests and still manages itself.

**SERCA** — the calcium pump that resets muscle for the next contraction. It accounts for roughly a quarter of resting muscle metabolism and produces heat every time it fires.

**Shear stress** — the frictional force of flowing blood against the vessel wall. It is the signal that tells the endothelium to produce nitric oxide.

**Sickness behaviour** — the cluster of fatigue, withdrawal, appetite loss and heightened pain sensitivity produced by inflammatory cytokines. It appears identically in infection and in grief.

**Size principle** — the fixed order in which the nervous system recruits muscle fibres: smallest and most fatigue-resistant first, largest and most powerful last. Light effort never reaches the largest units at all. They are called in only once everything smaller is spent, which is why the closing repetitions of a set to failure differ in kind from the first ones.

**Sleep inertia** — the grogginess of waking out of deep slow-wave sleep rather than out of a light stage.

**Sleep pressure** — the accumulating drive to sleep, produced by adenosine building through the waking day.

**Slow wave sleep (N3)** — the deepest sleep stage, when tissue repair, immune function, memory consolidation and glymphatic clearance are at their peak.

**Somatosensory cortex** — the strip of brain holding a point-by-point map of the body surface, disproportionately weighted toward hands and face.

**Splanchnic vasoconstriction** — the clamping of blood vessels feeding the intestine when the body registers a threat, beginning within roughly 200 milliseconds — faster than the fright itself is consciously felt. Blood is redirected to muscle, heart and brain on the logic that digestion is a luxury in an emergency.

**Stratum corneum** — the outermost skin layer, 10–15 micrometres thick on the hands. It is a selective barrier, not a seal.

**Suprachiasmatic nucleus** — the master circadian clock, sitting above the optic nerve crossing and set primarily by light.

**Sympathetic nervous system** — the activating branch of the autonomic nervous system: alertness, mobilisation, threat response.

**Synovial fluid** — the viscous fluid lubricating joints. It thickens sharply in cold and thins with movement.

---

## T

**Telomeres and telomerase** — the protective caps on chromosome ends, and the enzyme that rebuilds them. Telomeres shorten with each cell division and faster under chronic cortisol.

**Thermogenin (UCP1)** — the protein in brown fat that lets protons bypass the ATP-making turbine, converting the entire energy gradient directly into heat.

**Thixotropy** — the property of a fluid that thins under movement. It is why joints free up during the first steps of the day.

**Thymus** — the organ behind the breastbone where every T cell is trained to distinguish self from non-self. It peaks at puberty and is roughly 85% fat by 65.

**Tight junctions (claudins and occludins)** — the sealing proteins stitching neighbouring intestinal lining cells to one another, tight enough that not even a bacterium passes between them. Picture floor tiles with a bead of living grout along every seam, constantly reapplied. They are maintained moment to moment rather than built once, so anything that reduces their manufacture lets the seams open.

**Time under tension** — the total duration for which a load holds muscle fibres deformed, as distinct from how heavy the load is. A weight lowered over four seconds keeps the bolts bent for the whole descent where a dropped weight grants only a glimpse — the stimulus is integrated the way a long photographic exposure gathers light.

**TRPM8 and TRPA1** — molecular cold sensors. TRPM8 opens below about 25 °C and signals cold; TRPA1 opens below about 17 °C and signals pain. Menthol opens TRPM8 without any temperature change.

**Type II fibres (fast-twitch)** — the largest and most powerful muscle units, recruited last and lost fastest with age. They are the fibres that stand you out of a chair, catch you when a curb surprises you, and break the seal on a jar. Because recruitment runs smallest-first, comfortable effort leaves them asleep entirely.

---

## V

**Vagus nerve** — the tenth cranial nerve, running from the brainstem to the heart, lungs and gut. It is the main parasympathetic pathway, and it can be reached mechanically through laryngeal vibration, respiratory pressure, cold on the face, chest pressure and the diaphragm's motion during laughter.

**Vagal tone** — the baseline activity of the vagus nerve, measured through heart rate variability. It is trainable, like a muscle.

**Vestibular system** — the balance organ of the inner ear. Beyond balance, it projects directly to the brainstem nuclei governing arousal and mood.

**Volatile organic compounds** — molecules light enough to evaporate at ordinary temperatures. They carry the scent signatures of soil, trees, skin and cooking.

---

## W

**Wolff's law** — bone remodels along the lines of mechanical stress, thickening where forces are greatest and thinning where they are absent. Stated by Julius Wolff in 1892; the piezoelectric charge is the mechanism by which it works.

## Z

**Zinc-clamped protein switches** — gene-reading proteins that hold their gripping shape only while a zinc atom is clamped into the fold of each one. Without it the switch goes limp and can no longer find its place on the blueprint. Hundreds of cellular tools borrow zinc for the same structural trick, so the metal is stretched thin across a great deal of work at once.

## Fasting

# 07 — Plain English Glossary

Every technical term introduced across this knowledge package, defined the way the source
material defines it. Terms are alphabetical. Where a source hedges its own claim about a term,
that hedge is kept rather than smoothed away.

---

## A

**Autolysis** — the enzymatic self-digestion of the body's own stored and "less essential" tissue
to nourish vital tissues during a fast; the source's core proposed mechanism for how a faster is
sustained without food.

## D

**Decompensation** — the medical term (used accurately) for the heart's inability to maintain
adequate circulation.

## E

**Enervation** — nerve-energy depletion, attributed to stimulants and overwork, said to check the
body's elimination of waste.

## F

**"Fasting" vs. "starving"** — a foundational distinction in this material: fasting is abstinence
while adequate nutritive reserves remain; starvation begins only once those reserves are exhausted
and vital tissue itself must be sacrificed.

## H

**Hygienic practice / Natural Hygiene** — the broader health framework fasting is situated within
in this material, built around a plant-based diet, avoidance of drugs and surgery, and reliance on
the body's self-healing capacity.

## P

**Physiological compensation** — energy freed by suspending digestion becoming available to other
bodily systems.

## T

**Toxemia** — accumulated bodily waste, said in this material to result from enervation and to be
the shared root cause of a wide range of named diseases and conditions.
