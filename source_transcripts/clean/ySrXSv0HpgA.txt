The physics of that question has almost nothing to do with better arguments. Claude Shannon, in his foundational 1948 paper on information theory, gave a precise mathematical definition of when a system updates its model, when new information reduces uncertainty. A

[18:16] signal that tells you something you already expected carries low information. It confirms the existing model and produces minimal updating. A signal that contradicts a strong expectation carries high information. It represents a large deviation from the model, and in a well-functioning system, produces proportional updating. The strength of the updating is proportional to the size of the surprise. That framework, updating proportional to surprise, is the foundation for everything that follows. Karl Friston extended it to the brain with his predictive processing model. The brain, in Friston's framework, is a prediction machine. It constantly generates models of what will happen next and compares those predictions against incoming sensory data. When the prediction matches reality, nothing updates. The model holds. The prior stands. The belief persists. When reality deviates from prediction, a

[19:17] prediction error, the brain must either update its model or explain away the error. The strength of the existing model determines how large the prediction error must be before updating occurs. A concrete analogy makes this tangible. Imagine a weather forecaster who has predicted sunshine for 40 years and been right every time. On day 14,000, one person tells him it might rain tomorrow. The forecaster rationally weighs that one report against 14,000 days of confirmed sunshine. The prior is overwhelming. The single data point is noise. That is Bayesian inference operating correctly. The forecaster would be irrational to abandon a 40-year model based on one report. Your brain does the same calculation with beliefs using the same mathematics reaching the same conclusion.
