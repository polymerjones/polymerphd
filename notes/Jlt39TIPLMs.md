# Why Poor Circulation Is the Wrong Answer for YOUR Numb Feet

## Central claim

**"Circulation" describes blood flow. What is failing is the nerve itself** — and it fails at the toe
first because the toe sits at the end of the longest supply line in the body `[00:00]`.

> "The toes were always going to be the first to feel it" `[26:30]`.

**A single sensory neuron spans roughly a metre** from a cell body near the spine to a terminus at the
toe. **Nothing is manufactured at the terminus** — every protein, enzyme and mitochondrion must travel
the full distance.

## Supply system 1 — axonal transport

`[01:01]`–`[04:04]`

Two systems run along microtubule rails spanning the axon:

| System | Speed | Time to reach the toe |
|---|---|---|
| **Fast transport** — vesicles, mitochondria, membrane components, carried by kinesin motors | 200–400 mm/day | **3–5 days** |
| **Slow transport** — cytoskeletal elements, soluble proteins | 1–5 mm/day | **200–1,000 days** (6 months to nearly 3 years) |

**The molecular detail** `[01:01]`–`[02:03]`: kinesin walks hand-over-hand in **8-nanometre steps**, each
consuming one ATP — roughly **100 steps per second**, repeated across the multi-day journey.

**The circular dependency that makes the far end doubly vulnerable** `[02:03]`–`[03:03]`: fast transport
needs intact microtubules; intact microtubules need **slow** transport to deliver the tubulin that
maintains them. Near the cell body both run with short distances and rapid turnover. A metre away, the
maintenance subunits take **months** to arrive.

> "If slow transport fails at the distal end… the terminus loses **both its structural maintenance and its
> energy supply simultaneously**" `[03:03]`.

## Supply system 2 — vasa nervorum, and why they fail first

`[04:04]`–`[08:08]` — **the most transferable idea in the video.**

The **vasa nervorum** are the arterioles and capillaries running alongside and into the nerve bundle,
**50–100 micrometres** across — among the smallest named vessels in the body.

**Poiseuille's fourth power law does the damage:**

| Vessel | Effect of the same ~10 µm layer of glycated endothelium |
|---|---|
| **Vasa nervorum (100 µm)** | 20% radius reduction → **0.8⁴ = 0.41 → a 59% flow reduction** |
| Coronary artery (4 mm) | ~0.5% radius reduction → **too small to measure** |

> "The same chemistry, the same rate, the same millimetres of endothelial change. **The difference in
> clinical consequence entirely a function of the starting diameter**" `[23:28]`.

**No diabetes required** `[06:06]`: normal glucose exposure across six decades produces measurable
glycation. Each cross-link is permanent — "no enzyme in the body can remove" it. Elevated glucose only
accelerates it: **"Diabetic neuropathy is the fastest version of a process that runs in every aging
body."**

**The diameter hierarchy — and why numb feet may be an early systemic alarm** `[22:26]`–`[23:28]`:

> vasa nervorum (50–100 µm) → penile arteries (1–2 mm) → renal arterioles → coronary arteries (3–4 mm) →
> carotids

> "The foot that went numb may be sounding the earliest alarm in a vascular timeline that **will not reach
> the coronary arteries for another decade**."

*(Vessel wall changes preceding nerve fibre loss attributed to Malik and colleagues, *Diabetologia*.)*

## Why the pattern is a stocking

`[09:09]`–`[12:12]`

When supply degrades **systemically** — glucose, B12 deficiency, inflammation, alcohol — every nerve is
affected at once. **But the longest nerves cross the failure threshold first**, because losses accumulate
along the route.

**The water main analogy** `[10:09]`–`[11:11]`: as the pump weakens, friction drops pressure at every
metre. The nearest house is fine; the farthest falls below threshold first. **"The toes are the last
house. The spine is the pump station."**

> "**The spatial progression is the temporal progression.** The pattern on your body is a clock, with the
> boundary between numb and normal marking exactly how far the supply failure has advanced" `[11:11]`.

**And why the hands follow** `[12:12]`: cervical-to-fingertip nerves span roughly **0.7 m** — the second
longest. When degradation progresses, the glove pattern appears. **"You did not develop a second
disease."**

## Which fibres fail first — and the danger it creates

`[13:13]`–`[15:15]`

**Small-diameter fibres (A-delta and C) fail before large-diameter (A-beta) fibres**, because smaller
fibres have proportionally more membrane surface requiring maintenance per unit of volume.

| Fibre type | Carries | Order |
|---|---|---|
| **Small** | **Pain, temperature, autonomic** | **Lost first** |
| Large | Touch, vibration, proprioception | Lost later |

*(Attributed to Cashman and Höke, *Current Opinion in Neurology*.)*

**The clinical danger this creates** `[14:15]`–`[15:15]` — **the most important practical content here:**

> "You can feel the shoe pressing against the foot. **You cannot feel the blister forming inside it.** You
> can feel the floor beneath your sole. **You cannot feel that the bathwater is scalding.**"

**The foot becomes an unmonitored structure.** A pebble that a healthy foot would flag within three steps
goes undetected for a day of walking; a friction blister grows for hours because the alarm never fires.

> "The small fibre loss does not merely reduce sensation. **It removes the early warning system**"
> `[15:15]`.

**Practical consequence:** daily visual foot inspection becomes necessary once pain and temperature
sensation are reduced, because the body's own damage alarm is offline.

## Why tingling feels the way it does

`[15:15]`–`[18:23]`

**Myelin** is 70–80% lipid, maintained by **Schwann cells**, each wrapping about **1 mm** of axon — so a
spine-to-toe nerve needs roughly **1,000 Schwann cells** in series, each maintaining its own segment
independently.

When vasa nervorum supply falls, distal Schwann cells cannot sustain the energy-intensive work of myelin
synthesis and repair. **Insulation degrades segmentally** — some territories bare, adjacent ones intact.

**The conduction consequence:**

| Mode | Velocity |
|---|---|
| **Saltatory** (healthy, jumping node to node) | 40–60 m/s |
| **Continuous** (across a bare segment) | 1–2 m/s |

**A 30–60 fold reduction at each gap**, and the delays accumulate along the metre.

> "The brain, receiving the delayed and jittered signal alongside normal-speed signals from adjacent
> territories, **cannot resolve the asynchronous input into a coherent sensation.** … **The signal is
> arriving, but it is arriving wrong**" `[17:21]`–`[18:23]`.

That mistimed arrival **is** the pins-and-needles quality — distinct from the clean blank of a dead nerve.

## The reversible window — the key distinction

`[18:23]`–`[22:26]` — **the single most useful thing in this video.**

| Stage | Signature | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| **Demyelination** — insulation failing, axon intact | **Tingling.** Intermittent, comes and goes, varies with position and time of day, sometimes better and sometimes worse | **Potentially reversible.** Schwann cells can remyelinate if supply is restored |
| **Axonal degeneration** — the wire itself dying back | **Fixed numbness.** Constant, non-fluctuating, unchanged by position, "a permanent blank rather than a buzzing signal" | **Regeneration at ~1 mm/day** across a distance measured in metres — months to years, "if regeneration occurs at all" |

> "One was the insulation failing, and the other was the wire itself dying back. **The window between the
> two stages is the window where intervention changes the outcome**" `[19:24]`.

**Three supply-restoration routes named for the demyelination phase** `[20:24]`–`[21:25]`:
1. **Glucose control** — reducing the glycation rate on the vasa nervorum endothelium.
2. **B12 repletion** — an obligate cofactor in the methylation reactions Schwann cells need for myelin
   lipid synthesis. **Independently common after 50**, because gastric acid production declines with age
   and B12 must be liberated from protein by adequate acid.
3. **Exercise-induced angiogenesis** — sustained aerobic activity releases vascular endothelial growth
   factor, stimulating **new capillary formation in the vasa nervorum network**, "potentially opening new
   supply routes around segments that have narrowed beyond recovery." **Walking is named as the simplest
   sustained aerobic activity available.**

## What a clinician adds

`[23:28]`–`[25:30]` — **routed appropriately and worth preserving.**

> "Whether the numbness you experience requires clinical evaluation, medication adjustment, glucose
> management, or nutritional supplementation **is a determination your physician makes.** … The physics
> teaches what is happening inside the nerve. **Your physician determines what is driving the supply
> failure in your specific case.**"

**Nerve conduction testing distinguishes the two stages:**
- **Demyelination** → dramatic **velocity** reduction, because the signal must crawl through bare
  segments.
- **Axonal loss** → reduced signal **amplitude**, because fewer fibres contribute, while survivors keep
  normal velocity through intact myelin.

**And a metabolic workup identifies the driver** — glucose, B12, thyroid function, inflammatory markers.
**"Each cause operates through a different pathway to the same endpoint"**, and identifying it lets the
intervention target the source rather than the symptom.

## Symptoms and body signals addressed

- Numbness starting at the toes and creeping upward, **symmetric in both feet** `[00:00]`.
- The sensation of the sole being wrapped in something thin `[00:00]`.
- Tingling that comes and goes, varying with position `[19:24]`.
- Tingling appearing in the fingertips months or years after the toes `[12:12]`.
- Being able to feel pressure but not pain or temperature `[14:15]`.
- Blisters or pressure wounds discovered only on removing a shoe `[15:15]`.

## Glossary terms introduced

- **Axonal transport** — fast (200–400 mm/day) and slow (1–5 mm/day) delivery along the axon `[01:01]`.
- **Kinesin** — the motor protein walking in 8 nm ATP-powered steps `[01:01]`.
- **Vasa nervorum** — the 50–100 µm vessels supplying the nerve along its route `[04:04]`.
- **Stocking-glove distribution** — the length-dependent pattern of loss `[11:11]`.
- **Small versus large fibres** — pain and temperature versus touch and vibration `[13:13]`.
- **Schwann cell** — maintains ~1 mm of myelin each `[16:18]`.
- **Saltatory conduction** — signal jumping node to node at 40–60 m/s `[17:21]`.
- **Demyelination versus axonal degeneration** — the reversible and non-reversible stages `[18:23]`.
- **Diameter hierarchy** — smallest vessels showing consequences first `[22:26]`.
